---
type: concept
title: Story-Based Elicitation Questions
description: >
  Asking a stakeholder to narrate one specific, recent instance produces
  more reliable requirements input than asking them to generalize or
  answer a direct, hypothetical question about their own behavior.
sources:
  - title: Continuous Discovery Habits
    resource: "Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres), ch. 5"
---

People are unreliable narrators of their own behavior and motives — not
from dishonesty, but because of how memory and reasoning actually work.
Asked a direct question ("what criteria do you use when choosing X?"),
people tend to answer with their aspirational self-image rather than their
actual behavior, favor plausible-sounding generalities over specifics, and
will confidently fabricate a coherent-sounding explanation even when they
have no real access to their own reasons — a well-documented pattern in
cognitive-psychology and investigative-interviewing research alike.
Confidence in an answer is not evidence the answer is accurate.

The elicitation countermeasure is to ask for a **story**, not an opinion:
"tell me about the last time you [did X]," anchored to one specific,
recent, real instance, rather than "what do you usually do" or "what
would you want." A remembered specific episode resists confabulation in a
way a generalization does not. When a stakeholder's answer drifts from the
specific instance back into generalization ("I usually...", "in
general..."), redirect to the concrete case rather than letting the
generalization stand as the data point: "in that specific example, did you
run into that?"

This is a targeted refinement of [apprenticing and
interviewing](requirements-elicitation-techniques.md) rather than a
replacement for it: use story-based questions specifically when the goal
is uncovering actual past behavior, and scope the story's breadth to the
kind of opportunity you're looking for — a narrow scope ("last time you
used this feature") surfaces problems with the current design, a broader
one ("last time you needed to do this at all, however you did it")
surfaces needs the current design has not yet addressed, including ones
met by a competitor or a workaround. See [essence of the business
work](essence-of-the-business-work.md) for the companion technique of
laddering from a stated solution back to the need behind it once the story
has surfaced one.
