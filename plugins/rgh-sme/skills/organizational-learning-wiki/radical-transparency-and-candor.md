  Extreme openness norms — speaking every critical opinion, prohibiting talk
  behind backs, making assessments visible — as one path to learning culture.

Bridgewater Associates codifies "radical truth and transparency" as a culture
where meaningful work and meaningful relationships depend on extreme candor.
Ray Dalio's principle: leaders must create an environment where no one has the
right to hold a critical opinion without speaking up — silence is treated as
unethical because unspoken ideas belong to the collective enterprise.

**Transparency norms** include prohibiting discussion of people not present;
violators are called "slimy weasels." "Baseball cards" tally ongoing assessment
statistics per employee, visible firm-wide, informing compensation and promotion.
A transparency library of executive-meeting video is available to any employee.
"It is okay to make mistakes, but unacceptable not to identify, analyze, and
learn from them."

**Productive conflict** serves finding "what is true and what to do about it."
Dalio: "don't try to 'win' the argument. Finding out that you are wrong is even
more valuable than being right." Managers enforce conversational logic calmly
when emotions run hot. Dalio distinguishes discussion (open exploration),
debate (between approximate equals), and teaching (different understanding
levels) — showing explicit hierarchy and [psychological safety](psychological-safety.md)
are not mutually exclusive.

This is a more extreme stance than Edmondson's general framing but illustrates
one way [institutionalized candor](institutionalized-candor.md) can scale.
High attrition shows the culture is not for everyone. Dalio warns: "Ask yourself
whether you have earned the right to have an opinion" — through track record,
not mere conjecture.

Pairs with [uncoupling fear and failure](uncoupling-fear-and-failure.md) and
[responding productively to voice](responding-productively-to-voice.md).
