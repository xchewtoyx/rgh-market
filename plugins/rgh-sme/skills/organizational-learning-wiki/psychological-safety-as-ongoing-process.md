Creating [psychological safety](psychological-safety.md) is not a project with
a finish line. Edmondson likens it to sailing upwind: you cannot head straight
at the mark; you tack at alternating angles, trimming sails through small
adjustments and large pivots until you arrive.

Everyday acts of voice — questioning a medication protocol, critiquing a film
plot, suggesting forklift clearance, advocating for mine safety — are the
tacks. When voice lands well, progress feels smooth. When it does not —
superiors too busy, no response, promotion passed over — the wind leaves the
sail and leaders must recover through inquiry, apology, admitting ignorance,
or reassuring people that speaking up will not endanger them.

The [leader's toolkit](leaders-toolkit-for-psychological-safety.md) practices
are ongoing repetition, not one-time events. Psychological safety can feel
present one quarter and erode the next when norms are not continuously
reinforced.

**Continuous renewal** is required because [psychological safety](psychological-safety.md)
is fragile. Building organizations where people bring their full selves means
swimming upstream against deeply ingrained currents — the [asymmetry of voice and silence](asymmetry-of-voice-and-silence.md)
favoring self-protection over self-expression will always be present. But the
rewards of voice are also asymmetrical: self-protection is a hollow victory
compared to fulfilling an inspiring purpose as part of a team.

Psychological safety is **dynamic, not fixed**. A workplace with unusual candor
may shift under new leaders or circumstances; one dominated by fearful silence
can change toward thoughtful input — often through deliberate effort to learn
from painful past failures. Cases capture moments in time, not permanent
organizational character.
