Senge distinguishes two forms of openness:

**Participative (expressive) openness** — speaking openly about one's views.
Important but "dangerously incomplete." It rose in the 1980s with participative
management; organizations that institutionalized formal open-communication
procedures (focus groups, surveys) largely fell out of favor because they were
not effective.

Chris Argyris criticized "good communication that blocks learning": formal
mechanisms let employees tell management what they think without taking
responsibility for problems or their own role in them. They fail because they
"do not get people to reflect on their own work and behavior... do not encourage
individual accountability... do not surface the kinds of deep and potentially
threatening or embarrassing information that can motivate learning and produce
real change."

**Reflective openness** — looking inward, letting conversation reveal one's own
biases and limitations and how one's thinking and actions contribute to problems.
Described as "the cornerstone of the discipline of mental models." None of us
has "a company in our heads," only assumptions, feelings, and partial hypotheses;
reflective openness means continually testing those views.

Participative openness alone does not generate commitment and shared understanding.
Real learning starts when people actually listen rather than talk at each other.
Reflective openness requires an organizational environment with deep commitment
to helping people grow and to trust and mutuality.

Roger Saillant (Ford) turned around multiple manufacturing sites by engaging
people differently, but noted bosses never asked "How did you really do that?"
— they sensed the answer but were not prepared to be that vulnerable themselves.
"If you can get connected with what's inside of you, you can be fearless in
your openness. That can be scary as a leader."

Molly Baldwin (Roca): "I always have to be willing to work on my own stuff" —
even in conflicts with police, examining her own part rather than externalizing
blame. Saillant: "It all starts with personal mastery... my willingness to see
the shortcomings that are all too evident to those around me. I can never expect
the people around me in an organization to be more open and willing to learn and
improve than I am."
