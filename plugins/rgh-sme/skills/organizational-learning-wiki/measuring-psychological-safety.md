Surveys are the most common tool for measuring
[psychological safety](psychological-safety.md). Edmondson's dissertation
introduced a seven-item scale on a seven-point (or five-point) Likert scale,
with three positively worded items and three reverse-scored negative items.

Published variants illustrate construct robustness across settings:

- **Garvin, Edmondson, & Gino (2008)**, α = .94 — five items including ease of
  speaking up, problems held against you (reverse), comfort discussing
  disagreements, sharing what does not work, and keeping cards close (reverse).
- **Tucker, Nembhard, & Edmondson (2007)**, α = .74 — NICU-worded items on
  checking with colleagues, valuing unique skills, and raising tough issues.
- **Nembhard & Edmondson (2006)**, α = .73 — overlapping NICU items plus ease
  of asking for help and mistakes held against you (reverse).

The measure has shown robust psychometric properties — inter-item reliability
and predictive validity — despite wording variations, and has been translated
into multiple languages.

Qualitative approaches complement surveys: coding interviews for
presence or absence of psychological safety; presenting hypothetical gray-area
scenarios and asking how people would respond (best under confidentiality);
vignette surveys extend this to larger samples.

Measurement at the [group level](psychological-safety-as-group-climate.md)
matters because climate varies by team even within one organization.
