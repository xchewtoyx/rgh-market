type: concept
title: Inviting Participation
description: >
  Leadership practices that lower the bar for voice through humility, inquiry,
  and structures that elicit input.
sources:
  - title: The Fearless Organization
    resource: "The Fearless Organization (Edmondson), ch. 7"
---

**Inviting participation** is the second category in the
[leader's toolkit](leaders-toolkit-for-psychological-safety.md). Speaking up
is only possible if leaders lower an otherwise too-high bar for what counts as
"appropriate" to raise.

**Situational humility** — a learning mindset recognizing "there is always
more to learn" — mitigates imposing ideas on a boss who seems to know
everything. Confidence and humility are not opposites. "Leadership
inclusiveness" (approachable leaders who acknowledge fallibility and invite
input) predicted better quality-improvement results in NICU research.

**Proactive inquiry** is purposeful probing grounded in genuine interest.
Good questions: you don't know the answer; they aren't yes/no; they help others
share thinking. Naive realism — the felt experience of seeing objective
reality — suppresses curiosity. Examples: "What did the dissenter say?"
(MTV's Bob Pittman); Morath's "Was everything as safe as you would like it to
have been this week?"

**Structures for input** — focus groups, safety action teams, peer mentoring
networks, knowledge marketplaces — make silence feel more awkward than
speaking, countering the [asymmetry of voice and silence](asymmetry-of-voice-and-silence.md).

Explicit **invitations for pushback** lower indirect, careful language that
masks disagreement. In Tetlock's superforecasting teams, members who said "I
want push-back from you if you see something I don't" and thanked each other
for constructive criticism replaced early "dancing around" where people
disagreed but couched it to avoid offense. Without assigned leaders, Marty
Rosenthal practiced "leading from behind" — explaining forecasts in detail to
model good discussion rather than taking over — building stronger team
commitment through a voluntary conference call most members joined.

Barry-Wehmiller illustrates **inviting the responsible parties into the room**:
setting up a Green Bay machine shop, two rounds of analysis by divisional
presidents without floor workers failed; a third round included forklift drivers,
assemblers, pickers, and clerical staff who used cardboard cutouts on the factory
floor to map clearances and workflow — converging on a layout still in place five
years later. CEO Bob Chapman institutionalized "listening sessions" that surfaced
trust-destroying practices (time clocks, break bells, locked inventory cages) for
removal.

Eileen Fisher models **"don't knower" leadership**: "when you don't know and you're
really listening intently, people want to help you." Managing by not-knowing means
"people feel safe to explore their own ideas instead of feeling like they just need
to do what you tell them to do." Circle meetings, a minute of silence, and passing
an object so whoever holds it speaks deemphasize hierarchy — "a leader in every
chair." Saying "yes" to staff-initiated sustainability commitments gives "permission
to care."
