**Personal mastery** — continually clarifying personal vision and seeing reality
objectively — is perhaps the most radical of the five disciplines in retrospect:
the idea that an organization could be an environment in which people truly grow
as human beings.

Most companies espouse "people are our most important asset" and fund training
programs, but genuine commitment requires more. Rich Teerlink (former Harley-Davidson
CEO): "Being truly committed to growing people is an act of faith. You have to
believe... that people want to pursue a vision that matters, that they want to
contribute and be responsible for results, and that they are willing to look at
shortfalls in their own behavior." These beliefs are hard for control-oriented
managers — hence the persistent gap between talk and walk on developing people.

William J. O'Brien's conviction: "the best way to grow financial capital is
through growing human capital." The organization's capacity for learning can be
no greater than its members' — see [five disciplines as ensemble](five-disciplines-as-ensemble.md).

[Reflective openness](reflective-openness.md) depends on leaders modeling
vulnerability first. Saillant: people in an organization cannot be more open
and willing to learn than their leaders are. Baldwin at Roca: growth work starts
with "my own stuff."

Brigitte Tantawy-Monsou (Unilever) found organizational learning tools (personal
mastery, mental models, systems view) helped align personal and company goals,
eventually redefining her job around sustainability — illustrating how genuine
growth environments let people connect individual purpose to organizational
[purpose beyond profit](purpose-beyond-profit.md).
