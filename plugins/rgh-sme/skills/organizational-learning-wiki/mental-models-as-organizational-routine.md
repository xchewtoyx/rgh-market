title: Mental Models as Organizational Routine
description: >
  Surfacing and testing the assumptions behind positions — a collective
  inquiry practice that makes dialogue and learning possible.
sources:
  - title: "The Fifth Discipline: The Art and Practice of the Learning Organization"
    resource: "The Fifth Discipline (Senge), ch. 9, ch. 11"
---

Mental models are the assumptions and beliefs people hold — often invisible to
the holder — that shape how they interpret events and choose actions.
Organizational learning requires surfacing and testing these assumptions behind
positions, not just debating conclusions.

**Why the best ideas fail:** brilliant strategies often fail not from weak
intentions but because they conflict with deeply held internal images of how
the world works. Chris Argyris: people behave congruently with theories-in-use
(mental models), not always with espoused theories (what they say). Mental
models are **active** — they affect what we see, not just what we conclude.
Two people observe the same event differently because they attend to different
details.

**The problem is not whether models are right or wrong** — all models are
simplifications. It is when they become **implicit**, existing below awareness.
Detroit auto executives touring Japanese plants dismissed just-in-time inventory
as "staged" because it did not fit their image of a "real" plant. Unexamined
models remain unchanged as the world shifts; the gap widens and action becomes
counterproductive. Close-knit industries are especially vulnerable because
firms benchmark only each other.

**Organizational capacity** for mental-model work has three facets whose
connections matter most: (1) tools for personal awareness and reflective skills,
(2) infrastructures institutionalizing regular practice, (3) culture promoting
inquiry. Espousing "openness" without skills and practice opportunities fails.
[Reflective openness](reflective-openness.md) — looking inward at how one's own
thinking contributes to problems — is the cornerstone; participative openness
(speaking one's views) alone is "dangerously incomplete."

Corporate examples:

- **Shell:** Pierre Wack shifted scenario planning from producing documented
  futures to influencing decision-makers' "microcosms" — walking managers from
  current assumptions through how they'd manage in a seller's market. Planning
  became institutional learning (Arie de Geus: "planning as learning").
- **BP:** distributed decision power to ~150 profit centers while building
  cross-connecting networks and a climate of openly challenging thinking.
- **Hanover:** values of openness and merit as antidotes to hierarchy's
  "diseases" — gamesplaying and bureaucratic politics — combined with Argyris's
  tools for [defensive routines](defensive-routines.md) and [skilled incompetence](skilled-incompetence.md).
- **Harley-Davidson:** circle coaches facilitate getting different mental models
  into the open; progress sounds like "This is the way I am seeing things" rather
  than "This is the way things are."

[Suspending assumptions](suspending-assumptions.md) in dialogue makes mental
models visible as assumptions rather than facts. Reflection and inquiry skills
enable teams to distinguish thinking from thoughts.

This bundle treats mental models as **organizational routine** for learning
culture, not technical system modeling (which belongs in engineering domains).
Pairs with [systems thinking as learning language](systems-thinking-as-learning-language.md):
mental models expose hidden assumptions; systems thinking restructures them to
reveal leverage.
