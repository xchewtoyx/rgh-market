  Five personal learning disciplines must work together as an ensemble — like
  the DC-3's five component technologies — for the learning-organization
  innovation to succeed at scale.

Senge frames learning organizations through five **component disciplines** that
must converge as an ensemble — analogous to the McDonnell Douglas DC-3 (1935),
the first plane to support itself economically as well as aerodynamically by
combining five critical technologies: variable-pitch propeller, retractable
landing gear, lightweight monocoque construction, radial air-cooled engine, and
wing flaps. The Boeing 247 had all but wing flaps and was unstable; four of
five were not enough.

The five disciplines:

1. **[Systems thinking as learning language](systems-thinking-as-learning-language.md)**
   — seeing interrelated actions connecting distant events.
2. **Personal mastery** — continually clarifying personal vision and seeing
   reality objectively; the organization's capacity for learning can be no
   greater than its members'.
3. **[Mental models as organizational routine](mental-models-as-organizational-routine.md)**
   — surfacing deeply ingrained assumptions that block new insights from reaching
   practice.
4. **Shared vision** — genuine pictures of the future that foster commitment
   rather than compliance ([creative tension](creative-tension.md)).
5. **[Team learning](team-learning.md)** — dialogue, suspending assumptions,
   thinking together; teams are the fundamental learning unit.

"Discipline" (Latin *disciplina*, to learn) means a body of theory and technique
studied and mastered through practice — a developmental path, not enforced order.
You can never say "We are a learning organization" any more than "I am an
enlightened person." These differ from accounting-like management disciplines
in being **personal** — about how people think and interact.

Senge warns against emulating "best practices" piecemeal — a Toyota manager
noted visitors copy Kan-Ban, quality circles, and standard work descriptions but
"do not see the way all the parts work together." Great organizations are never
built by emulating another.

The convergence will not "create the learning organization" but spark "a new
wave of experimentation and advancement."
