---
type: concept
title: Leaps of Abstraction
description: >
  Untested jumps from observation to generalization that freeze mental models
  and block learning when treated as fact.
sources:
  - title: "The Fifth Discipline: The Art and Practice of the Learning Organization"
    resource: "The Fifth Discipline (Senge), ch. 9"
---

Our minds leap quickly from specific observations to broad generalizations —
"castles in the sky" about our own thinking more than we realize. Abstracting
strength becomes a learning limit when we are unaware of the leap.

Colleagues observing Laura's specific behaviors (rare praise, looking away,
curt reviews) concluded "Laura doesn't care about people" and treated that
generalization as fact. No one tested it; the fixed frame produced greater
indifference toward Laura, removing opportunity to show caring — a self-
reinforcing frozen state. Laura might have revealed she cared but had an
undisclosed hearing impediment.

In business, top managers convinced "customers buy on price; service quality
isn't a factor" never tested the leap — reinforced by discount requests — and
turned down service investment while a competitor grew share through service
customers had never experienced and therefore never asked for.

**To spot and test leaps:**

1. Ask what you believe about how the world works.
2. Ask what data the generalization is based on.
3. Ask whether you are willing to consider the generalization inaccurate — if
   not, stop; there is no point proceeding.
4. Explicitly separate generalization from data ("Paul Smith and several
   customers said X; thus I conclude Y") — put all cards on the table.
5. Where possible, test directly using [balancing inquiry and advocacy](balancing-inquiry-and-advocacy.md)
   skills rather than blunt confrontation.

Facts are observable data; inferences are not facts. Confusing the two prevents
testing — core to [mental models as organizational routine](mental-models-as-organizational-routine.md).
