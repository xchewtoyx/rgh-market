**Growth and underinvestment** combines [limits to growth](limits-to-growth-archetype.md)
and [shifting the burden](shifting-the-burden-archetype.md): a company limits its
own growth through underinvestment — building less capacity or maintaining lower
standards than needed to serve rising demand.

WonderTech, a composite high-tech startup, doubled sales annually for three years
then never sustained growth. Management pumped revenue into sales and marketing
while delivery times slipped from eight weeks to sixteen. The reinforcing loop
(more salespeople → more sales → more revenue) ran against a balancing loop
(disgruntled customers → harder closes → slower growth) — but financial metrics
masked delivery problems, and a six-month-or-longer delay separated worsening
delivery from visible sales difficulty ([delusion of learning from experience](delusion-of-learning-from-experience.md)).

Simultaneously, disgruntled customers walking away became the symptomatic fix
for delivery delays — reducing order volume without fixing capacity. After being
"stung" by idle capacity from a mistimed factory, management grew cautious,
weakening the fundamental response. The symptomatic response became faster;
the fundamental response more sluggish — "addicted to limiting its own growth."

**Recognition signs:**

- Failure to achieve potential despite everyone working extremely hard.
- Continuing financial stress — both cause and consequence of underinvestment.
- Eroding quality standards (product, service, delivery reliability).
- Disgruntled customers reducing demand, removing visible symptoms of unmet
  demand and available investment funds.

The overlooked leverage point was the original eight-week delivery standard —
silently eroded to ten weeks and beyond because financially preoccupied
management never took it seriously. Holding critical performance standards
"through thick and thin" — especially those customers care about most — is the
general management principle.

The pattern afflicted entire US manufacturing industries in the 1960s–70s,
often masked by advertising, discounting, restructuring, or tariff protection.
Gradual erosion makes it hard to see — structural underpinning of the
[boiled frog](boiled-frog.md) learning disability.

Used with [systems archetypes](systems-archetypes.md) to depersonalize why
sales leaders' structural arguments were dismissed as excuses for poor performance.
