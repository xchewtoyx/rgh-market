title: Groupthink
description: >
  A group-interaction failure mode where small cohesive teams develop shared
  illusions that suppress critical thinking and reality testing in pursuit of
  unanimity.
sources:
  - title: "Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction"
    resource: "Superforecasting (Tetlock & Gardner), ch. 9"
---

Psychologist Irving Janis coined **groupthink** in *Victims of Groupthink*
(1972): members of small cohesive groups "tend to maintain esprit de corps by
unconsciously developing a number of shared illusions and related norms that
interfere with critical thinking and reality testing." Groups that get along
too well stop questioning assumptions; unanimity itself becomes taken as
evidence the group is right — "we can't all be wrong, can we?"

The Bay of Pigs invasion (1961) is a canonical case. Kennedy's advisers
proceeded with a covert exile invasion despite a front-page leak and obvious
planning flaws — including a contingency plan copied from an earlier version
that assumed retreat to mountains 80 miles away across swamp and jungle when
the landing site changed. Historians across the political spectrum agree the
plan was badly flawed in ways visible at the time; the failure was of planning,
not execution.

The striking contrast: the **same team** resolved the Cuban Missile Crisis
(1962) successfully through negotiated blockade rather than invasion. Personnel
did not change; the group's decision-making process did. Kennedy's post–Bay of
Pigs inquiry identified "cozy unanimity" as the core problem — see
[countering groupthink](countering-groupthink.md) for the reforms applied in
1962.

Groupthink overlaps with the [myth of the management team](myth-of-the-management-team.md)
and other [learning disabilities](seven-learning-disabilities.md): teams avoid
embarrassment, suppress dissent, and mistake agreement for correctness. It is
the cooperative extreme that [team collective open-mindedness](team-collective-open-mindedness.md)
must avoid — the opposite extreme, rancorous dysfunction, is equally dangerous.

Groups can be wise or mad depending not only on who is in the room but on how
the group is designed and run. Deliberation collapses the independence that
makes aggregation work; loud voices, bullies, or credentialed figures can pull
a group toward shared error. [Precision questioning](precision-questioning.md)
and [productive conflict in learning teams](productive-conflict-in-learning-teams.md)
offer constructive alternatives.
