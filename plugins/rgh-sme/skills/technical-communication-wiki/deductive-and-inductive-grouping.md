---
type: concept
title: Deductive and Inductive Grouping
description: >
  Ideas grouped together under one point in a document must be related
  either deductively (a chain of reasoning ending in "therefore") or
  inductively (a set of similar things supporting one inference), never
  a mixture of both within the same group.
sources:
  - title: "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving"
    resource: "The Minto Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto), ch. 2, 5"
---

Within one grouping of a [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md), the ideas have to relate to each other in exactly one of two ways, and mixing the two inside a single group produces a group that doesn't actually cohere.

**Deduction** states a situation, comments further on its subject or predicate, and then draws an implication — "therefore" — that follows from the chain. The group's summary leans on that final conclusion. A reader has to be able to supply any steps a deductive chain skips, which puts a practical limit on how long a deductive chain can run before it stops being followable — as a rule of thumb, no more than about four points and two "therefore" links in a row.

**Induction** notices that several ideas, events, or facts are alike in some specific way, names the kind of thing they collectively are (with a plural noun), and infers a significance from that shared property. The two skills induction actually requires are defining the group precisely enough that its members really do share the claimed property, and finding misfits — items that don't actually belong and are quietly weakening the group's coherence. The resulting inference has to be something specifically justified by the members present, not a leap to a broader claim than the evidence supports.

A quick test for which relationship a pair of sentences has: if the second sentence comments on the subject or predicate of the first, the relationship is deductive; if the sentences instead have to share membership in some named plural-noun class before an inference follows, the relationship is inductive. Facts that are individually true "news" but share no common subject/predicate and support no resulting inference don't belong together in a document meant to communicate reasoning, however factually accurate they are.

Induction is generally preferred at a document's key-line level — the top tier of support directly beneath the main point — because it lets the reader see what to do or believe first and keeps each recommendation attached to its own supporting case; a deductive key line forces the reader to replay the writer's reasoning before reaching the point, which is worth doing only when the conclusion is genuinely unexpected or can't be understood without the reasoning behind it. Neither form is inherently stronger — the choice is about which layout serves the reader, not about which kind of logic is more rigorous.
