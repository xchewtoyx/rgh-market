Metaphor is not only decorative; used well, it takes a technical or abstract idea further than literal clarity alone can — historical facts as fish on a fishmonger's slab that different historians "cook and serve" differently, or lepton pairs as the "offspring" of a "parent" virtual photon. Some writers use metaphor mainly to intensify an already-clear point; others use it mainly to explain a genuinely hard concept, sometimes with a bit of play — either use is legitimate, and the second is directly useful for [translating technical material for general readers](translating-technical-material-for-general-readers.md).

A metaphor fails in one of two ways. First, **mixed metaphor**: once a metaphor is introduced, its own literal logic creates expectations, and switching to a second, inconsistent metaphor partway through breaks them — describing values as something "born" through "osmotic flow" (a slow, invisible process) and then having conflicting values "collide" (a sudden, violent one) asks the same image to mean two incompatible things at once, and piling a third metaphor ("spawns new values") on top just reads as silly. Second, a metaphor can unintentionally **revive its own buried literal meaning** in an embarrassing way — military-jargon prose describing an army "running amuck in the defender's undefended rear" after "penetrating his forward defenses" resurrects meanings the writer didn't intend, precisely because the writer stopped noticing the metaphor was a metaphor.

The underlying skill, per Aristotle's observation that mastering metaphor "is a sign of genius" because it requires "an intuitive perception of similarity among dissimilars," is not avoiding metaphor but staying aware of the image once it's introduced, checking that the image's own logic still holds a sentence or two later. There is no way to guarantee this in advance; the only fix once a metaphor has visibly gone wrong is to notice and cut it — even confident, skilled writers produce failed metaphors regularly, and the recommended response to an embarrassing one is to revise it and keep trying, not to abandon metaphor as a technique.

A third failure mode is more consequential than either kind of internal inconsistency: a metaphor that stays perfectly self-consistent but gets the underlying thing's actual behavior wrong, so a reader who trusts the image ends up with a false expectation about what to watch for. Explaining a heart attack via "the heart is a balloon that bursts" is vivid and coherent on its own terms, but it implies a sudden, unheralded event — when the more accurate picture, a muscular pump that clogs, often gradually, produces detectable warning signs well before a crisis (skin discoloration, swelling, changes in exertion tolerance). A reader taught the balloon version has no reason to look for those signs, because nothing in that image suggests they'd exist. This failure can't be caught by checking the metaphor's internal consistency the way a mixed metaphor can — the balloon image is entirely consistent with itself — which means the check has to be external: does the chosen image's own logic actually match the mechanism being explained, not just whether the image holds together on its own terms.

When the goal is teaching a reader to actually *do* something rather than just understand a static idea, the strongest metaphors are the ones that trade on a skill the reader already has, not merely a concept they already know. A tennis instructor telling a student that a net volley "is like pushing a pie into someone's face" outperforms a technical breakdown of leg spacing, elbow angle, and swing arc, because the pie metaphor imports an entire already-coordinated motion (a short, decisive push) in one move, where the itemized breakdown leaves the student to assemble the pieces into one fluid action from scratch — decomposing a coordinated action into separately-explained parts defers the genuinely hard part, coordinating them, to the very end of learning, while a good action-metaphor makes that coordination the starting point. A metaphor that only supplies a label, without connecting to any skill the reader has already practiced, doesn't get this benefit: telling a pilot that a failed hydraulic system is "sick" gives the failure a name but no well-rehearsed response to reach for, unlike telling the same pilot to fly in formation on a visual reference, which borrows a genuinely well-drilled coordinated skill wholesale. The test for this kind of explanatory metaphor is not just whether the image is accurate and consistent, but whether a reader who has never done the task before comes away knowing what to physically or procedurally *do*, because the metaphor pointed at something they can already do.
