title: Cutting Clutter
description: >
  Clutter — inflated diction, redundant modifiers, throat-clearing phrases,
  and euphemism — is the main enemy of clear prose and should be stripped
  word by word until every remaining word earns its place.
sources:

Clutter is not a stylistic flaw but the default state of unedited prose: needless particles ("free up" instead of "free"), empty intensifiers, adjectives that state the obvious, wordy substitutes for short words ("due to the fact that" instead of "because"), and throat-clearing phrases that delay the point. Institutional and bureaucratic language is a special case — euphemisms like "involuntary methodologies" or "collateral damage" do not just clutter a sentence, they conceal responsibility and reality, which makes cutting clutter partly an ethical act, not only a stylistic one.

The test for any word or phrase is functional, not aesthetic: does it do work the sentence needs, or can the sentence survive without it? Bracketing or deleting each questionable component exposes redundancies and bloated constructions that are otherwise invisible on a normal read. This is a distinct pass from checking whether the content is correct — it is a search for words that are simply not earning their place.

Five concrete edits catch most clutter in a draft, applied in this order:

1. **Delete words that mean little or nothing** — filler used as unconsciously as clearing one's throat: "kind of," "actually," "particular," "certain," "really," "basically."
2. **Delete words that repeat the meaning of other words** — doubled pairs left over from a habit of pairing a Latinate word with its native-English synonym: "full and complete," "each and every," "first and foremost." Keep one word of the pair, cut the other.
3. **Delete words implied by other words** — a redundant modifier ("future events," since "predict" already implies the future), a redundant category noun ("pink in color" needs only "pink"), or a diffuse general implication spread across a whole clause ("trying to learn the rules for playing the game of chess" needs only "learning the rules of chess").
4. **Replace a wordy phrase with a single word** — "due to the fact that" becomes "because," "in the event that" becomes "if," "prior to" becomes "before." This is the hardest of the five to apply mechanically since it takes vocabulary and alertness rather than a rule, but it is also where several nominalizations quietly turn back into verbs — see [diagnosing nominalizations](diagnosing-nominalizations.md).
5. **Change negatives to affirmatives** — a negative construction costs an extra word and makes the reader do a small algebraic conversion to recover the actual meaning ("not different" for "same"); state the affirmative directly unless the negation itself is the point. Watch especially for combining an explicit "not" with an implicitly negative word (preclude, fail, unless, without) — stacking two negatives is far harder to parse than either alone.

Clear thinking and clear writing are inseparable: a cluttered sentence is usually evidence of unclear thinking about what the writer is actually trying to say, not just a surface polish problem. There is also a floor to this process: cutting a passage as hard as it will possibly go often produces prose that is technically tight but "all gristle and bone," having bled out whatever made it pleasant to read — there's no fixed rule for how far to cut, and the only reliable check is how the result reads to an actual reader, not how few words remain.
