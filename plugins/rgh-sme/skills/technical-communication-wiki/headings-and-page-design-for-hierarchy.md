type: concept
title: Headings and Page Design for Hierarchy
description: >
  Visual devices — titles, headings, numbering, and indentation — should
  make a document's pyramid structure visible on the page, and headings
  in particular must represent real, parallel divisions of thought
  rather than just naming a subject.
sources:
  - title: "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving"
    resource: "The Minto Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto), ch. 10"
---

Page design exists to make a document's underlying [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md) visible to the reader's eye, which in turn serves the reader's mind: reflect the main point in the title, state the introduction and the key-line points early, and use headings, underlining, numbering, or indentation to let the major points "jump out" rather than requiring the reader to extract the hierarchy from undifferentiated prose.

Headings carry real structural obligations, not just labeling ones: never introduce a heading level with only one member (a lone subheading under a section implies a division that doesn't actually exist); write headings at the same level in parallel grammatical form; keep the wording down to the essence of the point rather than a full sentence; treat headings as outside the prose, not as its first clause; introduce each group of headings before diving into the first one; and don't add heading levels beyond what the actual structure needs. A well-built table of contents, built from a document's headings, should function as a precis of the whole argument — a reader should be able to reconstruct the document's key-line points from the table of contents alone.

Underlined or numbered point lists demand the same rigor as headings: strict question-answer logic between levels (see [the vertical question-answer relationship](vertical-question-answer-relationship.md)), short proposition-style wording rather than full explanatory sentences, and a real limit on how many items sit at one level before the list becomes a dump. Decimal numbering can help with later reference but only works well paired with meaningful headings, not as a substitute for them — and introductory, transitional, or concluding prose should never be numbered as though it were itself a support point. For short documents, a simple indented display can make a group's structure visible without full heading machinery; a "lap visual" outline of short, direct, parallel statements with clearly indented subordinate points suits material meant to be read together with the audience, like a progress review.

Transitions between sections should restate what the adjacent sections actually say — a brief S-C-Q-style recap or a reference back to a key word or idea — rather than mechanically naming the section that follows ("Section 3 discusses..."). A genuine conclusion should add significance or motivate action, not simply repeat what the key line already stated; a "next steps" section, if used, should be limited to actions that are logically uncontroversial given everything already established, not a place to sneak in a new, unargued recommendation.
