---
type: concept
title: Selection and Unity
description: >
  A clear piece of writing is defined as much by what the writer deliberately
  leaves out as by what it includes, held together by one sustained
  organizing point of view.
sources:
  - title: On Writing Well
    resource: "On Writing Well, 30th Anniversary Edition (William Zinsser), ch. 8, 23"
---

Unity comes from constraint, not coverage. A writer decides what a piece is about — and, just as importantly, what it is *not* about — and then cuts material that is interesting but diverts from that governing point of view, however much effort went into gathering it. Trying to include everything known about a subject produces the opposite of clarity: a reader's subconscious need for order is met only when the writer visibly controls these choices, not when every fact is preserved out of a reluctance to waste research.

This selection has to stay consistent at the mechanical level too: a piece needs one sustained principal pronoun, tense, mood, and attitude. Deliberate variation is possible, but uncontrolled switching between them disorients the reader independently of whether the content itself is well chosen.

Because writing is a sequence of choices — subject, angle, order, detail, [what to omit](selecting-content-by-stakeholder-concerns.md) — made under no fixed rule, the writer's job is to keep making them in the reader's favor: orienting the reader through structure and transitions, and [revising a choice when later discovery proves it wrong](separating-drafting-from-revision.md), rather than defending an early decision out of inertia.
