A number chosen for display should already be the [comparison the reader actually needs](numbers-need-comparative-context.md) — a raw figure without a target, baseline, or prior value to measure it against communicates little regardless of how it's formatted.

Whether to represent information visually or in prose is a decision, not a default — choose a visual when it reveals a pattern, comparison, process, relationship, or quantity more efficiently than a paragraph would; choose prose when interpretation, nuance, or qualification matters more than the shape of the data itself. Within "use a visual," the specific graphic form still has to match the task: a table for exact values a reader needs to look up precisely, a chart for a comparative pattern or a change over time, a diagram or map for a relationship or spatial arrangement that prose would have to painstakingly reconstruct in words. The wrong graphic form for the task — a chart when a reader actually needs to read off exact values, a table when a reader actually needs to see a trend — costs as much clarity as no visual at all.

A figure is part of the argument and needs the same design discipline as a sentence: an informative title (not just a label describing its type, e.g. "Figure 3," but what it shows), clear axis labels, units, a legend where more than one series or category is shown, and a visible source for the underlying data. Avoid distortions that misrepresent the actual data even unintentionally — a truncated axis that exaggerates a difference, a proportion rendered visually misleading, decorative elements that compete with the data for attention, or a level of precision the underlying data doesn't actually support.

A formal check for that last failure catches most accidental distortion: the **principle of proportional ink** states that when shaded area represents a numerical value, the area used must be directly proportional to that value. This is why a bar chart has to start at zero — it uses area to represent magnitude, so truncating the axis breaks the proportionality between ink and value even without any intent to mislead — while a line chart, which uses position rather than area to show a trend, is exempt from that particular rule (though it can still mislead if the plotted range obscures or exaggerates the trend it's meant to show). The same check catches less obvious violations: encoding one ratio into both the width and height of a shape (area then scales as the *square* of the underlying value, badly understating it), donut or ring charts where outer rings sweep more physical area per unit value than inner rings at the same angular width, and any 3D chart, where perspective rendering systematically gives unequal ink to bars or wedges of equal true value depending on their position in the frame. This is also why pie charts, bubble charts, and other area- or angle-based encodings are risky even when drawn correctly: humans are reliably bad at judging 2-D area and angle, one of the weakest [preattentive channels](preattentive-attributes-for-rapid-visual-perception.md) for the "how much more" judgment those charts are asking the reader to make — a simple bar graph, which relies on line length and position instead, usually communicates the same comparison more accurately in less space.

Two named failure patterns are worth checking a draft against even without any suspicion of deliberate deception. A **"duck"** (after a building shaped like its own product) is a visualization where decorative form has overwhelmed the data — lipstick tubes standing in for bar-chart bars, a pie chart bent into a novelty shape — which may not misrepresent any single number but still impairs a reader's ability to extract the actual data from the noise around it. A **"glass slipper"** is a visualization that borrows a visual form's implied rigor for data that doesn't actually have the structure that form assumes — a Venn diagram used as a decorative container for unrelated percentages with no real set relationship between them, or a subway-map or periodic-table aesthetic applied to content with no genuine topological or sequential structure underneath it. Both failures are worth checking for even in good-faith work, because the visual form itself is quietly making a claim about the data's structure that the underlying numbers may not support.

A visual does not interpret itself, and dropping one into a document without surrounding text asks the reader to do work the writer should have done: introduce a figure before it appears (what is this, why is it here), tell the reader explicitly what to notice in it (the specific pattern or comparison that matters, since a reader can look at the same chart and notice something different from what the writer intended), and explain its bearing on the claim it's supporting. This is the visual equivalent of [communicating evidence in prose](storyboarding-an-argument.md) — evidence, visual or textual, doesn't argue for itself; the surrounding explanation is what turns a fact into support for a claim.
