---
type: concept
title: Numbers Need Comparative Context
description: >
  A bare number communicates almost nothing on its own — it becomes
  meaningful only when paired with something the reader can measure it
  against, so every reported figure should carry or point to its
  comparison.
sources:
  - title: "Information Dashboard Design"
    resource: "Information Dashboard Design (Stephen Few), ch. 2-3"
---

"Quarter-to-date sales total $736,502" tells a reader almost nothing by itself: is that good or bad, on track or behind, an improvement or a decline? A raw figure only becomes informative once it's placed against something — the same measure at an earlier point in time, a target or budget, a forecast, a norm or benchmark, someone else's version of the same measure, or a related measure that gives it scale. Reporting a number without deciding what it should be compared to leaves the reader to supply their own comparison, which they often can't do from the number alone.

This means choosing a comparison is a required step in presenting any measure, not an optional enrichment. The choice should follow directly from what the reader needs to decide: if the real question is "how far off budget are we," reporting actual and budget as separate figures forces the reader to compute the gap themselves — reporting the variance directly (e.g., "-9%, -$8,066") answers the actual question in one step. A measure that's technically accurate but answers the wrong question — actual and budget shown separately when variance is what's needed — wastes the reader's attention as surely as an error would.

The comparison should also stay visually or textually subordinate to the primary figure it supports — supplying rich context (several comparisons at once: to budget, to last year, to a longer trend) adds real value, but only if the primary number stays dominant and the supporting comparisons are visibly secondary, so a reader scanning quickly isn't forced to sort out which figure is the point and which is context. See [communicating evidence visually](communicating-evidence-visually.md) for how this same judgment extends to choosing a graphic form once a comparison has been decided on, and [trimming precision to what the decision needs](trimming-precision-to-what-the-decision-needs.md) for the companion failure of supplying too much of the wrong kind of detail instead of too little context.
