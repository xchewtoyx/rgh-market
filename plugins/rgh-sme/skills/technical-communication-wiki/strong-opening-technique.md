---
type: concept
title: Strong Opening Technique
description: >
  An opening's only job is to make the reader want the next sentence, which
  favors a surprising, specific detail over a generic scene-setting
  preamble.
sources:
  - title: On Writing Well
    resource: "On Writing Well, 30th Anniversary Edition (William Zinsser), ch. 9"
---

The opening of a piece has one job: earning the reader's commitment to the next sentence, not summarizing the piece or establishing credentials first. Gathering more raw material than will be used gives the writer the surplus to find one odd, specific detail that can do this work, rather than defaulting to a generic scene-setter. Exhausted opening devices — a contrived anecdote, a rhetorical question with an obvious answer, a device that could preface almost any piece on almost any topic — fail precisely because they could belong to any piece; a strong opening could only belong to this one.

The corresponding close deserves equal deliberate craft rather than a mechanical summary of what was just said: when the point has been made, the piece should stop. A closing line that surprises while still feeling exactly right — by encapsulating the point, echoing the opening, or landing on a well-chosen closing detail — sends the reader out of the piece with more than a restatement would.

This is a narrative technique rather than the [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md)'s answer-first ordering used in reference and business documents — the two solve different problems: an opening earns attention before the reader knows the point, while pyramid ordering states the point immediately because the reader is there to extract it. Which one applies depends on whether the reader chose to read for the experience or needs the conclusion to act on.
