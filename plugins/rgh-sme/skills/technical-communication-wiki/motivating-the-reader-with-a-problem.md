---
type: concept
title: Motivating the Reader with a Problem
description: >
  Readers engage attentively with a document when its introduction frames
  the content as the answer to a problem they already care about, not as
  a topic that merely happens to interest the writer.
sources:
  - title: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace"
    resource: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, 9th ed. (Joseph M. Williams, Joseph Bizup), Lesson 10"
---

A reader given an interesting topic reads passively; a reader given a problem they recognize as their own reads attentively, because they no longer need to be persuaded to care about the solution. From the moment a document is planned, the governing question is not "what do I want to say about this topic" but "what problem does my reader want to see solved" — and the introduction is the one place a writer gets to make that case, since a reader who doesn't see why the content matters may simply stop.

A **problem**, in this sense, has two required parts: a **condition** (some situation, event, or open question) and a **cost** — the consequence of that condition that the reader doesn't want to pay and wants eliminated or answered. Naming the condition alone ("binge drinking," "rising tuition") is not enough; a callous or uninvested reader can always ask "so what?", and the introduction has to answer that question explicitly or the reader has no reason to keep going.

Two kinds of problems need different treatment. A **practical problem**'s condition can be anything that makes the reader unhappy, and its cost is stated directly as a consequence in the world; it is solved by getting the reader (or someone) to do something. A **conceptual problem**'s condition is always something not yet known or understood, often expressible as a question, and its cost is something bigger and more significant that also isn't understood as a result; it is solved with information or understanding, not action. Recognizing which kind of problem a document poses determines what its solution has to deliver.

Most effective introductions in this pattern run three parts: first, **establish shared context** the reader will readily agree with — often opening with what looks like settled background, signaled by a pivot word ("but," "however") that flags the coming qualification; second, **state the problem** in its condition-and-cost form; third, **state the solution** — for a practical problem, what the reader or someone should do; for a conceptual problem, what the writer wants the reader to understand or believe. A "prelude" — a quotation, a startling fact, or a short anecdote — can open before this structure to catch attention, but the strongest hook is still a problem the reader recognizes as needing a solution.

A conclusion can be built by running the same structure in reverse: restate the point/solution, answer "so what?" again (now as an achieved benefit rather than a looming cost), raise a further question the piece doesn't resolve, and optionally close on an anecdote or fact that echoes the opening. This problem-first framing is the motivational counterpart to [the pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md)'s ordering discipline — pyramid structure controls what comes first once the reader is already engaged; this technique is what earns that engagement in the first place.
