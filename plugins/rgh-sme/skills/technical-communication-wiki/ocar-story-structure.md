title: OCAR Story Structure
description: >
  A technical document reads clearly when it is shaped as a story with
  four parts — Opening, Challenge, Action, Resolution — rather than a
  flat report of what was done and found.
sources:
  - title: "Writing Science: How to Write Papers That Get Cited and Proposals That Get Funded"
    resource: "Writing Science (Joshua Schimel), ch. 2, 4, 8-9"
---

A technical document is not raw data; it is an evidence-grounded story about what a piece of work found and why that matters, and the most-cited papers and best-funded proposals make that story visible rather than leaving readers to reconstruct it. The **OCAR** model names its four parts: the **Opening** frames the driving problem, introduces the relevant "characters" (which may be organisms, systems, or concepts rather than people), and signals who the piece is for; the **Challenge** states the specific question the work answers; the **Action** presents the approach and the evidence produced by it; the **Resolution** explains how the work changes what readers understand, closing the loop back to the problem raised in the Opening.

A satisfying OCAR is a spiral, not a straight line: the Resolution returns to the Opening's problem at a new level of understanding, rather than just stopping once results are reported. The document also has an hourglass shape — a broad problem narrows through the Challenge and Action to a specific piece of evidence, then widens back out to a conclusion pitched at roughly the same breadth as the opening problem. A useful test for a draft: the Opening, Challenge, and Resolution should each be intelligible in isolation, without needing the Action's detail to make sense — a reader skimming just those three parts should still get the whole story.

IMRaD (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion) is the physical section convention many technical documents use; OCAR is the underlying conceptual structure those sections carry. The Introduction contains the Opening, the funnel of background (see [the funnel technique](the-funnel-technique.md)), and the Challenge; Methods and Results carry the Action; Discussion develops the rest of the Action and delivers the Resolution. A document can use different section labels and still succeed or fail by whether it delivers a genuine OCAR underneath them.

Within the Action, treat Methods, Results, and Discussion as story rather than inventory: explain why each method, result, or argument belongs and how it advances the Challenge, rather than just listing what was done. Select results by their value to the story, not by how much effort produced them — condensing minor or covarying data and using figures to show patterns usually serves readers better than including everything gathered. Keep three levels distinct as the Action unfolds: the data itself, the robust inference drawn directly from it, and the broader interpretation built on that inference — ordering data before interpretation signals to readers that the conclusion follows from evidence rather than the reverse.

The Resolution is not a summary of results; it is the answer to "so what?" — see [motivating the reader with a problem](motivating-the-reader-with-a-problem.md) for the equivalent principle applied to introductions generally. A good Resolution reorients the reader to the main question, works through the evidence and any competing explanations, and lands a conclusion scaled to match the size of the problem the Opening raised — a narrow study should claim a narrow, real insight rather than overclaiming, and should not end on caveats alone, which leaves the caveat as the reader's final impression (see [disclosing limitations early](disclosing-limitations-early.md)). Proposals resolve prospectively: instead of reporting a finding, they state what new understanding the proposed work would make possible and why that matters.

See [anatomy of a case story](anatomy-of-a-case-story.md) for the parallel structure used for a different genre — an incident writeup or case study meant to transfer hard-won expertise rather than report a scientific result.
