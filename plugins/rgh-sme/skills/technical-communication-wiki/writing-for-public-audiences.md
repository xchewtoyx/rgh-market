A specialist reader and a public reader move through material along different paths. Public audiences follow something closer to data → information → knowledge → understanding → application, and at nearly every step they're implicitly asking "how does this affect me?" — a question a specialist audience, already invested in the subject, doesn't need answered explicitly. Writing that connects a piece of work's intrinsic interest to an honest implication for the reader's own life (or to a genuine "who knew?" surprise) succeeds with this audience in a way that simply restating the technical finding does not — see [translating technical material for general readers](translating-technical-material-for-general-readers.md) and [strong opening technique](strong-opening-technique.md), which this specializes for a public rather than a specialist reader.

Practically, this means leading with a simple, front-loaded lead-development-resolution shape (see [matching document structure to reader patience](matching-document-structure-to-reader-patience.md)), foregrounding human characters where the material allows it, and either defining technical terms in context or avoiding them — never opening with jargon. None of this means talking down to the reader: the goal is treating the audience as intelligent but unfamiliar with the field's specifics, not as people who need the content simplified past the point of being true.

A **message box** is a drafting tool that forces the translation to happen explicitly rather than by feel: state the overarching **Issue** and the **Audience** being addressed, then work through **Problem** (the specific gap or question), **So what?** (why the audience should care), **Solution** (what the work found or proposes), and **Benefits** (what changes for the audience as a result). Filling in every cell is what prevents the common failure of public science writing — stating an interesting finding without ever connecting it to why a non-specialist reader should care. A worked example: research on gene regulation in zebrafish doesn't itself cure cancer, but a message box forces the writer to state the plausible chain connecting the gene-regulation finding to the public's actual cancer concern, without overclaiming an immediate cure. The same box structure works for framing funding proposals to non-specialist reviewers, not only for public communication.

A further-removed audience — policymakers — has even less patience than the general public, and operates under political and managerial constraints that aren't resolved by clarity alone; reaching that audience takes the same clear-writing discipline plus a separate understanding of their incentives, vocabulary, and what specific action they're being asked to take.
