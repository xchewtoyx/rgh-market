---
type: concept
title: Conditions-Actions-Needs Status Briefing
description: >
  A time-pressured status update stays both complete and brief when it's
  organized into three fixed parts — what's currently true, what's being
  done about it, and what's still required — instead of a free-form
  narration of everything the speaker knows.
sources:
  - title: "Incident Management for Operations"
    resource: "Incident Management for Operations (Schnepp, Vidal, Hawley), ch. 3"
---

A status update given under time pressure — to a newly-joined participant, to a stakeholder checking in, or to reset a group's shared understanding mid-task — tends to drift toward one of two failure modes: an unstructured narration that includes everything the speaker currently knows in whatever order it occurs to them, or a status so compressed it omits what a listener actually needs to act. A fixed three-part structure avoids both: **Conditions** (the current state of things, stated as fact), **Actions** (what is being done about it right now), and **Needs** (what additional resources, decisions, or information are required to keep progressing). Because the three parts are fixed in advance, a speaker can fill each one briefly without having to improvise what to include, and a listener can extract exactly the piece they need — the current state to reorient, the actions to avoid duplicating effort, the needs to know whether they can help.

This structure earns its place specifically where a status has to be given repeatedly, to different audiences, under time pressure — a periodic check-in during an ongoing effort, a briefing to someone who just joined partway through, or a summary to someone with less context who needs the state of play in one pass. Repeating the same three-part shape every time also gives listeners the [same benefit consistent visual encoding gives a reader](consistent-visual-encoding-across-a-document.md): once they've learned to expect Conditions-Actions-Needs, they can parse each new briefing faster, because they already know where in the update to listen for the piece that matters most to them.

The discipline of forcing every update into exactly these three buckets also catches a common omission on its own: a speaker who has no "Needs" to report has either genuinely resolved everything or hasn't stopped to consider what would actually help — making the Needs section an explicit, recurring prompt to ask for help rather than silently pushing through.

This is the reporting-upward counterpart to [communicating intent, not just instructions](communicating-intent-not-just-instructions.md), which covers the same discipline applied to instructions flowing the other way — from someone assigning a task to someone who has to carry it out.
