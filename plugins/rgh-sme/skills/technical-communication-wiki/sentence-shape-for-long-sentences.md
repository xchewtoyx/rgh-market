title: Sentence Shape for Long Sentences
description: >
  A long sentence stays clear when it reaches its point quickly through a
  short subject-verb-object core and then extends that core with
  modifiers and parallel coordination instead of a chain of tacked-on
  clauses.
sources:
  - title: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace"
    resource: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, 9th ed. (Joseph M. Williams, Joseph Bizup), Lesson 8"
---

Length is not what makes a sentence hard to read; shape is. A writer who can only produce clear sentences under twenty words hasn't learned economy, they've avoided the harder skill of writing something long that still holds together. Readers experience a sentence as shapeless sprawl for four reasons: it doesn't open with its point, it takes too long to reach the verb of the main clause, the material after the verb piles up as an undifferentiated string of subordinate clauses, or readers hit one interruption after another. A rough diagnostic: mark sentences longer than two lines and read them aloud — running out of breath before the parts resolve into a single whole, or feeling the sentence "stop and start," signals the same problem a silent reader will hit.

**Get to the point, then to the verb, quickly.** In a long sentence, find the single claim you most want readers to grasp and put it in a short opening main clause, adding supporting or qualifying material afterward. Then keep the path to that clause's verb short: avoid opening with a long introductory phrase or clause (move it to the end, or make it its own sentence — except an if-clause, which usually belongs early because it refers to something already known); keep the subject itself short (a long nominalized subject or one carrying a heavy relative clause should be rebuilt — see [diagnosing nominalizations](diagnosing-nominalizations.md)); and never wedge a long interruption between subject and verb, or between verb and object — move it to the sentence's beginning or end instead.

**After the verb, extend the line without stacking.** Once a sentence has an uninterrupted subject-verb-object core, readers can absorb more complexity — but not an undifferentiated pile of relative clauses each hung off the last one ("...genetic engineering, which is a new way of manipulating...which are the genes...that tell...that constitute..."). Three ways to fix a stack: cut a relative clause down to a phrase; break it into its own independent sentence; or convert it into one of three modifier types, each set off by a comma at the end of a clause:

- A **resumptive modifier** repeats a key word from just before the break point, then extends it with a new restrictive clause — "...we need a word to name what I am about to do in this sentence, a sentence that I could have ended at that comma, but extended to show you how resumptive modifiers work."
- A **summative modifier** sums up everything before the break in one noun phrase, then continues with a "that" clause — "...has reduced Russian population growth to less than zero, a demographic event that will have serious social implications."
- A **free modifier** doesn't repeat or summarize anything; it comments on the subject of the nearest verb, usually opening with an "-ing" participle, and — unlike the other two — can sit at the beginning, middle, or end of a sentence.

**Coordination**, joining parallel phrases or clauses with and/or/but, is what lets a sentence stay long and well-formed rather than sprawling through one tacked-on subordinate clause after another. A general design principle applies: order coordinated elements from shorter to longer and simpler to more complex — ending on the short element makes a sentence feel like it stops abruptly, while building toward the longer, more elaborated element gives it a satisfying close. Coordinate elements must match grammatically (clause with clause, phrase with phrase) and, just as important, must actually be parallel in thought — chaining unrelated claims together with "and" just because they're both true doesn't make them coordinate, and produces exactly the kind of shapeless prose this technique is meant to fix.

None of this rescues content that doesn't make sense: flawless shaping devices can produce a syntactically well-formed sentence whose underlying chain of ideas is still incoherent. Shape is necessary, not sufficient.
