Before writing about a subject domain, interview the people who already understand it — not to gather feature requests, but to extract the domain's important concepts, the relationships between them, and whatever complexity resists easy explanation. Framing this distinction explicitly at the start of the interview matters: people with a grievance about an existing product will want to use the time to vent about it, and the interviewer's job is to acknowledge that politely and defer it, not to let it take over a conversation meant to map the subject itself.

Prepare with desk research beforehand so the interviewer can drive the conversation with informed questions, then deliberately practice **professional naiveté** — asking an expert to explain things in their own words as though the interviewer doesn't already know the answer, even where they do. This surfaces the expert's own vocabulary and mental model rather than just confirming what the interviewer already assumed. When two experts give conflicting explanations of the same thing, treat that as a genuine finding rather than noise to average away — real domains are often messier than any one expert's account of them, and the contradiction may be exactly what the eventual content needs to represent.

A practical time budget for a one-hour interview: a short introduction explaining who you are and why you're there; a few minutes setting context so the expert understands why the questions might seem oddly specific; the bulk of the time on broad-to-detailed questioning, listening specifically for the **nouns** the expert uses — the concepts they name — and noting each one for follow-up; a closing summary where the interviewer reflects their understanding back to the expert for correction; and thanks, including an offer to follow up and a request for other experts worth talking to. Deliberately ending the scheduled interview a few minutes early is worth planning for — an interviewee often has a late flash of insight while packing up that the formal question sequence never reached.

The direct output of this process is a table of terms and their definitions, gathered from what the expert actually said rather than from any pre-existing glossary — this becomes the foundation for a shared, precise vocabulary the rest of a project can build on (see [expert-versus-audience vocabulary](expert-versus-audience-vocabulary.md)) and the raw material for structuring content around the domain's real concepts (see [structuring content for multi-channel reuse](structuring-content-for-multi-channel-reuse.md)). In-person interviews, conducted where the expert actually works, tend to surface more than a remote call — contextual cues around the expert prompt detail that pure recall alone doesn't.

Asking for a recent nonroutine or exceptional case, rather than asking generally about the domain or about routine handling, is a specific and reliable lever for getting past an expert's rehearsed, generic answers: a general question tends to produce the same tired summary an expert has given many times before, while a request for a specific hard case they remember pulls out a genuine story, complete with the details, judgment calls, and context that a general answer omits. Experts are also often glad to be asked this way, since narrating their own hard-won expertise is something people enjoy doing, not a chore — cooperation and engagement noticeably improve once the question is "tell me about a time when..." rather than "how does this generally work." A narrow, decontextualized question ("how do you judge a tricky case?") reliably produces a vague, almost mystical-sounding non-answer from a genuine expert — the fault lies in the question's abstraction, not in some ineffable quality of the expertise itself; anchoring every question to the specific incident already on the table is what keeps answers concrete.

Once a promising incident surfaces, a second pass extracts far more than the first telling did. A workable structure: get a brief first telling to judge whether the incident is actually rich enough to be worth the time; get a full retelling and pin its events onto an explicit timeline, which surfaces inconsistencies the first telling glossed over; then probe the thought process directly — what was noticed at each point the expert's understanding shifted, what alternatives existed and whether they were considered, and "what if" counterfactuals (pick a pivotal element of the story and ask "now imagine that hadn't happened — why not, and what would that have meant?"). A further, high-value probe, time permitting: ask explicitly where a less experienced person would have gotten confused or made a mistake at each choice point — this reliably surfaces exactly the tacit judgment the expert wouldn't otherwise think to mention, since it's invisible to them precisely because it's second nature.
