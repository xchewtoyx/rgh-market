---
type: concept
title: Pyramid Structure
description: >
  Organize a document as a pyramid — the governing point stated first,
  then the grouped ideas that support it, then the evidence beneath
  those — so the reader receives the answer before being asked to
  reconstruct the writer's reasoning.
sources:
  - title: "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving"
    resource: "The Minto Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto), ch. 1"
---

The mind instinctively groups incoming ideas, imposes order on them, and looks for the point a group is making — working memory can hold only a handful of unrelated items at once, fewer if the ideas are complex, so a reader forced to hold ungrouped facts in mind while waiting for their significance is being asked to do work the writer should have done first. A pyramid structure does that grouping work for the reader: it states the top-level answer or main point first, then the key-line points that support it, then the detailed evidence supporting each of those — top-down, from most abstract to most concrete, rather than in the chronological order the writer discovered them or a flat list of facts.

Each level of the pyramid is simultaneously a summary of the level below it and a question the level below answers, which gives the reader a continuing question-and-answer dialogue rather than a monologue — see [the vertical question-answer relationship](vertical-question-answer-relationship.md). This structure also lets a reader self-select their depth: someone satisfied by the top-level point can stop there, and someone who wants the reasoning can descend a level at a time, because each level is genuinely a coherent summary rather than a teaser that requires the detail to make sense.

A pyramid can be built two ways. **Top-down** works when the writer already knows the reader's question and the answer: state the answer, then work out what new question that answer raises in the reader's mind, and supply the key-line points that answer it. **Bottom-up** works when the material is known but its meaning isn't yet: list the available points, work out the logical relationships between them, and draw the conclusion those relationships support — then reconstruct the introduction afterward once the actual top point is clear. Both methods must converge on the same end state: a structure ordered by increasing abstraction toward the top, not by the sequence in which ideas were discovered or by an unordered dump of everything gathered.

Three governing rules apply at every level: ideas at any level must genuinely summarize the ideas grouped below them, rather than just labeling the group (see [summarizing grouped ideas](summarizing-grouped-ideas.md)); ideas within one grouping must share one logical relationship — see [deductive and inductive grouping](deductive-and-inductive-grouping.md) and [imposing logical order on groupings](imposing-logical-order-on-groupings.md); and the introduction must establish the question the reader actually has before the top point tries to answer it — see [the situation-complication-question introduction](situation-complication-question-introduction.md). A pyramid is a discipline for thinking through a document's argument before writing it, not a formatting template applied after the fact.
