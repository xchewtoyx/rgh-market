Content design applies the same discipline UX design applies to interfaces — understand the audience and their needs, explore options, refine through feedback — directly to the words and structure of the content itself, not only to the screen around it. A useful checklist for evaluating a piece of content against:

- **Useful** — content has a job to do; readers arrive to accomplish something, not to experience a brand. If a piece of content doesn't help a reader do or understand something specific, nothing else on this list matters.
- **Usable** — easy to access, understand, and act on: audience-appropriate terminology and reading level, consistent [formatting](formatting-for-scanning.md), and support for whatever device or format the reader is actually using it in.
- **Findable** — published in an open, crawlable form, carrying enough metadata to describe what it's about, sitting inside a well-designed structure, and phrased in the terms readers actually search for (see [labeling systems for clarity](labeling-systems-for-clarity.md)).
- **Focused** — stays on one topic, as short as it needs to be and no shorter, leads with the most important information, and is self-contained enough to make sense even if a reader arrives at it out of sequence.
- **Targeted** — built from research into the actual audience and domain rather than the author's own assumptions about what matters; content for a specialist and content for a newcomer are not the same content with the vocabulary swapped (see [the curse of knowledge and audience research](curse-of-knowledge-and-audience-research.md)).
- **Distinctive** — a piece of content is rarely the first thing written on its topic, and a reader scanning between several similar-looking sources ("pogo-sticking" between search results) stops at whichever one is actually distinguishable and useful, not at the one that merely covers the topic adequately.
- **Connected** — understanding comes from context as much as from content itself; a piece of content that links out to related material — including material outside the writer's own document set, when that's genuinely the best answer — serves the reader better than one that tries to be self-sufficient by omission.

A much older version of the same discipline: S. R. Ranganathan's five laws of library science (1931) — books are for use, not preservation; a book for every reader; a reader for every book; save the reader's time; and the library must grow. Restated for content generally: content exists to be used, not to exist; it should be discoverable by the people who need it; effort shouldn't go into content nobody reads; structure and cross-referencing should minimize the time a reader spends searching; and a content set is a living system that has to keep growing and evolving to stay relevant, not a fixed deliverable. See [strong opening technique](strong-opening-technique.md) and [motivating the reader with a problem](motivating-the-reader-with-a-problem.md) for techniques that specifically serve the "useful" and "targeted" properties at the level of an individual document's opening.
