title: Topic Position and Old-Before-New Information
description: >
  Readers process a sentence more easily when it opens with information
  they already have and saves new or difficult material for the end, and
  a passage reads as coherent when a consistent topic occupies that
  opening position sentence after sentence.
sources:

Two related but distinct judgments determine whether prose "hangs together." Readers judge a pair of sentences **cohesive** based on how the first ends and the second begins — a local, pairwise fit, like two jigsaw-puzzle pieces. They judge a whole passage **coherent** based on what all its sentences cumulatively add up to — like recognizing the picture on the puzzle box. Locally cohesive sentences do not guarantee a coherent passage: six writers can each add one sentence that connects smoothly to the one before it, and still produce a passage that is nonsensical as a whole, because the sentences share no consistent subject matter and the passage never states what it is collectively about.

The mechanism behind cohesion is the **old-before-new principle**: begin a sentence with information the reader already has — either because it was in the sentence just read, or because it is background knowledge the topic already implies — and end it with the new or complex material the reader could not have anticipated. This is one of the strongest reasons the passive voice exists in the language: a passive construction can put something familiar (echoing the end of the prior sentence) at its own beginning, and push new, complex content to the end, in a way the corresponding active sentence often can't. See [active voice and concrete language](active-voice-and-concrete-language.md).

**Topic** is a separate concept from grammatical subject, though readers expect them to coincide. The topic is what a sentence is psychologically "about" — the idea everything else in the sentence comments on — and it is not always the grammatical subject: in "In regard to this question, I believe more research is needed," the grammatical subject is "I," but the topic is "this question." A passage feels coherent when a small, consistent set of topics — ideally the passage's actual characters — recurs across its sentences, occupying the early, topic position sentence after sentence.

**Diagnosing a weak topic string**: underline the first seven or eight words of every sentence (and the first five or six of every clause) in a passage. If those underlined openers name a scattered set of unrelated abstractions rather than a small, consistent set of characters, the passage will read as choppy and disorganized regardless of how clear any individual sentence is. The fix is to identify the real characters, then consistently make them the subjects — and therefore the topics — of most sentences in the passage.

Two common misconceptions to guard against: writers often think varying their sentence-opening subjects avoids monotony, but readers rarely notice topic repetition — they track ideas, not word-position patterns — so the more common real failure is changing topics too often, not too rarely. Separately, some writers try to fake coherence by lacing prose with connectives (thus, therefore, however, moreover) whether or not those words track a genuine logical relationship; readers are not fooled by scaffolding that isn't load-bearing, so use a connective only when it names a relationship that is actually there.

The last few words of a sentence carry a complementary function — see [stress position and sentence emphasis](stress-position-and-emphasis.md) — and both operate at every scale from the sentence up through the whole document, which is developed further in [point sentences and document-level coherence](point-sentences-and-document-coherence.md).
