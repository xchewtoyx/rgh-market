A correction or a piece of critical feedback can be factually right and still fail, because a reader who feels accused stops evaluating the content and starts defending themselves. Four specific phrasing habits keep the same substantive disagreement from triggering that shutdown.

**Express uncertainty about the claim rather than certainty about the other person's error.** "I'm not sure that's right" or "have you considered it this way instead?" invites the other person to weigh in on a shared question; "you're wrong" turns the exchange into a verdict they now have to either accept or fight. The content of the objection can be identical — only the framing of who is being judged changes.

**Lead with genuine agreement, and connect it to the disagreement with "and," not "but."** Finding something specific and true to agree with before raising an objection signals that the feedback is aimed at the work, not at demolishing the whole thing — and "but" grammatically cancels whatever came before it, while "and" lets both things stand together. "This section is clear, but the example doesn't fit" reads as a takeback of the compliment; "this section is clear, and the example doesn't quite fit yet" reads as two things that are both true.

**Check whether critique was actually invited before giving it.** Someone recounting a frustrating experience is often looking for acknowledgment, not a diagnosis of what they could have done differently — offering unsolicited analysis to someone who wanted sympathy reads as dismissive regardless of how correct the analysis is. A quick check ("do you want my honest take, or do you just need to vent about this one?") costs one sentence and avoids delivering good feedback to someone who isn't in a position to use it yet.

**Focus on what to do next rather than re-litigating what already happened.** People get defensive discussing a past decision because it requires them to admit fault; the same people are often willing to discuss future action on the very same topic without that defensiveness, because it doesn't require conceding anything about the past. Acknowledging the situation and then redirecting to "is there anything that would help going forward?" gets to the same substance as a past-focused critique with much less resistance.

These are the same underlying discipline as [metadiscourse](metadiscourse.md) and [stress position and sentence emphasis](stress-position-and-emphasis.md) applied to disagreement specifically: the exact words surrounding a claim shape how a reader receives it, independent of whether the claim itself is correct.
