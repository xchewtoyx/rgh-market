title: "Incorporating Sources: Summarize, Paraphrase, or Quote"
description: >
  Choose deliberately between summarizing, paraphrasing, and quoting a
  source based on whether the reader needs its overall point, its
  information, or its exact words — and use each to advance your own
  argument rather than to replace it.
sources:
  - title: "The Craft of Research, Fifth Edition"
    resource: "The Craft of Research (Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup, FitzGerald), ch. 12"

Sources should be used to advance the writer's own argument, not to substitute for it — a document that strings together summaries or quotations of what other sources said, without ever stating what the writer themself is arguing, has [reported research rather than made a case](storyboarding-an-argument.md). Three distinct tools exist for bringing a source into a document, each suited to a different reason for including it:

- **Summarize** when the reader needs the source's overall point, not its specific wording or every supporting detail. A fair summary preserves the source's context, its own qualifications, and the actual line of reasoning it used to get to its conclusion — compressing a source's claim while quietly dropping the caveats attached to it misrepresents the source, even if every individual word used is accurate.
- **Paraphrase** when the source's specific information matters more than its exact wording. A paraphrase has to be a genuine restatement in the writer's own sentence structure and vocabulary, not just synonym-substitution over the source's original sentence shape — copying a source's structure while swapping a few words for synonyms is a recognized form of plagiarism (sometimes called patchwriting) even when the source is cited, because the citation credits the idea but the close imitation still borrows the source's actual expression without saying so.
- **Quote** when the exact language matters — because the source's authority rests specifically on having said it that way, because the wording itself is what's being analyzed, or because no paraphrase could convey the same precision. A quotation has to be accurate, grammatically integrated into the surrounding sentence, introduced so the reader knows who's speaking and why it's relevant, and followed by an explanation of its bearing on the argument — a quotation dropped in without any surrounding explanation asks the reader to do the interpretive work the writer should have done, and padding an argument with quotations to borrow a source's authority rather than to make a real point is a related, more subtle version of the same failure.

Across all three tools, the reader needs to always be able to tell where the source's view ends and the writer's own claim begins — an unmarked boundary between the two is a comprehension failure independent of whether the underlying citation is technically present. Every borrowed idea, wording, data point, or distinctive structure needs attribution as the relevant convention requires, both because it's the source's due and because it's what lets a reader trace a claim back to check it themselves. Whether the underlying claim is actually trustworthy is a separate question from whether it's represented clearly and honestly in the writer's own prose — this note covers the latter.
