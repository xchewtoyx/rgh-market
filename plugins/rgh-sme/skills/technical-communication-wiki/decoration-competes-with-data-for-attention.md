---
type: concept
title: Decoration Competes with Data for Attention
description: >
  Decorative visual elements that don't encode information — logos,
  background images, gradients, ornamental borders — cost a viewer
  processing effort they'd rather spend on the actual content, so
  blank space is a better default than filler.
sources:
  - title: "Information Dashboard Design"
    resource: "Information Dashboard Design (Stephen Few), ch. 3"
---

A visual display with empty space left over often gets filled with something decorative — a large logo, a background image, a color gradient, an ornamental border — on the assumption that bare space looks unfinished and viewers will find it more engaging. The opposite tends to happen: whatever visual interest decoration provides is a one-time effect that wears off within days of regular use, while the processing cost of visually filtering it out to get to the real content is paid every single time the viewer looks. A large decorative logo occupying the most prominent part of a page is a direct cost, not a neutral one, because that same space could have carried the content the viewer actually needs first.

The underlying rule is unqualified: **blank space is better than meaningless decoration.** This isn't an argument against visual appeal — a display can and should be designed to look good — but the appeal has to come from the data's own presentation being clean and well-organized, not from additions bolted on around it. Edward Tufte's diagnosis of this pattern is that treating the underlying subject matter as inherently boring, and trying to compensate with decoration, has the causality backwards: if a presentation of real information reads as boring, the fix is a better presentation of the information, not a distraction layered on top of it.

This connects directly to [the closure principle](gestalt-principles-for-visual-grouping.md): because a reader's perception fills in an implied shape from partial cues, many structural elements that feel necessary — a full box around a chart, a heavy divider between sections — can be simplified or removed without the reader losing the boundary they were meant to convey, freeing that visual weight for content that actually needs it. It is the visual-design counterpart of [cutting clutter](cutting-clutter.md) in prose: the same test applies — does this element do work for the reader, or is it just occupying space that could have been left alone or given to something useful?
