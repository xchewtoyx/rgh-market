title: Metadiscourse
description: >
  Metadiscourse is language that refers to the writer's own thinking,
  the reader, or the structure of the text rather than to the subject
  matter, and while some is necessary, excess buries the actual content.
sources:
  - title: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace"
    resource: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, 9th ed. (Joseph M. Williams, Joseph Bizup), Lessons 4, 7"
---

Metadiscourse names three kinds of language that comment on a piece of writing rather than stating its substance: the writer's own intentions and thinking ("we will show," "to sum up," "I believe," "candidly"), directions aimed at the reader ("note that," "consider now," "as you recall"), and connectives marking the text's own structure ("first," "therefore," "however," "finally"). Every piece of writing needs some — a claim with zero framing or connectives reads as an inert list — but it is also the most common source of avoidable wordiness, because it is invisible to the writer who is using it (announcing a topic feels like progress, even when it delays the actual claim).

Specific patterns are usually safe to cut:

- **Attributing your own claim to an act of observing it**, rather than just stating it: "high divorce rates have been observed to occur in areas with low population density" should simply assert the correlation.
- **Announcing a topic instead of stating a claim about it**: "The first thing to say about noise pollution is that it exists..." can almost always be folded directly into a sentence that just makes the claim. The extreme case — a whole sentence that only announces what will be discussed, with no claim in it ("In this essay, I will discuss the role of metaphor in style") — is often a sign the writer doesn't yet know what they want to say.
- **Hedges and intensifiers used past the point of honesty.** Hedges (perhaps, seems, could, may, suggests) understate a writer's certainty; intensifiers (clearly, obviously, certainly, totally) overstate it. Both are sometimes exactly right and sometimes redundant padding, and both are read by the audience as a direct signal of how much confidence the writer actually has in the claim — miscalibrating either one, in either direction, damages credibility. An unhedged flat statement is already the strongest form of intensification available; stacking more intensifiers on top of it usually just sounds defensive.

One useful, non-obvious exception: verbs that name the writer's own metadiscursive acts — cite, show, argue, claim, suggest — are the one place first-person active voice is normal even in fields that otherwise avoid it, because they describe the writer's reasoning rather than a research procedure. See [active voice and concrete language](active-voice-and-concrete-language.md) for the related choice between active and passive more generally, and [cutting clutter](cutting-clutter.md) for the broader editing pass this belongs to.

A related, forgivable case: writers new to a field or genre often produce a different kind of redundancy — narrating their own thinking process, or restating what their audience already takes as common ground — simply because they haven't yet learned what that audience leaves unsaid. This is a normal stage of learning a professional register, not a concision failure to drill out immediately; the fix, once the writer knows the community's baseline, is the same deletion technique used elsewhere.
