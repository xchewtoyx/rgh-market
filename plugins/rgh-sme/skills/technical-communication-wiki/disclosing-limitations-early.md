---
type: concept
title: Disclosing Limitations Early, Not Last
description: >
  State a work's constraints and caveats early and move on to the
  contribution, rather than ending on them — the reader's last
  impression should be the finding, not its limits.
sources:
  - title: "Writing Science: How to Write Papers That Get Cited and Proposals That Get Funded"
    resource: "Writing Science (Joshua Schimel), ch. 18"
---

Every piece of work has limits, and naming them honestly is a matter of the writer's credibility — but where those limits sit in a document changes what a reader remembers from it. A "yes, but" ending — stating the contribution and then closing on its caveats — puts the caveats in the document's final power position, and readers tend to carry away whatever occupies that spot: the piece reads as apologetic even when the underlying contribution is solid. A "but, yes" structure inverts the order: disclose the constraints early, state honestly what the work does and doesn't support, and then close on the contribution itself, so the last thing a reader carries away is what was actually accomplished, not a list of what wasn't.

This has a companion discipline further upstream: promise only the story the methods actually deliver. If a method can't answer the grand question a piece originally set out to ask, the right fix is to narrow the [challenge](the-funnel-technique.md) itself to the smaller, defensible thing the method genuinely supports — a small, real, filled gap in understanding is worth more than an unsupported large claim, and it also removes the need for a defensive caveat-heavy ending later.

Methodological limitations belong where a method is introduced, explained as part of why the approach is appropriate and how the design limits the risk they pose to the conclusion — not deferred to a single limitations section at the very end. In a proposal, this matters even more: reviewers will find the weaknesses regardless, so showing early that the writer has already thought them through is itself part of the case for funding. Broader interpretation-limiting constraints belong in the body of the discussion, not in its opening (which should reorient the reader to the main finding) or its resolution (which should land the contribution). Only unusually novel or artificial methods justify a dedicated up-front "experimental considerations" section, and even then it should pair the boundaries candidly with what the design uniquely makes possible, rather than reading as a pre-emptive apology.

See [separating exceptions from the main line](separating-exceptions-from-the-main-line.md) for the structural version of this same instinct applied more broadly: pulling minor exceptions out to their own clearly marked spot so the main explanation isn't interrupted by them.
