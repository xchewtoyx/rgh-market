---
type: concept
title: Sentence Order for Reader Visualization
description: >
  Because a reader processes words one at a time through a narrow
  channel, a sentence should be composed by first forming a clear mental
  image of what it describes and then setting the words down in the
  order that lets the reader reconstruct that same image without having
  to revise it partway through.
sources:
  - title: "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving"
    resource: "The Minto Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto), ch. 12"
---

A [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md) supplies a document's large-scale organization, but that structure still has to survive being expressed one sentence at a time, and reading passes words through a narrow serial channel — a reader can't see a sentence's whole shape at once the way a writer, who already knows what it means, can. Treating sentence order as an obligation to the reader rather than a matter of style follows directly from that constraint.

The recommended composition process is to build the image before choosing the words: state a subject and predicate the reader can actually picture — an actor doing something, an object in some state, a process unfolding, a relationship between two things — before worrying about phrasing. Abstract, unvisualizable wording is a warning sign at the drafting stage, not just at the editing stage, because if the writer can't picture what a sentence describes, there's no way to check whether the sentence actually says what was meant. This is the same underlying discipline as [characters as subjects, actions as verbs](characters-as-subjects.md) approached from the writer's compositional process rather than from a revision checklist.

Once the image is clear, copy it into words in the order the eye or mind would naturally encounter it — concrete nouns, active verbs, specific end states — and arrange modifiers and subordinate clauses so the reader never has to revise a mental picture they've already formed partway through the sentence. A sentence that establishes one image and then retroactively corrects it (a modifier arriving too late, a clause that reframes the subject after the fact) forces the reader to redo work they'd already completed; see [sentence shape for long sentences](sentence-shape-for-long-sentences.md) for the mechanics of avoiding that in longer sentences specifically. None of this is in service of literary flourish — the goal is a sequence that lets the reader reconstruct genuinely complex material in memorable, manageable pieces, the sentence-level expression of the same discipline the pyramid applies at the document level.
