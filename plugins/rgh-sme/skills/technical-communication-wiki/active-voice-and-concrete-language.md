Active verbs that identify who is doing what carry more information and read faster than passive constructions, which hide the actor and let sentences drift toward abstraction. The passive voice is not forbidden — it is the right choice when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or when the sentence needs to foreground the thing acted upon — but it should be a deliberate choice, not the writer's resting state.

"Active" and "passive" are ambiguous, because they describe both a grammatical construction and how a sentence impressionistically feels. A sentence can be grammatically active yet still feel passive and flat — "Problem management requires cost control" has no passive verb, but it still fails to read as direct, because neither action is a verb (both are nominalizations), the subject is an abstraction, and there are no flesh-and-blood characters in it at all. This is a distinct, more fundamental failure than voice choice — see [characters as subjects](characters-as-subjects.md) for the underlying principle.

Given the choice between a genuinely active and a genuinely passive construction, use three diagnostic questions rather than a blanket rule:

1. **Must readers know who is responsible for the action?** Often the answer is no — nobody needs to be told who specifically "keeps records safe" in a routine institutional sentence — and the passive is the natural, honest choice there. (Contrast the illegitimate case, where a writer chooses the passive specifically to hide who is responsible for something readers would want to know — see [the ethics of clear writing](ethics-of-clear-writing.md).)
2. **Would active or passive help readers move more smoothly from one sentence to the next?** Sentences read best when they open with old, familiar information and save new, complex information for the end (see [topic position and old-before-new information](topic-position-and-old-before-new-information.md)). If the active subject would front new, complex material, the passive can instead open with something familiar from the prior sentence and push the complex material to the end.
3. **Would active or passive give readers a more consistent point of view?** A passage that keeps switching which character is the subject, sentence to sentence, disorients readers regardless of individual sentence quality — pick a point of view and stick to it, using the passive as needed to keep the same character as the subject across a run of sentences.

The same discipline applies below the sentence level: prefer concrete nouns to abstract ones, use qualifiers ("very," "rather," "quite") sparingly since they usually weaken rather than strengthen a claim, and let adjectives demonstrate a quality through detail rather than merely announcing it ("a productive meeting" tells the reader nothing that a supporting detail wouldn't show better). None of this should harden into a rigid mannerism — an occasional passive or qualifier is fine when it is chosen for a reason.

Paragraph length should follow the shape of the thought rather than a fixed rule: a one-sentence paragraph can deliver punch, while a paragraph carrying a complex idea needs room to move through it. This is one of the small, cumulative decisions — alongside [cutting clutter](cutting-clutter.md) — that add up to clear prose; there is no single grand technique that substitutes for making each of them well.
