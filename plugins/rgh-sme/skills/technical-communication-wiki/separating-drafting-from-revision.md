---
type: concept
title: Separating Drafting from Revision
description: >
  Write a rough first draft to get material onto the page, then treat
  shaping, cutting, and refining it as a distinct later pass, rather than
  trying to produce a finished sentence on the first attempt.
sources:
  - title: On Writing Well
    resource: "On Writing Well, 30th Anniversary Edition (William Zinsser), ch. 22"
---

Writers commonly stall by imagining the finished document — its final polish, its reception, its judgment — before a single sentence exists. That imagined final product is a form of tyranny: it makes starting feel like a referendum on the writer's ability rather than the first of several ordinary steps. The practical fix is to treat drafting and revision as separate activities with different goals: a first draft's job is only to get material onto the page, however roughly; a later pass's job is to shape, cut, and refine what is now in front of the writer instead of only in their head.

This is a permission structure, not indulgence: clear sentences almost never arrive right on the first attempt, so demanding first-draft polish guarantees stalling. Concentrating on the next paragraph or the next problem, rather than the whole finished piece, keeps the work moving. Revision is where techniques like [cutting clutter](cutting-clutter.md) and [reading prose aloud to revise](reading-aloud-to-revise.md) are applied — they are revision-phase tools, not things a writer should expect to get right while still drafting.
