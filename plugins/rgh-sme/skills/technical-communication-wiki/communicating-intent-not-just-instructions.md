title: Communicating Intent, Not Just Instructions
description: >
  An instruction that states only the literal steps forces its reader to
  guess at everything it left unstated — stating the purpose behind a
  request, not only the request itself, is what lets a reader fill gaps
  correctly and adapt when the literal instruction stops fitting.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power (Gary Klein), ch. 13"
---

No instruction can specify every detail its reader will need — the writer cannot anticipate every condition the reader will actually encounter, and trying to spell out every contingency in advance buries the genuinely important points in noise. The gap always has to be filled by the reader's own judgment, which means the real question is not "did I specify enough" but "did I give the reader enough to correctly guess the parts I didn't specify." A reader who only receives the literal steps has to guess at the reasoning behind them from scratch, and a plausible-sounding guess is often wrong: a request that names a step but never says why routinely gets satisfied to the letter while completely missing the point, because the reader filled the gap with their own reasonable-seeming assumption instead of the writer's actual one.

Naming the **purpose** behind a request — not just what to do, but why it matters — is the single highest-leverage piece of information for closing this gap, and it is also the piece most routinely left out, because it feels obvious to the person who already has it in mind. A request for "a mouse compatible with this specific old computer" fails just as easily as a request that names only an old purchase order to reference, if the underlying need — cheap, and specifically compatible with this machine — is never stated; a reader handed only the literal reference point has no way to improvise correctly once that literal thing no longer exists. Sharing rationale has a second payoff beyond preventing misreads: a reader who understands *why* a plan is shaped the way it is can propose a better plan, or flag one of the plan's own weak points, in a way a reader following bare steps never could.

A fuller checklist for what to include when handing someone a task, useful precisely because most real instructions default to over-specifying only one of these while omitting the rest:

- **Purpose** — the higher-level goal the task serves, and why it matters.
- **Objective** — a concrete image of what success actually looks like, not just the topic of the task.
- **The plan** — the steps, kept as loose as the reader's own competence allows; this is the part writers reflexively over-specify at the expense of everything else on this list, which is exactly backward, since the steps are the part a competent reader is best equipped to work out themselves once they understand the rest.
- **Rationale for the plan** — why this approach, not some other one.
- **Key decisions or contingencies to watch for** — points where the plan is most likely to need adjusting, including known weaknesses in the plan itself.
- **Antigoals** — outcomes to explicitly avoid, stated only where there's a real risk the reader might otherwise drift toward a plausible-looking but unwanted result.
- **Constraints and other things to watch for** — anything else the reader needs but wouldn't think to ask about.

How much of this to spell out is a genuine judgment call, not a fixed template to fill exhaustively every time: a more experienced reader needs less of the plan and more of the purpose; a less experienced one needs the reverse; a fast-changing situation makes committing to antigoals or fixed contingencies premature; and a genuinely fuzzy goal needs more attention paid to the decision points, since the image of success may itself keep shifting as the reader works. The craft is compression, not completeness — state as little as will actually let the reader fill the rest in correctly, since padding with everything on this list every time defeats the purpose as thoroughly as omitting purpose does.
