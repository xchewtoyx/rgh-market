---
type: concept
title: The Problem-Definition Framework
description: >
  Before writing about a problem, define it with a fixed structure —
  the existing situation, what disturbed it, the undesired and desired
  results, and the gap between them — which both clarifies the reader's
  real question and supplies the introduction's raw material directly.
sources:
  - title: "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving"
    resource: "The Minto Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto), ch. 8"
---

A problem, defined precisely before any analysis or writing starts, has a fixed shape: an **Opening Scene** (the existing structure or process, visualized simply), a **Disturbing Event** (external, internally initiated, or newly recognized — sometimes unknown, in which case the framework starts directly from the undesired result instead), an **R1** (the current, undesired result), an **R2** (the desired result, stated as specifically and quantitatively as possible so candidate solutions can actually be judged against it), and the question created by the gap between R1 and R2. This is a scaffold for locating exactly what's missing in the writer's own understanding of a situation, and it converts directly into the raw material for [the situation-complication-question introduction](situation-complication-question-introduction.md): moving through what's known in order, the last known fact becomes the Complication, and everything before it becomes the Situation.

The reader's actual question depends on which stage the problem is at, and getting this wrong produces a document that answers a question nobody was asking: how to get from R1 to R2; whether a specific proposed solution is the right one; how to implement an already-agreed solution; what to do after a previously tried solution failed; which of several known alternatives to choose; what strategy or objective to pursue when R2 itself isn't yet clear; or, in a benchmarking-style document, whether R1 even exists as a real problem at all. Each of these implies a different key-line form — changes, steps, reasons, a strategy, or a choice among named options — so identifying the stage correctly is what determines the shape of everything that follows in the document.

A clear R1/R2 with explicit constraints does real structural work: it can convert a long, meandering history and a pile of loosely related options into one answerable question and a cleanly grouped set of supporting reasons, rather than leaving the reader to extract the actual decision point from a narrative dump.
