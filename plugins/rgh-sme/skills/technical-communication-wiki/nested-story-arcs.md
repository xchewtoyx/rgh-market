---
type: concept
title: Nested Story Arcs
description: >
  Every level of a document — the whole piece, its sections, subsections,
  paragraphs, and sentences — should form one complete, resolved arc
  rather than leaving readers carrying an unresolved thought into the
  next unit.
sources:
  - title: "Writing Science: How to Write Papers That Get Cited and Proposals That Get Funded"
    resource: "Writing Science (Joshua Schimel), ch. 10"
---

A document is a story made of smaller stories: the whole piece, each section, each subsection, and each paragraph should make one complete, linked point with an opening, a development, and — where the unit calls for it — a resolution, the same way [OCAR story structure](ocar-story-structure.md) works at the whole-document scale. Beginnings and endings of any unit are power positions precisely because a reader notices them; complete, self-contained arcs let a reader compartmentalize each idea and see what's being emphasized, while broken or interleaved arcs force the reader to carry an unresolved idea forward into material about something else, producing a "spaghetti" argument where nothing feels finished.

Subheads, paragraph breaks, and explicit signal words (however, consequently, we conclude) mark where one arc ends and the next begins — they are not decoration, they are the reader's map of the document's structure. A single section can legitimately contain several smaller, linked local arcs, each with its own mini opening-development-resolution, as long as each one actually resolves before the next begins.

To diagnose arcless, stream-of-consciousness writing, ask of each unit: does it make one clear point; are its links to the surrounding units explicit; has its topic been resolved before a new one starts; and is every major unit visibly introduced rather than just starting mid-thought? A thought that fails this check should be split into its own unit, relocated next to the arc it actually belongs to, or cut if it's a genuine digression.
