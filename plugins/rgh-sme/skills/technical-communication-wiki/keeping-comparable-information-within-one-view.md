---
type: concept
title: Keeping Comparable Information Within One View
description: >
  Information a reader needs to compare or relate has to be visible at
  the same time, because working memory can't reliably hold what has
  scrolled or paged out of view while the reader looks at something else.
sources:
  - title: "Information Dashboard Design"
    resource: "Information Dashboard Design (Stephen Few), ch. 3-4"
---

Something happens perceptually when two pieces of information are seen together, within the same eye span, that doesn't happen when a reader has to hold one of them in memory while looking at the other: simultaneous comparison produces recognitions ("that number is way higher than usual") that sequential viewing doesn't reliably produce. The reason is a hard limit on working (short-term) memory — it holds only roughly three to nine chunks of information at a time, so anything that has scrolled off-screen, moved to another tab, or been paged past is at risk of being displaced before the reader can use it in a comparison.

The practical failure mode is fragmenting content that should be considered together: splitting a set of related figures across separate pages or screens, or burying part of a comparison below a scroll boundary while the rest stays visible above it. A reader facing this either does the comparison from memory (unreliably) or pays the cost of paging back and forth, losing whatever they were just looking at each time they do. Fragmentation isn't wrong in general — detail that's genuinely a drill-down from a summary, or a separate concern a reader wouldn't need at the same time, belongs on its own page. The failure is specifically splitting apart information whose entire value comes from being seen side by side.

This is the structural reason a "chunk" of visual information can carry more than a "chunk" of text: a well-designed graphical pattern — the shape of a line in a trend chart, the relative heights of a set of bars — can be perceived and held as a single chunk, where the same information as a table of individual numbers would consume many chunks of the same limited capacity. See [formatting for scanning](formatting-for-scanning.md) for the related but distinct concern of how a reader scans a single page, and [communicating evidence visually](communicating-evidence-visually.md) for choosing the graphic form that turns a comparison into one visual chunk rather than several separate numbers.
