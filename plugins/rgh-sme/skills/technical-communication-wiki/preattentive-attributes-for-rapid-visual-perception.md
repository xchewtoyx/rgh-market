title: Preattentive Attributes for Rapid Visual Perception
description: >
  A small set of visual properties — position, length, color hue and
  intensity, shape, orientation, size — are recognized by the brain in
  a fraction of a second, before conscious attention engages, and only
  some of them can carry an accurate sense of "more than / less than."
sources:
  - title: "Information Dashboard Design"

Vision happens in two stages with very different speeds. **Preattentive processing** recognizes certain visual attributes almost instantly and below conscious awareness — a red item in a field of blue ones "pops out" without the viewer searching for it. **Attentive processing** is what's left: a slow, conscious, sequential search, the kind needed to count how many "5" digits appear in a grid of numbers distinguished only by their glyph shapes rather than by color. Choosing a visual encoding that rides on preattentive processing, instead of forcing attentive search, is what makes a well-designed chart or table readable at a glance rather than something the reader has to work through.

Only about a dozen attributes trigger preattentive processing, and they fall into a few families: **color** (hue; intensity, meaning saturation and lightness combined), **position** (2-D location), **form** (orientation, line length, line width, size, shape, added marks such as a circle or asterisk, enclosure by a border or fill), and **motion** (flicker). Two practical limits apply to all of them: short-term memory can hold the *meaning* of only around five distinct expressions of most attributes at once (five gray shades, five shapes) before they stop being reliably distinguishable, and hue is a partial exception at up to about nine distinguishable meanings — beyond either ceiling, the reader can no longer keep straight what each variant is supposed to represent, and adding more variety only makes a display look cluttered. Position and line length are the two exceptions with no such ceiling, which is part of why they carry the heaviest load in ordinary charts (point position on a scale, bar length).

Not every preattentive attribute can support quantitative comparison. **2-D position and line length reliably convey "greater than / less than"** and are the right choice for encoding a magnitude a reader needs to judge accurately. **Intensity, line width, size, and flicker convey magnitude only weakly** — usable for a rough sense of more/less but not for a comparison the reader needs to get right. **Hue, orientation, shape, added marks, and enclosure convey no ordering at all** — they can only tell a reader "this is different from that," which makes them well suited to marking categories, linking related items, or highlighting, but the wrong choice whenever the actual question is "how much more." This is the perceptual mechanism behind concrete chart failures such as [misjudging area and angle in pie and bubble charts](communicating-evidence-visually.md): humans are demonstrably poor at judging 2-D area and angle accurately (a circle with 16 times the true area of another is typically guessed at a far smaller ratio), so any encoding that asks a reader to compare areas or angles is choosing one of the weakest possible channels for exactly the judgment the chart is supposed to make easy.

The color half of this — hue and intensity specifically — carries an added complication: color is never perceived in isolation. The same gray square looks a different intensity depending on the lightness of what surrounds it, and identical text is more or less legible depending on the background color behind it. A color scheme has to be chosen and checked in its actual surrounding context, not swatch by swatch in isolation, or two values meant to look the same (or different) will do the opposite of what was intended once placed on the real page. See [gestalt principles for visual grouping](gestalt-principles-for-visual-grouping.md) for how these same low-level attributes combine into higher-level grouping judgments, and [consistent visual encoding across a document](consistent-visual-encoding-across-a-document.md) for the practical discipline of picking a fixed, reused set of these attributes instead of choosing ad hoc each time.
