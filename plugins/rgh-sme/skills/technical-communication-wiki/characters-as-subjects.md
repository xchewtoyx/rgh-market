---
type: concept
title: Characters as Subjects, Actions as Verbs
description: >
  Readers judge a sentence clear when its grammatical subject names the
  real character responsible and its verb names the real action, rather
  than burying both inside abstract nouns.
sources:
  - title: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace"
    resource: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, 9th ed. (Joseph M. Williams, Joseph Bizup), Lessons 3-4"
---

Two principles predict, mechanically, whether a sentence will feel dense or clear: readers want the main characters as the grammatical subjects of sentences, and they want the important actions expressed as verbs. Compare "The Federalists' argument in regard to the destabilization of government by popular democracy was based on their belief..." (dense: the subject is the abstraction "argument," and the real actors — Federalists, popular democracy — sit buried in prepositional phrases and possessives) with "The Federalists argued that popular democracy destabilized government, because they believed..." (clear: the actions are verbs, and the characters are their subjects). When the two principles pull apart, character-as-subject matters more than action-as-verb: a sentence with a concrete subject but a weakened, nominalized verb reads more clearly than one with a strong verb attached to an abstract subject.

Characters are not always missing outright — they often hide in predictable places: possessive pronouns or adjectives attached to a nominalization ("governmental intervention" hides "the government"), objects of prepositions (especially "by" and "of"), or they may be implied and absent from the sentence entirely. A quick diagnostic: skim the first seven or eight words of a sentence. If no concrete character appears there as the subject of a verb naming a specific action, the sentence is a candidate for revision — recover the hidden character, make it the subject, and rebuild the sentence around a verb naming what it does. See [diagnosing nominalizations](diagnosing-nominalizations.md) for the mechanical version of this procedure.

Abstractions can serve as characters too, so long as they are consistently the subject of a sequence of sentences that tells a coherent story about them, and readers are already familiar enough with the concept to track it as a "virtual character" (freedom of speech, taxation, inflation). The risk is piling several unfamiliar abstractions around one another — a passage built from one abstract character is tolerable; a passage where every sentence introduces a new abstraction becomes unreadable regardless of grammar.

This is not a mandate for maximal simplicity: some ideas are complex enough that expressing them plainly, in short primer-style sentences, makes the writing sound simpleminded rather than clear. The fix for an idea that is genuinely complex is not to keep chopping sentences shorter, but to consolidate around a consistent character as subject while using subordination to carry the complexity — see [sentence shape for long sentences](sentence-shape-for-long-sentences.md). The underlying test, per the field's own professional-voice norm, is the writer's golden rule: write to others as you would have others write to you — make ideas no simpler than they deserve, but no more complex than they have to be.
