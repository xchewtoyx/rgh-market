---
type: concept
title: Asking "How," Not "Why," for Non-Defensive Recall
description: >
  When reconstructing what happened from the people involved, "why"
  questions tend to produce a justification instead of a description —
  "how," "what," and "when" get a participant recalling events rather
  than defending a decision, and yield material that's actually usable.
sources:
  - title: "Incident Management for Operations"
    resource: "Incident Management for Operations (Schnepp, Vidal, Hawley), ch. 6"
---

Interviewing participants after the fact to reconstruct what happened — for a retrospective, a case study, a written account of a decision — runs into a framing problem before it ever reaches the facts: "why did you do that?" reads as a demand to justify a choice, not a request to describe one, and people respond to a justification demand by explaining and defending rather than recalling. The resulting material is skewed toward whatever makes the decision look reasonable in hindsight, which is exactly the kind of narrative flattening that loses the texture the actual account needs.

Questions built around "how," "what," and "when" don't carry the same accusatory weight. "What did you see at that point?", "how did you decide what to do next?", "when did you first notice something was off?" ask for a description of a sequence, not a defense of a choice — and a participant answering a descriptive question tends to produce far more usable, concrete detail than one answering a justification demand. The distinction is in the grammar, not the topic: the same underlying event can be probed either way, and only one framing reliably gets the texture of what actually happened rather than a retrospectively tidied account of why it was fine.

This matters most where an account will be assembled from more than one participant's telling, since there's rarely a single, complete, objective version of a fast-moving event — each person only ever had their own local view of it. Treating the interview as a way to collect and triangulate several honest, descriptive perspectives, rather than a search for the one authoritative explanation, produces a much more accurate composite account than any single "why" answer would. This is the retrospective-interview counterpart to [subject-matter expert interview technique](subject-matter-expert-interview-technique.md)'s practice of treating conflicting expert explanations as a genuine finding rather than noise to average away — here applied to reconstructing a sequence of events instead of mapping a domain's concepts.
