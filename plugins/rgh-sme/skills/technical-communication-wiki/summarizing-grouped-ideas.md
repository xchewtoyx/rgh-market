A point placed above a [grouping](deductive-and-inductive-grouping.md) in a [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md) has to be derived from, and actually summarize, the ideas grouped beneath it. "There are three reasons," "there are three problems," "there are three recommendations" is intellectually empty: it names a category the items happen to fall into rather than stating what those items, taken together, actually mean — it bores the reader and, worse, conceals that the writer hasn't finished doing the thinking the summary was supposed to represent.

What a proper summary says depends on the kind of grouping beneath it. For a **deductive** group, summarize the conclusion the chain of reasoning reaches. For an **inductive action group**, state the direct effect of carrying out the actions as a set — not that actions exist, but what happens if they're all done. For an **inductive situation group**, state the implication of the shared similarity the items have in common. A properly written summary should be strong enough to naturally stimulate the next deductive comment or the next inductive comparison the document needs to make — a weak summary stalls the argument even if every item beneath it is individually correct.

Action ideas carry a particular risk and need especially rigorous wording: phrase each one so a real person can be visualized actually doing it, with a tangible, checkable condition for having completed it. Vague verbs like "strengthen," "improve," or "tackle" make it impossible to verify later whether the listed steps were actually sufficient to achieve the stated effect, because there's no concrete end-state to check against. Build action hierarchies from small, MECE cause-and-effect groups — five steps or fewer is a reasonable ceiling — and keep clear which actions are taken *before* another action versus which actions are taken *in order to produce* another; conflating those two relationships, or classifying actions under labels like Tasks/Objectives/Benefits that slice the hierarchy horizontally instead of vertically, repeats content and obscures what the actual end-product sequence is.

To summarize a situation grouping, look for structural similarity across the items' subjects, predicates, or implied judgments, then look for a closer common link before making the inductive leap to a stated implication — and test the resulting inference two ways: that it isn't broader than the evidence in the group actually supports, and that it isn't merely a restatement of the category the items were already grouped under.
