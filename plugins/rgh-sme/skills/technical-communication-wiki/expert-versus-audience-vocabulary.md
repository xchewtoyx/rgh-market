title: Expert Versus Audience Vocabulary
description: >
  A domain expert's own terminology and level of detail often diverge
  sharply from the audience's, so writing that borrows expert vocabulary
  wholesale can be precise and still fail the reader it's for.
sources:
  - title: "Designing Connected Content: Plan and Model Digital Products for Today and Tomorrow"
    resource: "Designing Connected Content (Mike Atherton, Carrie Hane), ch. 4"
---

An expert's own working vocabulary regularly diverges from the vocabulary a general audience would use for the same thing — a clinician's "xerostomia" is a reader's "dry mouth," a magician's "effect" is an audience member's "trick." These aren't cosmetic differences: a document that adopts the expert's own terms wholesale, on the assumption that precision requires it, can leave the actual audience unable to find or recognize the content at all, however accurate it is. What looks like one concept to a layperson can also unpack into several distinct concepts under expert scrutiny — a domain's real structure and its audience's naive mental model of that structure are not the same thing, and a document written for the audience needs to reflect the second even where the first is more technically correct. See [labeling systems for clarity](labeling-systems-for-clarity.md) for the related discipline of choosing terms readers will recognize over terms a system or its experts happen to use internally.

A second, less obvious gap sits alongside vocabulary: experts and audiences typically care about different levels of detail within the same subject. A community of enthusiasts may care intensely about distinctions a casual member of the audience never notices at all, while the casual audience cares about a much coarser-grained distinction the expert takes entirely for granted and rarely thinks to explain. One useful framing: experts map the whole territory in detail; audiences only mark the specific points of interest to them. A document aimed at a general audience needs the expert's accuracy but the audience's own sense of what's actually worth including — cross-referencing both sources, rather than defaulting to whichever one was easier to interview, is what keeps a document both correct and actually usable by the people it's for.

For a genuinely unwieldy, jargon-heavy, or multi-stakeholder domain where no single expert has a shared vocabulary with the others, a group technique — sometimes called a "vocabulary dump" — can surface the same gap at group scale: have participants write down every term they associate with the subject on individual note cards, then cluster the resulting pile into related groups. This makes the sheer size of a domain's vocabulary visible to everyone at once, surfaces which terms need a shared definition before the group can even discuss the subject coherently, and often reveals that different stakeholders have been using the same word to mean different things — a disagreement worth catching before it gets written into a document as if it were settled.
