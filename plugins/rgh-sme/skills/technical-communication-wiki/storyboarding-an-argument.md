---
type: concept
title: Storyboarding an Argument
description: >
  Plan a document that makes a case — not just presents information —
  with a flexible storyboard of its claim, reasons, evidence, warrants,
  and anticipated objections, rearranged as the underlying research
  changes rather than committed to prose too early.
sources:
  - title: "The Craft of Research, Fifth Edition"
    resource: "The Craft of Research (Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup, FitzGerald), ch. 7, 10"
---

A document built around an argument — this is the right approach, here's the evidence, here's why it matters — benefits from a planning stage distinct from both research and drafting: laying the argument's pieces out as separable, movable units before committing to prose order. A useful form for this is a card or index-card-style storyboard: one card per claim, reason, piece of evidence still needed, warrant, likely alternative, and anticipated objection. Because the underlying inquiry is still evolving while this planning happens, the storyboard needs to stay genuinely rearrangeable — as evidence turns out weaker than expected, or a new reason surfaces, or an objection turns out to matter more than assumed, the cards move rather than the prose being patched around a structure that no longer fits the argument.

Drafting from this storyboard means composing sections around claims, not around a source-by-source summary of what was read — a common failure mode is a draft organized as "here's what source A said, here's what source B said," which reports research rather than making an argument from it. Instead, each section should exist because it advances a specific claim in the storyboard, with its supporting reasons and evidence assembled underneath it, regardless of which sources those came from.

Draft early despite incomplete certainty about the argument's final shape — the act of writing a section exposes missing evidence and weak reasoning faster than continued planning does, the same discipline as [separating drafting from revision](separating-drafting-from-revision.md) applied specifically to argumentative structure rather than prose polish. Treat the plan itself as a working hypothesis, not a locked structure: it should be revised as research and early drafting reveal that the argument doesn't actually hold together the way the storyboard first assumed. See [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md) for a comparable planning discipline applied to business and reference documents, and [deductive and inductive grouping](deductive-and-inductive-grouping.md) for the underlying logical relationships a storyboard's reasons need to have with each other.
