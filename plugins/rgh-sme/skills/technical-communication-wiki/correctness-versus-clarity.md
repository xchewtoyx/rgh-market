---
type: concept
title: Correctness Versus Clarity
description: >
  Grammatical correctness and communicative clarity are separate axes, and
  effort spent policing minor or invented usage rules is effort not spent
  on the sentence-level revision that actually helps readers.
sources:
  - title: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace"
    resource: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, 9th ed. (Joseph M. Williams, Joseph Bizup), Lesson 2"
---

A sentence can be grammatically flawless and still unreadable: "Too precise a specification of information processing requirements incurs the risk of overestimation resulting in unused capacity or inefficient use of costly resources..." violates no rule of grammar at all, yet no reader would call it clear. Correctness and clarity are different properties, diagnosed and fixed by different means — see [characters as subjects](characters-as-subjects.md) and [diagnosing nominalizations](diagnosing-nominalizations.md) for the actual clarity work — and an editor who spends their attention on correctness alone is solving the wrong problem.

Usage rules come in three kinds, and only one of them is worth an editor's real attention. **Real rules** define the language itself (articles precede nouns) and are never actually violated by a competent writer except by accident. **Social rules** distinguish standard from nonstandard usage and are worth observing in most professional writing, not because nonstandard forms are less logical — many are more regular than the standard form — but because departing from the expected convention costs a writer credibility with readers who notice. **Invented rules** are the ones worth being skeptical of: a small set of usage prescriptions, mostly dating from the 18th century (don't split an infinitive, don't end a sentence with a preposition, use "that" not "which" for restrictive clauses), that even the most careful, respected writers violate constantly and unremarkably. The test for whether an invented rule has any real force: if writers judged competent by their peers violate it regularly and careful readers never notice, the rule doesn't bind — it's the rule that needs revising, not the writer's usage.

This produces a practical split for editing practice: some invented rules are pure folklore, safely ignored; others function as "elegant options" — optional, and their violation goes unnoticed, but observing them signals a self-consciously formal register (avoiding a split infinitive, using "whom" correctly) when that register is wanted. A handful of genuine word-choice distinctions are worth learning regardless (disinterested/uninterested, comprise/constitute, aggravate/annoy) because careful readers do notice them, and getting them right earns no credit but getting them wrong costs some.

The practical implication for a reviewer or self-editor: don't spend a revision pass hunting for split infinitives or sentence-final prepositions at the expense of checking whether the sentence's actual subject names a real character and its actual verb names a real action. Correctness housekeeping is worth doing, but it should never substitute for the harder, more consequential work of revising for clarity.
