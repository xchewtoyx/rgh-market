---
type: concept
title: Writing for Non-Native and Global Readers
description: >
  Grammatical correctness does not guarantee comprehension for readers
  who don't share the writer's linguistic and cultural background, so
  writing for a global audience needs deliberately overt structure and
  plain vocabulary.
sources:
  - title: "Writing Science: How to Write Papers That Get Cited and Proposals That Get Funded"
    resource: "Writing Science (Joshua Schimel), ch. 19"
---

English functions as a working international language in many technical fields, but a shared working language does not mean shared linguistic or cultural schemas — a sentence that is grammatically correct can still fail to land as intended for a reader whose native rhetorical conventions differ from the writer's. Writing for this audience takes deliberate effort beyond ordinary correctness: prefer simple sentence structures, direct verbs, and vocabulary that is readily understood rather than merely technically valid; make logical links between ideas explicit rather than implied, since implied connections rely on shared cultural inference a global reader may not have; and avoid idioms, slang, culture-specific allusions, puns, and casual metaphor, none of which travel cleanly across languages and backgrounds — see [using metaphor to explain](using-metaphor-to-explain.md) for the related risk that even well-intentioned metaphor can mislead if its logic doesn't hold.

A writer's or reviewer's own bias is worth naming directly here: non-native English is not evidence of lesser scientific or technical ability, and international collaborators or reviewers may simply be working from different, equally legitimate rhetorical conventions about how an argument is normally organized. The fix is not to assume unfamiliarity means confusion, but to give a piece's logic enough overt, explicit structure — clear [point sentences](point-sentences-and-document-coherence.md), explicit transitions, unambiguous topic strings — that differences in background convention don't obscure the underlying argument.

When editing a non-native speaker's work, the goal is reader accessibility, precision, and standard professional register — not stylistic assimilation toward the editor's own voice for its own sake. Preserve the author's actual meaning and their voice where it doesn't interfere with comprehension; focus revision effort on the places where a genuine ambiguity or missing logical link would cost a reader understanding, not on making the prose sound like it was written by someone else.
