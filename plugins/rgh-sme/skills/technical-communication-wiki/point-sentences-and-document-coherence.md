title: Point Sentences and Document-Level Coherence
description: >
  The clarity principle that a short, graspable segment should introduce
  a longer, more complex one applies recursively from sentences up
  through paragraphs, sections, and whole documents.
sources:
  - title: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace"
    resource: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, 9th ed. (Joseph M. Williams, Joseph Bizup), Lesson 11"
---

Locally cohesive sentences and a consistent topic string (see [topic position and old-before-new information](topic-position-and-old-before-new-information.md)) are not enough to make a whole document feel coherent — readers also need to see the point of each unit of the document, at every scale, before working through that unit's detail. The general, scale-invariant principle: **a unit of writing reads as clear when it opens with a short segment the reader can easily grasp, which frames the longer, more complex segment that follows.** This applies to a simple sentence (subject/topic first), a complex sentence (main clause stating the point, then supporting clauses), a paragraph (an opening sentence or two), a section or subsection (an introductory paragraph), and a whole document (an introduction, proportionally short relative to the body).

The technical name for the sentence that carries this framing role at any scale is a **point sentence** — the familiar terms "topic sentence" (paragraph level) and "thesis" (whole-document level) are just scale-specific names for the same thing. The point sentence belongs at the *end* of its unit's short opening segment — the last thing the reader reads before the longer material begins — and it should state, toward its own end (in its [stress position](stress-position-and-emphasis.md)), the key concepts the rest of the unit will develop. Readers scan an opening segment specifically looking for this: a section that buries its point mid-paragraph, or never states one, reads as unfocused even if every individual sentence in it is well written.

Beyond point sentences, two more things are required for a reader to experience a document as coherent:

- **Relevance**: every part of a section has to be visibly relevant to that section's point. Relevant material generally falls into a short set of kinds — background or context, the points of subsections, reasons supporting the point, evidence supporting a reason, an explanation of method, or consideration of another point of view. Content outside these categories reads as a digression even when the writer sees the connection.
- **Order**: readers need to perceive the principle behind how parts are sequenced, and different orders call for different signal words — chronological order (first, then, finally; or as a result, because of that, for cause-effect), coordinate order where sections function as equal, parallel pillars (first, second...; also, another, more important), and logical order such as example-then-generalization or premise-then-conclusion (for example, on the other hand, it follows that).

A practical, genre-honest caveat: not every paragraph needs to hit this structure perfectly. Newspaper paragraphs are often one sentence; scholarly paragraphs can run half a page; short [transitional or emphatic paragraphs](point-first-and-point-last-paragraphs.md) are normal and don't need their own point sentence. What matters more for felt coherence is that the *section* containing those paragraphs is well introduced — a reader who already knows a section's point will tolerate individual paragraphs within it that are structured loosely, but no amount of well-built individual paragraphs compensates for a section that never told the reader what they add up to.

**Checking a draft for global coherence**: mark where the introduction to the whole document ends; divide the body into sections and subsections; circle each section's introductory segment; circle each section's point sentence. Then verify that each point sentence sits at or near the end of its introductory segment, states the key concepts developed in what follows, and that the sequence of point sentences, read one after another, summarizes the document as a whole. If a reader might not find these features quickly, the fix is not to explain more — it's to move the point earlier and cut the material that isn't visibly relevant to it. See also [strong opening technique](strong-opening-technique.md) for how this document-level "state the point early" discipline differs from a narrative opening's job of earning attention before the point is known.
