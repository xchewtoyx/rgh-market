---
type: concept
title: Imposing Logical Order on Groupings
description: >
  An inductive grouping needs an order that reflects the kind of
  analysis that produced it — time order, structural order, or degree
  order — and a list that resists any of the three is probably an
  invalid or incomplete grouping.
sources:
  - title: "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving"
    resource: "The Minto Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto), ch. 6"
---

The mind has only three ways of analytically relating a set of items to each other, and each produces its own ordering principle for a grouping inside a [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md):

- **Time order** — the actions or steps required to produce an effect, used when the grouping represents a process or a cause chain. Separate causes from effects explicitly, and split a long procedure into nested sub-processes rather than one flat sequence; visualizing each action and the concrete result it leaves in hand is what reveals missing steps, inconsistent levels of abstraction between items, or process logic the writer hadn't made explicit even to themselves.
- **Structural order** — the order that follows a properly visualized object, system, organization, or process, used when the grouping divides a whole into its parts. The division has to be MECE — mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive — and physical parts should be described in the order the reader would naturally see or build them, mirroring the real structure rather than an arbitrary listing order.
- **Degree order** — used when the grouping classifies like things and ranks them by how much of some shared characteristic they possess, normally strongest first. This requires defining the characteristic precisely enough to check that every relevant member of the group is actually included; presenting the ranking in reverse (weakest first, building to strongest) is a legitimate stylistic choice for effect, but it isn't a different logical order.

To repair a list that doesn't yet have a clear order: name the specific kind of problem or point each item represents, subgroup the items that share a kind, and then look for whichever of the three orders actually fits the resulting subgroups. If no order can be found no matter how the items are subgrouped, that's diagnostic, not just inconvenient — it usually means the grouping is invalid, incomplete, or is quietly hiding a different, better message than the one currently being forced into a flat list. See [deductive and inductive grouping](deductive-and-inductive-grouping.md) for the prior step of establishing that a set of ideas belongs together at all before ordering them.
