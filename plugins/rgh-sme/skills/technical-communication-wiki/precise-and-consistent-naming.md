A name — a variable, a field, a status value, a short identifier anywhere technical work gets discussed — functions as documentation whether or not anyone intended it to: a reader who encounters it forms an assumption about what it means and generally trusts that assumption without checking it against a fuller definition. This makes a name that's merely "reasonably close" to correct more dangerous than an obviously bad one, because a close name gets trusted, and a wrong trusted assumption is exactly the kind of error a reader won't think to double check. The same word used for two genuinely different things is the sharpest version of this failure: a reader who has learned what the word means in one context will unconsciously carry that meaning into the next place they see it, even when a careful read of the surrounding material would have revealed the difference — the name itself pre-empts the scrutiny that would have caught the error. The same failure recurs at the level of ordinary vocabulary, not just identifiers, when a document has been compressed to the point that it has no room left to disambiguate a recurring word — see [bullet-point compression hides technical nuance](bullet-point-compression-hides-technical-nuance.md).

**Precision** is the first pillar: a name should let someone who has never seen its definition guess, with reasonable confidence, both what it refers to and what it doesn't. A useful self-test is to imagine the name shown in complete isolation, stripped of its declaration, its surrounding context, and any attached documentation — how close would a guess based on the bare name land? Names fail this test in two opposite directions. Too generic, and the name invites the reader to project whichever meaning fits their own expectations rather than the actual one (a bare `count` or `status` says nothing about what's being counted or what the possible states are); a boolean in particular needs a name that reads as a true/false claim, not a noun that leaves what "true" even means unstated. Too specific, and a name smuggles in an assumption that isn't actually guaranteed — naming a general-purpose parameter after one particular caller's use of it implies a narrower contract than the thing actually has. A useful diagnostic in both directions: if a clean, precise, reasonably short name is proving hard to find, that's often not a naming problem at all — it's a sign the thing being named doesn't have a single, well-defined purpose, and the real fix is separating it into two things that each name easily.

**Consistency** is the second pillar, and it only holds if three conditions all hold together: the same name is always used for its designated purpose; that name is never reused for anything else; and the purpose itself is drawn narrowly enough that everything sharing the name genuinely behaves the same way. The third condition is the one that's easiest to violate without noticing — a name can be used consistently in the narrow sense of "always meaning roughly this kind of thing" while quietly spanning two behaviors that a reader needs to tell apart, which recreates the trusted-wrong-assumption failure even though nothing was technically renamed incorrectly. When two closely related things need to coexist under a shared concept, keep the shared base term and distinguish them with a prefix or qualifier, rather than inventing two unrelated names that hide their relationship or reusing one bare name for both.

One further calibration rule ties precision and consistency together: the greater the distance between where a name is defined and where it's used, the more the name needs to carry on its own, and so the more precise (usually longer) it needs to be. A short, generic name is genuinely fine when its entire span of relevance is visible at once — a loop counter used only within a few adjacent lines needs no more than `i` — but the same shortness becomes a liability once a reader might encounter a use of the name far from its definition, with no immediately visible context to disambiguate it.

A practical drafting technique for finding a precise name (or heading, or label) when one doesn't come immediately: write a full sentence describing what the thing does or contains, then compress that sentence down to its essential words. Naming top-down from a vague sense of "what to call this" tends to produce a name that only makes sense to the person who already understands the thing; naming by compressing an actual description forces the description to exist first, and the resulting name inherits its precision.

Renaming something isn't just a correction to a label that was slightly off — a new name can genuinely change how a reader perceives the thing itself. A name that finally fits reveals a role or a relationship to other things that a wrong or generic name had been quietly obscuring; the object hasn't changed, but what readers notice about it, and what they think to do with it next, can shift substantially once it's named accurately. This is worth remembering as a reason to treat renaming as a live, ongoing tool rather than a one-time fix applied only when a name is actively wrong. See [conversational vocabulary as a documentation diagnostic](conversational-vocabulary-as-a-documentation-diagnostic.md) for a way to catch a name that's already right in how people talk but hasn't yet made it into what's written down.

Abbreviations are a specific, easy-to-underestimate way consistency breaks down: once a term gets shortened, every later use has to guess at the exact same shortening, and a scheme that isn't fixed in advance (is it "Mgr" or "Mngr"?) reliably drifts, because each writer picks a plausible-looking abbreviation independently. The fix is the same consistency discipline applied specifically to abbreviations: pick one abbreviation per term, write it down, and reuse it exactly — or avoid abbreviating a term at all if there's any doubt a reader could reconstruct the shortening correctly on their own.

Because naming is a recurring, high-volume decision, a merely acceptable name in any one spot rarely seems worth the extra effort to improve — but small per-instance costs compound the same way [unnecessary precision costs a reader on every number](trimming-precision-to-what-the-decision-needs.md), not just once: a system has far more names in it than any other kind of documentation, so consistent rigor here has an outsized cumulative payoff or cost. See [labeling systems for clarity](labeling-systems-for-clarity.md) for the same precision-and-consistency discipline applied to labels a reader chooses between — headings, navigation items, link text — rather than to identifiers a reader has to trust at a glance; and, in the end, this is an audience question rather than a taste question: whether a given name is too cryptic or too unwieldy is something the actual readers of it get to decide, not its author. See [maintaining style consistency across a document set](maintaining-style-consistency-across-a-document-set.md) for the same discipline scaled up from individual names to conventions spanning a whole document set.
