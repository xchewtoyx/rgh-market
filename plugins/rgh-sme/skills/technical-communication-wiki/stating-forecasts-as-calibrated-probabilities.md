title: Stating Forecasts as Calibrated Probabilities, Not Binary Claims
description: >
  A recommendation or forecast written as a flat assertion hides the
  confidence behind it and invites a reader to judge it "wrong" whenever
  the less likely outcome happens — stating the actual probability
  instead lets both the decision and its later evaluation stay honest.

  - title: "Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts"

A recommendation, risk assessment, or forecast is often written as a flat claim — "this migration will succeed," "the vendor will hit the deadline" — even when the writer's actual belief is closer to "probably, but not certainly." Collapsing a genuine probability down to a binary claim throws away real information the reader needs: a reader told flatly that something will happen has no way to know whether the writer meant near-certainty or a confident guess, and has no basis for deciding how much contingency planning the situation actually warrants.

Writing the estimate as an explicit probability instead — "roughly 75% likely to land on time," not "it will land on time" — fixes this in both directions. It tells the reader how much weight to actually put on the claim, and it protects the claim from being misjudged later: a stated 75% is not falsified by the 25% outcome occurring, the same way a well-priced betting line isn't "wrong" just because the underdog won. A reader (or the writer, looking back) who treats any non-certain forecast as flatly right or wrong once the outcome is known is applying a standard no probabilistic statement could ever meet — and that same all-or-nothing standard quietly punishes writers for being honest about uncertainty, which pushes future forecasts back toward overconfident, falsely binary claims. Stating a real probability up front, and then evaluating it as a probability afterward — did outcomes at the "70% confident" level actually land around 70% of the time, tracked across many such calls — is the only evaluation standard that doesn't collapse the moment a single low-probability event occurs.

The same discipline works as a listener's tool, not just a writer's: when someone raises a bare "is it possible that...", the useful follow-up is asking them to attach a number to it, because almost anything is "possible" at a low enough probability and treating mere possibility as equivalent to likelihood is how speculative worries crowd out well-supported ones. Asking "what percent likely, roughly?" forces a vague hedge into a comparable, actionable estimate.

Two concrete phrasings make this practical without requiring real statistical rigor. A **numeric confidence figure** ("I'm about 60% confident this root cause is correct") is directly convertible to a shared scale a reader can compare across claims. A **range of plausible values** ("the migration will likely take three to five days") works just as well when a single number would overstate precision, and the width of the range itself communicates something a point estimate can't: a narrow range signals real information, a wide one signals that genuine uncertainty (or luck) is still in play.

Writing confidence explicitly this way has effects beyond accuracy. It's easier to revise in public later — updating "I was 60% confident" to "now I think it's 40%" reads as new information changing a considered view, where retracting a flat claim reads as simply having been wrong, which makes writers reluctant to revise at all. It also reads as more credible, not less: a stated confidence level signals that the writer has actually thought about how sure they are, rather than defaulting to false certainty because certainty sounds more authoritative. And it invites correction: a reader who sees an unqualified claim has little social opening to push back, while a reader who sees "I'm 70% confident" can offer the missing 30% without contradicting the writer outright — the same function [disclosing limitations early](disclosing-limitations-early.md) serves by placing caveats where a reader will actually register them, rather than defaulting to a falsely clean claim and hoping it holds up.

Verbal hedge words ("likely," "possible," "significant risk") look like they solve the flat-claim problem without requiring a number, but research on how readers actually interpret them shows they don't: different readers assign the same word wildly different numeric meanings, so two people can both nod along to "likely to succeed" while privately picturing 60% and 90%. Budescu, Broomell, and Por's study of the IPCC's climate reports — which deliberately standardized terms like "very likely" to specific probability ranges precisely to avoid this — found that readers largely ignored the standardized definitions and fell back on their own personal reading of the word anyway, producing what the researchers called an **illusion of communication**: everyone leaves the exchange believing agreement was reached, when in fact each party privately attached a different number to the same sentence. A defined glossary of hedge terms doesn't fix this on its own, because a reader who never consults the glossary — which is most readers, most of the time — still just reads the word. This is the direct argument for skipping verbal hedges in favor of an explicit number or range wherever the claim matters enough for the reader's interpretation to be worth getting right.
