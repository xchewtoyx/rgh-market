type: concept
title: Revising for Argument Visibility
description: >
  Revise an argumentative document's global frame — title, introduction,
  section order, claim hierarchy — before its sentences, and check
  specifically whether a reader skimming only its structural signals
  could recover the line of reasoning.
sources:
  - title: "The Craft of Research, Fifth Edition"
    resource: "The Craft of Research (Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup, FitzGerald), ch. 11"
---

Revise a draft the way a reader actually experiences it: try to identify the main claim, the problem it solves, the sequence of reasons supporting it, the evidence behind each reason, and how the document handles objections — purely from what's visible in the document's structure, not from knowledge the writer already has of what they meant. This has to happen at the global level — title, introduction, conclusion, section order, and the hierarchy of claims and subordinate claims — before sentence-level editing, because polishing sentences inside a section that's in the wrong place, or that doesn't actually advance the argument, wastes the polish the moment the section moves or gets cut.

Make the argument's logic visible through structural signals a skimming reader will actually use: informative headings that state a section's point rather than merely naming its topic (see [point sentences and document-level coherence](point-sentences-and-document-coherence.md)), topic sentences that keep each paragraph's subject consistent, explicit transitions between sections, deliberate repetition of key terms so a reader can track a concept across the document, and signposts that tell the reader where they are in the overall line of reasoning. A reader should be able to reconstruct the argument's skeleton by skimming only these structural elements — if they can't, sentence-level clarity elsewhere in the document won't compensate.

A useful diagnostic checklist for a specifically argumentative document, distinct from general clarity editing: is the main claim buried too late for the reader to orient around it; do any sections exist that don't actually advance the claim, however interesting they are on their own; is evidence presented without an explanation of what it's evidence for (see [assembling reasons and evidence](storyboarding-an-argument.md)); are there abrupt shifts between sections with no signaled connection; and does the conclusion do real work — stating implications, addressing "so what happens now" — or does it just repeat the claim already stated in the introduction. Getting feedback from readers who genuinely represent the intended audience, and treating their specific points of confusion as revision evidence rather than a comprehension failure on their part, is more reliable than a writer's own re-read, for the same reason self-editing misses clarity problems elsewhere in this domain — a writer already knows what they meant.
