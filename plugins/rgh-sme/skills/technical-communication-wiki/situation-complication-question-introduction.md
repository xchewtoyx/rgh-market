description: >
  An effective introduction is a short narrative — Situation, Complication,
  Question — that reminds the reader of what they already know rather
  than informing them of something new, and that narrative determines
  what question the rest of the document has to answer.
sources:
  - title: "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving"

The introduction to a [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md) is not a summary or a preview — it's a short story with four beats that puts writer and reader "in the same place" before the document's main point is stated. **Situation** opens with an agreed, self-sufficient statement grounded in a particular time and place, something the reader already accepts without argument. **Complication** states what changed or what tension arose against that situation. That tension produces the **Question** the reader now has — and the document's top-level point is the **Answer** to exactly that question, not to some other question the writer finds more interesting.

Most real documents are answering one of a small number of recurring questions: what should we do, how should/will/did we do it, should we do it, or why did it happen. The S-C-Q beats can be reordered for tone — leading with the complication reads as more urgent or concerned, leading with the situation reads as more measured — but all three elements have to be present somewhere in the introduction, in a form the reader recognizes as already true, before the document's answer is presented; an introduction that skips straight to the answer without establishing what question it's answering leaves the reader unable to judge whether the answer is even relevant to them.

Introduction length is governed by how much shared context the reader genuinely needs to reach the question, not by the length of the document that follows — a two-or-three-paragraph introduction is typical, and sometimes a single sentence suffices. Practical failure modes to check for: using exhibits or evidence to prove the situation or complication (the introduction's job is to remind, not to argue — argument belongs in the body); labeling a section "Introduction" or "Background" instead of writing an actual S-C-Q narrative; and introducing more than one independent starting question, which splits the reader's attention before the document has answered either one.

Certain business-document genres have recognizable S-C-Q shapes worth recognizing rather than reinventing: a directive frames a required action and answers with the steps to take it; a spending request's complication is usually urgency, and its key line typically defends the solution's adequacy, its financial case, and any extra benefit beyond the immediate need; a "how-to" document's complication is the gap between a current and a desired process, and its key line derives the steps that close it; a document comparing known alternatives measures each one against the reader's actual desired result, not against each other's pros and cons. Both consulting proposals and progress reviews have their own conventional S-C-Q variants worth recognizing in the same way.

Defining a problem precisely before writing its introduction — see [the problem-definition framework](problem-definition-framework.md) — is usually what surfaces the Complication and the last known fact needed to complete this narrative cleanly.
