title: Adapting Pyramid Structure for Oral Presentations
description: >
  An oral presentation is not a written document read aloud with slides
  attached — it needs its own storyboarded adaptation of the same
  pyramid logic to the audience's limited ability to read, listen, and
  interpret visuals all at once.
sources:
  - title: "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving"
    resource: "The Minto Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto), ch. 11"
---

The underlying [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md) of a piece of reasoning stays the same whether it's delivered as a document or a presentation, but a live audience can't read, listen, and interpret a visual simultaneously the way a solitary reader can process a page at their own pace — so the delivery format has to change even when the argument doesn't. Treat a presentation as four distinct elements to design separately: text slides, exhibit slides, the spoken script, and a storyboard that ties them together.

**Text slides** should display the point being made, not a visual transcript of everything the presenter is saying — the same question-answer and grouping logic used in prose applies to what a slide shows, and a dense bullet list forces the audience to choose between reading the slide and listening to the speaker, which loses both. This assumes the point genuinely survives compression to a slide in the first place; for complex or high-stakes technical findings, compression itself can discard the caveats and reasoning the audience needs — see [bullet-point compression hides technical nuance](bullet-point-compression-hides-technical-nuance.md). **Exhibit slides** (charts, diagrams, tables) should each answer one specific question — how items are distributed, how they compare, how something changes over time, how parts relate to a whole or to each other — and the visual form chosen should make that specific answer immediate to see; title an exhibit with its conclusion, not with its subject matter, the same discipline [point sentences](point-sentences-and-document-coherence.md) apply in prose.

**Storyboard before designing any slide.** Lay out the audience's starting situation, the complication, the question it creates, the answer, the key-line flow that supports the answer, and — for each point in that flow — the precise visual (if any) that point actually needs, before opening a slide-design tool. Storyboarding first is what keeps a presentation a coherent story rather than a sequence of visually polished but disconnected pieces of evidence; skipping straight to slide design tends to produce exactly that disconnected sequence, because slide-by-slide thinking replaces the argument that should be driving it.
