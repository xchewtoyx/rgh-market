title: Anatomy of a Case Story
description: >
  A well-told incident narrative — the kind used to transfer expertise,
  document a postmortem, or illustrate a lesson — has identifiable parts
  and quality criteria distinct from a scientific paper's structure, and
  a story missing them reads as a flat, forgettable chronology.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power (Gary Klein), ch. 11"
---

A narrative built to transfer expertise or document what actually happened in an incident — an internal case study, a postmortem writeup, an onboarding "here's a hard case we hit once" — has a recognizable anatomy: **agents** (who was involved), a **predicament** (the problem they were trying to solve), **intentions** (what they meant to do), **actions** (what they actually did), **objects** (the tools or artifacts involved), **causality** (the effects, intended and unintended, that their actions produced), **context** (the surrounding circumstances that made those actions make sense), and **surprises** (the unexpected turns within the incident). A writeup missing several of these — especially intentions and surprises — reads as a bare chronology, which readers absorb passively and quickly forget; a story with a genuine complication and turn is read attentively and retold.

**A good case story is judged by the same standard as a well-run informal experiment.** Because it reports what actually happened under one full, uncontrolled set of real-world conditions, a reader can extract genuine causal insight from it the way they would from a controlled study — provided the story satisfies four criteria: **plausibility** (each step follows believably from the last, or the writer explicitly accounts for why an implausible-looking step happened anyway), **consistency** (the pieces fit together into one coherent account), **economy** (no extraneous detail diluting the causal thread), and **uniqueness** (the account isn't just as easily explained by some other, equally-plausible story). These are the same four tests used to judge whether a mental simulation of a plan is sound — a case story is, structurally, a communicated simulation of something that actually occurred.

**A story needs an ending as much as an experiment needs its results analyzed.** A writeup that stops once the crisis is resolved, without stating what was actually learned or how the resolution bears on the original predicament, hasn't finished the job — the same way a plan that's handed off with no feedback about how it played out leaves nothing for the next person to learn from. Draft every case story back through its own predicament: state plainly what the resolution means for someone facing the same situation later.

**Some stories exist specifically to teach organizational permission, not just technique** — call these permission stories. A story where someone had to physically insist on being heard, override an instrument reading, or break protocol to prevent a bad outcome teaches a reader, implicitly, when it is culturally sanctioned to do the same in this organization — a lesson no policy document states outright, because it's really a story about the boundaries of acceptable behavior under pressure, not a procedure. When writing up an incident, watch for this second layer of lesson alongside the technical one; readers absorb both whether or not the writer intended to teach the permission lesson at all.

Whoever is the subject of a case story cares how they're represented in it, independent of how useful the story is to its intended audience — a technically excellent, well-told account that reduces a contributor's carefully prepared formal work to an informal anecdote can generate real resentment even when the story serves readers better than the material it replaced. Circulate a case story past its subjects before publishing it widely, and be prepared for the possibility that the version most valuable to future readers is not the version its subject wants told.
