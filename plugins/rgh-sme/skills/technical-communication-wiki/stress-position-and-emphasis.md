title: Stress Position and Sentence Emphasis
description: >
  The last few words of a sentence are where readers naturally hear
  emphasis, which makes sentence endings the right place for unfamiliar
  technical terms, new information, and whatever the writer most wants
  remembered.
sources:
  - title: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace"
    resource: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, 9th ed. (Joseph M. Williams, Joseph Bizup), Lesson 6"
---

Just as the opening of a sentence has a psychological role — see [topic position and old-before-new information](topic-position-and-old-before-new-information.md) — the closing few words have a parallel one, called **stress**: the position where a reader's inner voice naturally rises to emphasize a word, even when reading silently. Ending a sentence on low-content words (a trailing attribution, a hedge, a weak preposition) makes the whole sentence feel like it trails off, regardless of how strong the claim inside it actually was.

The practical payoff is a rule for handling technical difficulty: carry readers from simplicity to complexity within a sentence, not the reverse. A sentence that front-loads unfamiliar technical terms forces readers to process the hardest material before they have any momentum; the same content restructured so the technical terms land at the end — after a short, familiar subject and verb — reads as clear to readers who know nothing about the subject, because the sentence's shape does the work of easing them in. This is why even papers written for expert audiences often add a sentence purely to relocate a new term to the stress position ("We term these cells lymphokine-activated killer (LAK) cells.").

**Three tactical revisions** when the wrong words end up in stress position: trim the sentence's end if it's just restating something already implied; shift peripheral or hedging material (like "for the most part") leftward, away from the ending; and shift genuinely new or important information rightward, toward the end, if it currently sits earlier in the sentence.

**Six syntactic devices** relocate emphasis when a straightforward rewrite isn't enough: the passive voice (moves an object into subject position, closer to the end); "there is/there are" constructions (delays a subject past its verb, useful for introducing a new topic a paragraph will go on to develop, but wordy if overused); what-shift and it-shift constructions (both extract part of a sentence and push it rightward for emphasis); "not only X, but Y as well" (the second, final element of a paired contrast gets the emphasis — so put whatever you want stressed last); and pronoun substitution (a sentence that repeats, at its end, a word already used near the end of the previous sentence lands flatly, because a repeated word carries no fresh stress — substitute a pronoun or reword instead).

Beyond a single sentence, the stress position of a sentence that *introduces* a paragraph or document does extra work: readers scan that opening sentence specifically for the concepts the rest of the passage will develop, and are most likely to notice and retain them if they appear at that sentence's end rather than its beginning. This is the same mechanism the [pyramid structure](pyramid-structure.md) exploits for whole documents, and it is developed further at the document scale in [point sentences and document-level coherence](point-sentences-and-document-coherence.md).
