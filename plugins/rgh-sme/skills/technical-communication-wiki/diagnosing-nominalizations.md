title: Diagnosing and Revising Nominalizations
description: >
  A step-by-step procedure for finding actions that have been buried
  inside abstract nouns (nominalizations) and rebuilding the sentence
  around the character and verb they actually describe.
sources:
  - title: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace"
    resource: "Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace, 9th ed. (Joseph M. Williams, Joseph Bizup), Lesson 3"

A nominalization is a noun made from a verb or adjective — "discussion" from "discuss," "carefulness" from "careful," "our request is that you review" instead of "we request that you review." Prose reads as dense specifically when nominalizations sit in the subject position of a sentence, especially paired with a near-empty verb like "is," "has," or "occurs." "Our lack of data prevented evaluation of UN actions" buries two actions (lacking, evaluating) as nouns; the fix restores them as verbs with their real characters as subjects.

**Diagnose.** Ignoring any short introductory phrase, underline the first seven or eight words of the sentence. Look for an abstract noun functioning as the subject, and for a long stretch of words before the sentence reaches any verb at all.

**Analyze.** Identify the main characters, especially flesh-and-blood ones, and the actions they perform — particularly actions currently buried inside nominalizations. Ask directly: "who is doing what?"

**Rewrite.** Turn the nominalized action into a verb, and make the character that goes with it the subject. Reassemble the sentence using a subordinating conjunction (because, if, when, although, why, how, whether, that) to connect the resulting clauses, rather than the vague prepositions ("of," "by," "on the part of") that nominalizations require. "The outsourcing of high-tech work to Asia by corporations means the loss of jobs for many American workers" becomes "Because corporations outsource high-tech work to Asia, many American workers lose their jobs."

This mechanical check is necessary because writers routinely misjudge their own prose: a writer who already knows what a sentence means reads their intention into it, so a passage can feel perfectly clear to its author and dense to everyone else. Underlining the first several words of every sentence bypasses that self-familiarity and gives an objective signal — no character, no specific verb, revise.

**When to keep the nominalization instead.** Four cases don't need fixing: (1) the nominalization is a short subject that deliberately refers back to something in the previous sentence, serving flow rather than obscuring meaning; (2) it replaces an awkward "the fact that" construction ("her acknowledgment of the problem" beats "the fact that she acknowledged the problem"); (3) it names what would naturally be the object of the verb ("I accepted her request" beats unpacking "request" into a clause); (4) it names a concept so familiar to readers that it already functions as a virtual character — see [characters as subjects](characters-as-subjects.md). Turning every nominalization into a verb regardless of context produces choppy, over-simplified prose, not clarity.

Consistently fixing the nominalizations that do need it has compounding benefits: sentences get shorter (nominalizations require extra articles and prepositions that verbs don't), their logic gets clearer (subordinating conjunctions state a precise relationship where nominalized prepositional phrases only gesture at one), and the sentence tends to fall into the actual chronological order of events instead of a scrambled one.
