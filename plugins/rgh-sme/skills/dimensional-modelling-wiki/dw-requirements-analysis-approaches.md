Dimensional modeling (like 3NF ER modeling) is a database *design* technique — some form of *analysis* has to discover the actual BI requirements first. Two traditional approaches to that analysis exist, plus a third, more recent posture; most real DW/BI initiatives combine elements of all three rather than following one exclusively.

## Data-driven analysis

Requirements are inferred by analyzing the operational data sources directly, deliberately minimizing user involvement — the underlying assumption is that DW design is fundamentally about re-modeling several operational sources into one "perfect" normalized model, with users consulted only afterward, if at all. Without early user involvement to prioritize and scope, this produces designs that are slow and expensive to build, biased toward the source systems' own operational perspective rather than how the business actually thinks, hard to query, and frequently useless for the questions users actually have. Historically nicknamed "build it and they will come" or the "field of dreams" approach. It persists in practice because it stays within IT's technical comfort zone and avoids the harder work of engaging business stakeholders — but it has grown steadily more problematic as source systems (especially packaged/ERP applications) have become more generic and complex, undermining the premise that a single ideal normalized model can even be inferred from the sources alone.

## Reporting-driven analysis

Requirements are gathered by interviewing business stakeholders — individually or in small groups — about their reporting needs, then cross-referencing interview notes and report mock-ups into a consolidated, source-verified requirements list for stakeholder sign-off before design begins. This focuses effort on urgent, prioritized reporting needs and can deliver successfully when scope is well managed, but has two structural problems: it's time-consuming to interview enough stakeholders for a genuinely enterprise-wide (not just single-department) perspective, and detailed analytical requirements are inherently **accretive** — they build up in layers, with each answered report triggering the next question, so stakeholders genuinely cannot articulate future needs that depend on insights they don't have yet. Gaining consensus on shared data terms and obtaining broad sign-off can stretch out indefinitely.

Over-reliance on reporting-driven analysis has a specific, damaging failure mode: modelers who match a design too closely to the *current* set of requested reports, rather than treating those reports as clues pointing toward the underlying [business process](business-process.md) that genuinely needs modeling in depth, produce a design that can't handle change — see the [designing from reports antipattern](designing-from-reports-antipattern.md). This has unfairly led some to characterize dimensional modeling itself as inherently "too report-centric," suitable only for narrow data marts rather than enterprise design; the actual cause is analysis that reverse-engineers today's summary reports instead of modeling the atomic-level business process behind them iteratively.

## Proactive design

DW/BI development has increasingly moved from reactive (built long after an operational system proves inadequate for reporting, once a backlog has already built up) to **proactive** — designed and delivered concurrently with a new operational source system, or even ahead of it. Business-side reasons include the shrinking distinction between operational and analytical reporting, and organizations with an established DW/BI track record now expecting every major new system to need a mart or DW integration from day one, with no tolerance for an interim "good enough" gap.

Proactive design's benefit is that BI stakeholders can set the data agenda *while the operational system is still being built*, when requirements are cheap to incorporate — this matters especially for **mandatory data**: an attribute that looks optional from a purely operational viewpoint can be specified `not null` and captured correctly from day one, before operational users develop workaround habits that later defeat any attempt to backfill it. It also helps ETL: a still-moving-target source lets the DW/BI team hand the operational team a "perfect" extraction interface spec derived from the proactive model, and is a natural opportunity to get adequate change-data-capture support (consistent timestamps, update-reason codes distinguishing genuine change from mere correction) built into the source from the start.

The corresponding challenge is that requirements gathering has to happen before real data — or often any data — exists: reporting-driven techniques lose their usual icebreaker questions ("how could your favorite reports be improved?") since there are no existing reports to reference, and data-driven profiling is of little use against a source that's still under development or populated only with test data. This produces a **"data then requirements" conundrum**: genuine, detailed BI requirements are hard for either side to articulate until people have actually lived with real data for a while, but without early detailed requirements a proactive design still risks failing to deliver the right information on time — and a backlog builds the moment real data starts flowing regardless. Resolving this chicken-and-egg problem is a large part of the motivation for agile, iterative [dimensional design](four-step-dimensional-design-process.md): short cycles that get real (even imperfect) data in front of stakeholders quickly, rather than trying to fully specify requirements before any data exists at all. See [gathering business requirements](gathering-business-requirements.md) for the collaborative, example-driven technique this leads to, and [agile data profiling](agile-data-profiling.md) for validating the resulting model against whatever source data is actually reachable, however incomplete.
