When [gathering business requirements](gathering-business-requirements.md) by [modeling with example data](gathering-business-requirements.md), asking for a handful of stories that each illustrate a specific theme extracts far more design information per example row than asking for stories at random. Five themes, typically enough to produce 5-6 example rows per event, cover most of what a detail's design needs:

1. **Typical** — a story using common, representative values for every detail: a frequent customer, a popular product, average quantities. This establishes the baseline story everyone recognizes.
2. **Different** — a second story with different values for every detail, to discover the range of values the warehouse must represent. For *when* details specifically, use relative descriptions ("Today," "Yesterday," "5 Years Ago") rather than fixed dates, so the story stays meaningful long after modeling. If the most recent example given is "Yesterday," a daily refresh is probably sufficient; if it's "Today," near-real-time delivery may be a genuine requirement — confirm this is budgeted, since it significantly complicates ETL and cost, and pin down what "Today" actually means (an hour ago? ten minutes ago?) before committing to it. For *who*/*what* details, ask for both old and new values (an inactive customer alongside a brand-new one; a discontinued product alongside one just released).
3. **Repeat** — a story as close as possible to the typical story, used to discover what makes each story unique. Ask directly: "Can this [subject] [verb] this [object] again on the same day?" Every time a new detail is added to the event, return to this question and extend it with the new detail ("...from the same location?") to test whether uniqueness has been reached yet. This is the primary technique for discovering a fact table's [grain](grain.md) interactively with stakeholders, rather than declaring it in the abstract: as long as the repeat story's every detail still matches the typical story, the grain is not yet fully defined, and modeling continues until a detail (sometimes a transaction identifier) finally differentiates the two.
4. **Missing** — a story to discover which details can be absent (unknown, not applicable, or not yet available) versus mandatory, and how stakeholders want a missing value displayed on reports. For quantities, this also settles whether a missing value should be `NULL` (arithmetically correct — it drops out of sums and averages) or a default like zero. Details confirmed mandatory get marked **MD** (mandatory detail) directly in the column type. A missing-value story is allowed to be unrealistically sparse — that's fine, since its only job is to test optionality. A subject that is *consistently* missing in practice (an anonymous retail customer, say) is still given a placeholder name during storytelling to keep the story readable, even though the placeholder itself is dropped from the eventual physical fact table. Heavy missingness across many details in a *discrete* or *recurring* event (as opposed to an [evolving event](event-story-types.md), where many details are validly unknown until a later milestone) is a warning sign that the event has been over-generalized — it is often better split into several more specific events where the defining details are reliably present.
5. **Group** — a story that exposes variation in the *meaning* of a detail, not just its value: "Is a customer always an individual? Can a product be a bundle?" This surfaces mixed business models (B2B versus B2C customers) and multi-valued relationships (product bundles), pointing toward design patterns handled elsewhere — mixed-subtype and multilevel dimensions, hierarchy maps, and multi-valued (bridge-table) dimensions. Collect just enough group stories for clarity; the goal is not to enumerate every possible variation the warehouse will ever see — that is what the warehouse itself is for.

## Sequencing the themes

Typical and Different stories are collected first, once at least one *when* detail is captured (a story needs a point in time to feel concrete). Repeat, Missing, and Group stories are then interleaved as new details are discovered through the [7Ws](seven-ws-framework.md), since each new detail can change the answer to "does this repeat story now count as unique?" or "can this detail be missing?". Running all five themes against every event detail, rather than accepting the first plausible example offered, is what makes a handful of rows do the discovery work a much longer unthemed interview would otherwise need.
