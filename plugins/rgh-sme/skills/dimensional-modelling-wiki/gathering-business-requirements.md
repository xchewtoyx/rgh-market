Before modeling begins, the design team must understand both business needs and data realities. See [DW requirements analysis approaches](dw-requirements-analysis-approaches.md) for the traditional data-driven and reporting-driven techniques this collaborative, example-driven approach improves on, and for the proactive posture needed when a project must gather requirements ahead of a new operational source system existing at all. Business needs are gathered through sessions with business representatives covering KPIs, compelling business issues, decision-making processes, and analytic needs. Data realities are assessed through conversations with source system experts and high-level data profiling to establish feasibility.

The dimensional model should never be designed by a data modeler working in isolation. It unfolds through **collaborative dimensional modeling workshops** — sometimes called **modelstorming**, emphasizing that they should be quick, informal, and closer to a stand-up brainstorming session than a multi-day requirements-gathering exercise — where the data modeler leads, but business representatives and data governance representatives participate interactively, validating that the design matches how the business actually talks about and uses the data.

## Modeling by example, not by abstraction

Business stakeholders generally find abstract entity-relationship notation — even a simplified, conceptual-level version of it — too removed from how they think to engage with productively. A far more effective elicitation technique is to build the model directly from **concrete example data** rather than abstract attribute definitions: ask for a specific, realistic instance of an event (a named customer, a named product, an actual date and amount) rather than asking in the abstract what attributes an entity "has." Concrete examples reliably surface business reality an abstract model misses entirely — a worked comparison between an entity-relationship diagram and an example-driven table for the same order-processing process found the example-driven version alone revealed that customers could be individuals, companies, or government bodies, that products could be bundles of other products, that discounts were recorded as both percentages and money, and that orders were processed in more than one currency — none of which an abstract "Customer places Order for Product" diagram exposes on its own. Building a table of real example values column by column, directly with stakeholders, also produces something that looks and feels like a familiar report rather than a technical diagram, which stakeholders engage with far more readily and experience as visible, incremental progress.

These requirements feed directly into the first two steps of the [four-step dimensional design process](four-step-dimensional-design-process.md) — selecting the [business process](business-process.md) and declaring the [grain](grain.md). Requirements gathering should listen for and synthesize around business processes rather than a wishlist of reports or dashboard gauges — always ask what measurement event underlies a requested metric, since a dimensional model represents a measurement process, not an intended report. Classifying a candidate business event by its [event story type](event-story-types.md) — discrete, evolving, or recurring — gives a fast diagnostic for which fact table grain type the resulting design will need. Working systematically through the [7Ws](seven-ws-framework.md) for each event is the underlying discovery checklist that surfaces its dimensions and facts in the first place.

## Reusing conformed dimensions: abbreviated examples, not business keys

Once one event has been modeled with example data, later events that share its dimensions should reuse the *same* example values — a repeated customer name, a repeated product — rather than inventing fresh ones, since reusing examples is itself what confirms a dimension is genuinely [conformed](conformed-dimensions.md) across events. It is tempting to shortcut this by referencing stakeholders' cryptic operational **business keys** instead (an event table will end up with foreign keys anyway) — but business keys are usually opaque codes that strip event stories of the readability that makes example-driven modeling work in the first place, and (see [natural key](natural-key.md) and [surrogate key](surrogate-key.md)) don't make good physical keys in a dimensional model either.

The better technique is **abbreviated examples**: shortened forms of previously modeled example values (an employee or product name shortened to its initials, say) that stay readable, save whiteboard space, speed up storytelling, and can be expanded back to full examples later with a simple "replace all." Keep abbreviations unique *within* one dimension; where two examples collide (two employees both abbreviating to "JB"), append a sequence number (JB1, JB2) rather than reusing the same abbreviation for different people. An exceptionally memorable business key can still work well directly as a story example (an employee ID that's genuinely easy to remember) — the rule against business keys is about avoiding cryptic codes, not literal business-key values that happen to be readable. Giving stakeholders a handout of previously modeled dimension tables — just the example subjects and one or two defining attributes, not every column — alongside an up-to-date [bus matrix](enterprise-data-warehouse-bus-matrix.md) helps them decode abbreviations and spot reuse opportunities as new events are modeled.

## Time-boxing a collaborative modeling session

A collaborative requirements-and-design workshop benefits from an explicit shape and time budget rather than running open-ended: spend the first half modeling one event (typically the most important one) and its dimensions in real depth, then use the remaining time to storyboard several *related* events at a shallower level of detail — just enough to identify which further events and which dimensions look important enough to conform, without fully modeling all of them in the same session. A concrete four-hour split that works well: two hours on the first event and its dimensions in depth, one hour sketching related events more shallowly, and a final hour prioritizing what was found and confirming the most important item(s) are modeled in sufficient detail to act on. If time runs out before reaching a good stopping point, schedule a follow-up session rather than letting the workshop run over — a session that overruns tends to produce diminishing-quality output as participants tire, and a scheduled follow-up preserves both the energy and the discipline of the time-box.
