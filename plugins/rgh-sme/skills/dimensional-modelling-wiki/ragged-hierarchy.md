A ragged (variable depth) hierarchy doesn't have a fixed number of levels across all its branches, unlike a [fixed-depth positional hierarchy](fixed-depth-hierarchy.md) — an organization chart, a bill of materials, or a geographic hierarchy that's sometimes 4 levels deep and sometimes 6, depending on the branch.

A hierarchy is **balanced** when it has a fixed number of clearly defined levels (every [fixed-depth (attribute) hierarchy](fixed-depth-hierarchy.md) qualifies by definition) and **unbalanced** when it doesn't. Instance hierarchies are usually, but not always, unbalanced — a rare instance hierarchy (some departmental reporting structures, for example) still resolves into a consistent number of levels across every branch. Variable depth is not, by itself, the source of an instance hierarchy's reporting difficulty: the "looking up and looking down" problem described below afflicts a balanced instance hierarchy just as much as an unbalanced one — depth variability only adds the further, distinct problem that a fixed-depth positional workaround has no column to hold a level the hierarchy grows beyond what was originally provisioned for.

## Looking up and looking down

Users studying facts from a position in an instance hierarchy want to either **look down** (aggregate everything at or below a chosen member — total spending in departments beneath mine) or **look up** (aggregate everything at or above a chosen member — a volume discount computed against a parent company's total orders). Because facts in a fact table are tied to specific dimension members with no regard for hierarchy position, answering either question means traversing the recursive relationship first to obtain the relevant set of members, before that set can be used to constrain or join against the fact table. Recursive SQL (`CONNECT BY`, recursive CTEs) can answer these queries directly against a self-referencing dimension, but most BI tools cannot generate such queries, which is why designers generally reach for one of the two techniques below instead.

## Slightly ragged: force-fit into a positional design

When the depth range is narrow (for example, geographic hierarchies ranging 3-6 levels), it's often simpler to force-fit the hierarchy into a fixed-depth positional design sized for the maximum number of levels, populating the "missing" levels for shallower branches per business rule — propagating the lowest available value down into the empty attributes, propagating labels upward, or filling empty levels with "Not Applicable." This works cleanly only when the depth range stays narrow; a wider range (eight, ten, or more levels) makes the positional compromise break down, since attribute names must still make sense across every represented shape.

**Balancing technique when levels are simply missing, not conceptually absent**: many real-world ragged hierarchies aren't a true mix of shapes so much as a fixed-depth hierarchy with occasional gaps — a product with no subcategory because it's the only member of its type, say. Leaving the gap as a literal null, or filling it with a generic label like "Not Applicable," causes its own problems: nulls disappear from filters and groupings (see [null handling in dimensional models](null-handling-in-dimensional-models.md)), and a shared filler label like "Not Applicable" wrongly groups together every unrelated gap across the whole hierarchy, undermining stakeholder confidence and blocking drill-down through that level. The preferred fix is still to resolve the gaps with stakeholders directly during discovery, supplying real values; where that isn't possible, three mechanical interim techniques fill the gap with something more useful than a shared placeholder, pending a real answer: **top-down balancing** copies the value from the level directly above the gap down into it; **bottom-up balancing** copies the value from the level directly below the gap up into it; and **combined balancing** concatenates the values immediately above and below into a new, gap-specific fill value (so two different gaps produce two different, individually drillable filler values instead of one shared "Not Applicable"). If data profiling shows only a small fraction of paths (on the order of 1-2%) are actually ragged, that's more often a sign of data-quality errors than genuine structural raggedness — worth correcting at the source and treating the hierarchy as a plain [fixed-depth hierarchy](fixed-depth-hierarchy.md) instead of building any of this balancing machinery.

Flattening only ever answers "roll up to a specific named level," which is easy to query once the levels have consistent, business-meaningful names (a retail location dimension flattened into `store_name`/`warehouse_name`/`distribution_center_name`, for instance, makes "everything at or below Warehouse 57" an easy single-column constraint). It does not solve looking up and looking down in general: finding everything below a chosen member still first requires knowing which level that member sits at before constraining the right column, and looking up still requires reading the member's own row for its list of ancestors — neither is reasonable to expect from an end user with an ordinary BI tool, even after flattening. If business users are heard asking for a "level 2" member by name, that's usually a sign the hierarchy was already flattened somewhere upstream in the source system, and the true unflattened structure may no longer be recoverable.

## Fully ragged: indeterminate depth

For hierarchies of genuinely indeterminate depth, the positional compromise doesn't work at all. A recursive self-referencing parent key on the dimension row (classic parent/child) accurately represents the structure, but SQL historically had no native way to evaluate recursive pointers at query time; even with vendor extensions (`CONNECT BY`, recursive CTEs), the tree representation stays entangled with the dimension's own recursive pointer data — switching to a different rollup structure requires destructively modifying many pointers, and maintaining the dimension under [slowly changing dimension type 2](slowly-changing-dimension-type-2.md) is impractical, since changing a high-level node's key ripples key changes down its entire subtree.

This shape is also called an **instance hierarchy**, to distinguish it from a [fixed-depth (attribute) hierarchy](fixed-depth-hierarchy.md): it describes a recursive relationship among the *rows* (instances) of a single dimension — an employee reporting to another employee, a company owning another company, a part composed of other parts — rather than a master-detail relationship among a dimension's *columns*. It has no fixed number of levels, and every level shares the exact same set of attributes (since every row is an instance of the same entity), unlike an attribute hierarchy where each level typically has its own distinct attribute set.

A quick discovery-time diagnostic for this shape: try to give each level of a candidate hierarchy a distinct, meaningful business name. A [fixed-depth hierarchy](fixed-depth-hierarchy.md)'s levels always can be (day, month, quarter, year are genuinely different concepts) — an inability to do the same for a candidate hierarchy, because every level is really "the same kind of thing" one level removed (every level of a corporate ownership chain is just "a customer"; every level of a bill of materials is just "a part"), is a strong sign the candidate is an instance hierarchy in disguise, however many concrete levels it happens to have in the data at hand.

The standard solution is a [ragged hierarchy bridge table](ragged-hierarchy-bridge-table.md): a specially constructed table, independent of the primary dimension, holding one row per path in the tree from every node to every descendant beneath it — enabling full hierarchy traversal via standard SQL, with no recursion needed at query time.

Two alternative techniques embed the hierarchy directly in the dimension row instead of using a bridge table: see [pathstring hierarchy attribute](pathstring-hierarchy-attribute.md).

A candidate hierarchy's raggedness is typically discovered while positioning its levels on a [hierarchy chart](hierarchy-chart.md): a level found to have missing values for some members gets bracketed on the chart as ragged rather than assumed balanced.
