title: The Rational-Choice (Amoral-Calculator) Explanation of Failure
description: >
  The dominant post-1970s accident narrative that treats decision-makers as
  fully-informed utility-optimisers, so a bad-looking outcome must mean they
  were insufficiently informed, careless, or corrupt — the theoretical engine
  behind Bad Apple theory.
sources:
  - title: "Drift into Failure: From Hunting Broken Components to Understanding Complex Systems"

One of two recurring families of explanation for why disasters happen (the
other is the complexity family behind [drift into
failure](drift-into-failure.md)). It casts an organisation as rotten or
callous, running amoral cost/safety trade-offs, and prescribes finding and
punishing the culprits — fines, firings, criminal trials. This is the
explanatory engine behind what [old view / new
view](old-view-vs-new-view-of-human-error.md) calls **Bad Apple theory**.

Its psychological grounding is **rational choice theory**, formalised as
Subjective Expected Utility (SEU) theory, which assumes a decision-maker has:
complete information about the available alternatives and their consequences;
the objective, logical capacity to analyse them; the ability to discriminate
fine differences between options; and a stable, rankable utility function.
Given those assumptions, a decision that looks bad in hindsight can only be
explained by the decision-maker having been insufficiently informed or
insufficiently rational — they "should have tried harder."

Why it dominates: since the 1970s (Tenerife, Three Mile Island), the standard
explanatory move for large accidents shifted from "acts of God" to "failures
of risk management," constructed as human, organisational, or moral failure.
The rational-choice/amoral-calculator reading is not simply false — real
organisations do sometimes behave this way — but its danger is *invisibility
as a worldview*: when investigators do not recognise it as one lens among
several, they foreclose other readings and lose the ability to see drift,
[local rationality](local-rationality-principle.md), or [goal
conflicts](goal-conflicts-and-production-pressure.md) at all.

The direct rebuttal is the [local rationality
principle](local-rationality-principle.md): real decision-makers never have
SEU theory's complete information or stable utility function, so judging them
against that standard is judging them against knowledge only the outcome
later supplied — the mechanism of [hindsight bias](hindsight-bias.md). Both
the rational-choice family and the [chain-of-events
model](chain-of-events-model.md) it pairs naturally with share the same
ancestor: [Newtonian-Cartesian accident
thinking](newtonian-cartesian-accident-thinking.md).

Naturalistic studies of expert decision-makers sharpen the rebuttal further:
under real time pressure, experienced practitioners mostly do not use
anything resembling SEU-style option comparison even when they have the
skill to — see the [recognition-primed decision
model](recognition-primed-decision-model.md). Judging their choice against
a standard they never used, and normally could not have used, is not a
neutral yardstick.
