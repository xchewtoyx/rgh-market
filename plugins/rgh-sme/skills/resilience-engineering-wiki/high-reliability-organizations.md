  Empirical theory of how organisations (air traffic control, aircraft
  carriers, power plants) sustain consistently safe operation despite
  complexity, and its central blind spot around expert judgment under

Grew out of 1980s field studies of organisations that operate hazardous,
complex technology with an exceptionally low accident rate. The original
theory names four ingredients:

1. **Leadership safety objectives**: visible, consistent commitment from the
   top, with short-term efficiency sacrificed for chronic safety concerns —
   though the gap between the stated commitment and what happens in practice
   can stay wide.
2. **Redundancy**: building a reliable system from unreliable parts, via
   *duplication* (two people or parts doing the identical job) or *overlap*
   (partial, cheaper functional redundancy).
3. **Decentralization, culture, and continuity**: delegating safety-relevant
   decisions to local units close to the work, preferring active local
   exploration over passive top-down compliance, and constant
   training/rotation that keeps everyone simultaneously teaching and
   learning. Decentralisation only pays off when paired with an explicit
   [communication forcing function that pulls the periphery back across
   organisational boundaries](communication-forcing-functions-for-distributed-authority.md) —
   without one, delegated authority just relocates the coordination failure
   rather than removing it.
4. **Organizational learning**: incremental trial-and-error learning,
   deliberately courting small, contained dangers to forestall large ones,
   and simulation where real failure is too costly to risk.

Weick and Sutcliffe's widely cited restatement organizes five principles
into [anticipation versus containment](anticipation-vs-containment.md):

- **Anticipation:** [preoccupation with failure](preoccupation-with-failure.md),
  [reluctance to simplify](reluctance-to-simplify.md), [sensitivity to
  operations](sensitivity-to-operations.md)
- **Containment:** [commitment to resilience](commitment-to-resilience.md),
  [deference to expertise](deference-to-expertise.md)

These enact [mindfulness in high-reliability organizing](mindfulness-in-high-reliability-organizing.md)
— a rich awareness of discriminatory detail — through routines that convert
concerns about failure, simplicity, operations, resilience, and expertise into
practice. Example HRO settings include power grid dispatch, air traffic
control, nuclear aircraft carriers, nuclear power plants, hospital emergency
departments, wildland firefighting crews, and accident investigation teams.

Weick and Sutcliffe benchmark on HROs "not because they 'have it right' but
because they struggle to get it right on a continuing basis." Reliability does
not mean absence of variation — "It takes mindful variety to ensure stable
high performance." Most reliability lapses are slow accretions of weak signals
rather than sudden events; the behavioral difference from other organizations
shows up in making [strong responses to weak signals](strong-response-to-weak-signals.md).

The theory's own warning about its first commitment: **success is dangerous**
— sustained good outcomes lead managers to credit their own procedures, narrow
perception, entrench "the one way we do things," and reduce tolerance for
dissenting views, which is exactly the mechanism [chronic
unease](chronic-unease.md) and [amalberti's ultra-safe
plateau](amalberti-safety-continuum.md) exist to counteract.

**The theory's blind spot**: "deference to expertise" assumes experts have
enough of a God's-eye view to intervene before things go wrong. In practice,
experts operate on the same uncertain, incomplete knowledge of [unruly
technology](unruly-technology.md) as anyone else, and a slow drift toward
the margin can look like a small, acceptable cost even to the most
experienced insider — omniscient real-time oversight is not something
expertise actually confers. As Weick put it, seeing what one believes and
failing to see what one has no belief-category for are two sides of the
same coin, so warnings of a genuinely unprecedented failure mode can fail to
register as warnings at all, regardless of how expert the person receiving
them is. Challenger and Columbia are read as failures of exactly this
commitment: non-technical managers made continuation decisions while expert
input was unavailable or subordinated to schedule pressure at the meetings
that mattered.
