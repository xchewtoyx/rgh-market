---
type: concept
title: Confirmation-Seeking in Expectations
description: >
  Expectations function as hypotheses that steer attention toward confirming
  evidence and away from disconfirming cues — creating blind spots that let
  small errors incubate until a brutal audit.
sources:
  - title: Managing the Unexpected
    resource: "Managing the Unexpected (Weick, Sutcliffe), ch. 3"
---

Expectations are built into organizational roles, routines, and strategies;
they create orderliness but also blind spots — belated recognition of
threatening unexpected events. An expectation is envisioning something
reasonably certain to happen; holding one is functionally like testing a
hypothesis. Expectations direct attention, shaping what you notice, mull
over, and remember.

People are "awfully generous in what we accept as evidence that our
expectations are confirmed," actively seeking confirming evidence while
avoiding disconfirming evidence. Under pressure, both tendencies strengthen.
The same trap applies to routines and plans: activating a routine assumes
"the world today is pretty much like the world that existed at the time the
routine was first learned," and people see "more and more confirmation based
on fewer and fewer data" over time. "A preoccupation with plans and planning
makes it tough for you to act mindfully."

[High-reliability organizations](high-reliability-organizations.md) counteract
confirmation-seeking through the five principles; they "doubt those
expectations that seem to be confirmed most often." Landing-signal officers
assume pilots are coolheaded yet intentionally listen for voice-quality cues
suggesting a nighttime recovery is going sour — "they fight their own
expectations."

Three forms of the unexpected:

1. **An expected event fails to occur** — e.g., fatigued carrier crews
   sometimes perform better as a unit than fresh strangers at shift start.
2. **An unexpected event does occur** — e.g., several bolters in one squadron
   on one day signals system error, not pilot error.
3. **A simply unthought-of event happens** — e.g., first women pilots on
   all-male carrier crews; 9/11 Commission on failure of bureaucratic
   imagination.

The maxim "Expect the unexpected" is a hollow maxim — people live as if
expectations are basically correct because otherwise they'd forgo control.
[Mindfulness in high-reliability organizing](mindfulness-in-high-reliability-organizing.md)
— "a rich awareness of discriminatory detail" — is the counter.

Carriers instituted foreign-object damage (FOD) walkdowns after a stray bolt
was ingested into a jet engine — a disconfirmed expectation ("no big deal")
became learning the hard way. Carry expectations lightly: rigid expectations
create blind spots; weaker expectations give less guidance and impose
greater demands on attention.

See [anomalizing versus normalizing](anomalizing-vs-normalizing.md) for what
happens when disconfirming evidence gets relabeled as normal rather than
investigated.
