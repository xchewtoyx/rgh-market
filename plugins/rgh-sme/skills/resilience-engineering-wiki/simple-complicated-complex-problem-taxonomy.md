title: Simple, Complicated, and Complex Problems (Zimmerman & Glouberman)
description: >
  A three-way taxonomy of problem types — simple, complicated, complex —
  used to explain why a technique that works for one kind of problem
  (a recipe, a checklist) fails or even backfires on another.
sources:
  - title: "The Checklist Manifesto: How to Get Things Right"
    resource: "The Checklist Manifesto (Gawande), ch. 3"
---

Brenda Zimmerman and Sholom Glouberman's taxonomy sorts problems by how much
past success predicts future success on the *next* instance:

- **Simple problems**: governed by a repeatable recipe. Following the same
  steps reliably yields the same result (baking a cake, inserting a central
  line). Mastering the technique once is close to mastering it for good.
- **Complicated problems**: decompose into many simple sub-problems, but
  coordinating them requires specialized expertise and careful timing —
  unanticipated issues are common even though each part is individually
  well understood (sending a rocket to the moon, building a skyscraper).
  Success at one is a strong guide to success at the next, but no longer a
  guarantee.
- **Complex problems**: each instance is genuinely unique and uncertain;
  expertise is necessary but not sufficient, because the problem itself
  changes character as it unfolds and demands real-time adaptive judgment
  (raising a child, managing an unstable trauma patient).

The taxonomy's point is prescriptive, not just descriptive: a technique
matched to one tier fails, or actively misleads, on another. A checklist
that works beautifully on a simple problem (a fixed recipe of steps) cannot
be stretched into a script for a complex one — the complex case needs
judgment and adaptive coordination, not a longer list of steps to follow
(see [procedures as resources for
action](procedures-as-resources-for-action.md)). Conversely, treating a complicated problem as if it were merely simple —
assuming one master expert can hold the whole thing in their head — misreads
the tier of difficulty just as badly: modern work has largely outgrown the
single-expert model for complicated problems, but the right response has to
match the actual tier in front of you, not default either to more rigid
procedure or to unstructured improvisation.

This is a problem-level companion to [complicated systems become complex
once fielded](complicated-vs-complex-systems.md): that note describes how a
single *artefact* migrates from one tier to the other once it meets an open
environment; this taxonomy describes three different *kinds of task* an
organisation might be facing at any given moment, and warns against
misdiagnosing which one is actually in front of you.
