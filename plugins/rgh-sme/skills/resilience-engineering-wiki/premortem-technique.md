---
type: concept
title: The Premortem Technique
description: >
  Ask a team to imagine the plan has already failed and explain why, rather
  than asking them to critique the plan directly — reframing scrutiny as a
  creativity challenge breaks the overconfidence that vested authors bring
  to reviewing their own plan.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power (Klein), ch. 5"
  - title: "Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts"
    resource: "Thinking in Bets (Duke), ch. 6"
---

People become overconfident in a plan once they have finished constructing
it — the same [mental simulation](recognition-primed-decision-model.md) that
built the plan now works to defend it, absorbing objections rather than
weighing them. Asking the plan's own authors to review it for flaws
therefore produces only half-hearted scrutiny: they are emotionally
invested in the plan succeeding, and a request to "find problems with your
plan" is heard as a challenge to their competence.

The premortem reframes the task instead of asking for more effort at the
same task: imagine it is months in the future, the plan has been carried
out, **and it has failed** — participants must now explain why, generating
causes that would justify "of course it wasn't going to work, because...".
This converts the exercise from defending a decision already made into a
creativity and expertise challenge (who can find the smartest failure
mode), which breaks the emotional attachment that blocked honest review
under the direct-critique framing. In practice the imagined-failure
generation itself takes well under ten minutes, though the discussion that
follows can run much longer, and the technique measurably reduces
unwarranted confidence in the original plan.

The premortem is a structural countermeasure to exactly the overconfidence
that [risk homeostasis](risk-homeostasis.md) and [normalisation of
deviance](normalization-of-deviance.md) both feed on: a plan that has never
been mentally run to failure has had no opportunity to surface the
[garden-path](garden-path-fallacy-and-snap-back.md) assumptions baked into
it. It is most useful exactly where it is least likely to be requested
voluntarily — at the kickoff of a new project or a significant change,
before the plan has accumulated the sunk-cost investment that later makes
honest review even harder.

The premortem's mirror image is **backcasting**: imagine the goal already
*achieved* and work backward to identify the path that got there, which
Gary Klein calls "prospective hindsight" and which measurably improves
identification of the actual causal factors versus planning forward from
the present. Backcasting alone systematically over-represents the positive
branch, though — Gabriele Oettingen's "mental contrasting" research found
that vividly imagining only a successful outcome consistently produces
*worse* real-world results (across weight loss, job seeking, and exam
preparation) than pairing that vision with the obstacles standing in its
way. Because a plan's possible futures are a fixed pool of probability, a
premortem run immediately after a backcasting exercise corrects this
optimism skew directly: it forces the imagined negative-space branches back
into view before the team commits, rather than leaving them to surface only
in [hindsight](hindsight-bias.md) once the plan has already failed.
