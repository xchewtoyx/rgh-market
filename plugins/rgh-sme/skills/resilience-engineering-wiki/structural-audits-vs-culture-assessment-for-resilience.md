type: concept
title: Structural Audits vs Culture Assessment for Resilience
description: >
  Formal management-system audits and informal safety-culture assessments
  measure different halves of organisational resilience and neither
  substitutes for the other — audits anchor resilience in structure that
  outlasts any one team, but are structurally blind to exactly the criteria
  culture assessment exists to catch.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts (Hollnagel, Woods, Leveson), ch. 18"
---

An evaluation of whether formal management-system audits can measure
organisational resilience (using ARAMIS, a bowtie-based audit model built
for major-hazard chemical plants under the Seveso Directives) against
[Woods's eight criteria of organisational
resilience](eight-criteria-of-organizational-resilience.md) reaches a split
verdict: audits are necessary but not sufficient, and the gap is
structural, not a matter of writing a better audit checklist.

**Why structural anchoring matters.** Resilience needs to be anchored in
the formal structure of management systems — the thing audits actually
measure — to endure over time. Culture-assessment instruments measure
shared attitudes and beliefs operating largely in the informal sphere;
without a structural anchor, culture can drift unremarked, and good
performance can erode silently underneath attitudes that still look fine on
a climate survey. This is why a complete resilience-measurement framework
needs both traditions combined, not either alone.

**Where structural audits map cleanly onto the eight criteria, and where
they cannot reach at all.** Mapped against Woods's eight criteria across
four audit themes (clear risk picture, performance monitoring and learning,
continuing commitment, communication and coordination), structural audits
are strong exactly where there is a formal structure to inspect — a bowtie
diagram linking every barrier to the management system responsible for
maintaining it, explicit rule-modification procedures, formal safety cases
required before organisational changes. They are **structurally weak at
three specific criteria**: freedom from complacency (criterion 2),
systemic organisational learning (criterion 4), and adaptive flexibility in
genuinely unexpected situations (criterion 6) — because none of these are
properties a formal document or checklist can directly represent; they live
in attitude, disposition, and behaviour under conditions an audit
necessarily inspects after the fact, not in real time. Commitment versus
conflict-resolution shows the same split within a single criterion:
whether individuals comply with procedures is auditable, but management's
actual trade-off behaviour between safety and production/cost targets is
diffuse enough that structural audits are weak there too, and need
supplementing from safety-culture and climate instruments specifically.

**Why major accidents keep happening in heavily audited industries.**
Major disasters (Columbia, Challenger, Piper Alpha, Bhopal) rarely trace
back to an unknown, un-audited scenario — the bowtie diagram usually
already listed the relevant threat and barrier. What failed was that
barrier functioning and management's actual commitment to monitoring it had
degraded or gone unexamined, which is exactly the kind of drift a
structural audit checking "does the documented barrier exist" cannot catch,
because the barrier still existed on paper throughout.

**Auditor quality is itself a load-bearing, unauditable variable.** Whether
an audit surfaces anything real depends entirely on the auditors' domain
knowledge, independence, and critical competence in judging whether a
company's stated barrier rationale is actually valid — an audit conducted
by auditors without that competence produces a structurally complete but
substantively empty report. And even a fully accurate audit only identifies
off-specification indicators and eroded barriers; it cannot itself prescribe
how to execute the organisational culture change or political transformation
its findings imply is needed — diagnosis and remedy are separate
capabilities, and a resilience-auditing programme that stops at diagnosis
has done only half the job.
