Commitment to resilience is the fourth [high-reliability
organization](high-reliability-organizations.md) principle and the first
**containment** practice (with [deference to
expertise](deference-to-expertise.md)). "Human fallibility is like gravity,
weather, and terrain, just another foreseeable hazard" — if errors are
inevitable, managers should be as concerned with cures as with prevention.

Resilience is "the capability of a system to maintain its function and
structure in the face of internal and external changes and to degrade
gracefully when it must." Resilience is a form of control: a resilient
organization does not lose control of what it does but is able to continue
and rebound. The hallmark of an HRO is not error-free operation but that
errors don't disable it.

Aaron Wildavsky: "The mode of resilience is based on the assumption that
unexpected trouble is ubiquitous and unpredictable; and thus accurate advance
information on how to get out of it is in short supply." Resilient people
think mitigation rather than anticipation, attentive to expanding general
knowledge and resources that relieve surprises.

**Three abilities:**

1. **Absorb strain** and preserve functioning despite adversity.
2. **Recover or bounce back** — as stretch capacity grows, [brutal
   audit](brutal-audit.md) severity decreases.
3. **Learn and grow** from previous resilient action.

At Cerro Grande, resilience was depleted: crews worked close to 30 hours
against a 16-on/8-off norm; a "nondynamic crew was facing a dynamic
environment." Fresh resources arrived late Friday morning while exhausted
firefighters coped with spot fires.

**FedEx sweep network** illustrates resilience thinking: aircraft depart
60% loaded, leaving 40% capacity to recover at-risk cargo; GOC applies a
30-minute ground-time rule and a 1:30 a.m. hard deadline — "act while
thinking" rather than "think then act."

At Diablo Canyon, resilience shows in: (1) conviction that formal procedures
are fallible; (2) training in mental simulation of plant operations
unraveling; (3) **conceptual slack** — divergent analytical perspectives,
willingness to question rather than feign understanding, respectful
interaction to enrich information exchange.

HRO managers take pride in successful firefighting as evidence of resilience;
most business managers regard firefighting as distraction from "real work."
Resilient systems bear marks of the unexpected "not in the form of more
elaborate defenses but in the form of more elaborate response capabilities."

Plans create mindlessness in three ways ([anticipation versus
containment](anticipation-vs-containment.md)): they encode irrelevant
stimuli out of view; they specify contingent actions that preclude
improvisation; they presume repeating past-successful patterns handles novel
events. Managing the unexpected requires stable sensing and variable response.

Don't overdo lean ideals — leanness strips resilience; improve it via ad hoc,
self-organizing networks of knowledgeable people. Accelerate feedback so
improvisation can be detected and altered if things worsen.

A **limited action repertoire** is also a major source of limited perception:
people who cannot act on what they see impose old categories on new problems
just so they can respond — the mindlessness vulnerability Audit 5.2 of
[conscious mindfulness audits](conscious-mindfulness-audits.md) probes.
Expanding knowledge and technical capability improves both seeing problems
and dealing with them; even unexercised resilience capability aids diagnosis
of unwarranted simplifications and cumulative error trends.
