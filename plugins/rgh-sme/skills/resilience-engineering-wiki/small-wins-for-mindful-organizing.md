title: Small Wins for Mindful Organizing
description: >
  HRO principles are raised through concrete, complete outcomes of moderate
  importance — opportunistic cumulative changes at controllable sites, not
  by shutting the organization down or decomposing one big redesign into steps.
sources:
  - title: Managing the Unexpected
    resource: "Managing the Unexpected (Weick, Sutcliffe), ch. 8"
---

You cannot shut an organization down to redesign it for [mindfulness in
high-reliability organizing](mindfulness-in-high-reliability-organizing.md).
The five HRO principles must rise in priority through **small wins** — formally,
"a concrete, complete, implemented outcome of moderate importance."

A small-wins strategy produces change without confronting the system directly
or aggressively. Small wins test implicit theories about resistance and
opportunity, promote learning, and lower resistance to later proposals. This
is **not** decomposing one large task into sequential sub-steps: small wins
are *opportunistic*, often noteworthy because they move away from bad
conditions rather than because they are logical steps toward a predefined
goal. A steady march toward a fixed target assumes a stable environment —
not what HROs face. Accumulating small wins also uncovers achievable goals
not previously envisioned.

Small wins are controllable because they depend mostly on the actor's own
actions. Impact spreads through tangible examples others imitate, allies
attracted and opponents deterred, visible doing, and a context where change
now seems possible.

Managers attuned to small wins watch **sites** where principles can shift:
questions frequently or never asked, what is first and last on meeting
agendas, what is important enough to call a meeting for, and what gets
followed up versus forgotten. Your own managerial style is the most
immediate, influential, and controllable lever — you need not wait for slow
culture change.

Wildland firefighting after [Cerro Grande](preoccupation-with-failure.md)
illustrates the pattern. Agencies mostly spent on more equipment, staff, and
models — "easy changes" doing the same things in a more hostile environment,
not progress toward mindfulness. Genuine outcroppings included: Karl
Brauneis restoring sequence to the Ten Standard Fire Orders ([reluctance to
simplify](reluctance-to-simplify.md)); interagency relationship networks
reducing silo simplification; the Hawkins wildfire case as a vicarious
example for "what would I have done?"; [staff rides for safety
learning](staff-rides-for-safety-learning.md); After Action Reviews; and
peer-review near-miss reports probing discriminatory detail under [just
culture](just-culture.md). Despite these pieces, the authors judge the fire
community still lacks a culture that binds all five principles together —
[safety culture change](safety-culture-is-what-an-organization-does.md) is a
"two-sided coin" requiring both bottom-up practice change and top-down
leadership demonstration, not a magic bullet or single directive.
