---
type: concept
title: Fallacy of Centrality
description: >
  The mistaken belief that because one occupies a central, important role one
  must already know what others see — which stops subordinates from passing
  information upward and leaves the central figure less informed than assumed.
sources:
  - title: Managing the Unexpected
    resource: "Managing the Unexpected (Weick, Sutcliffe), ch. 8"
---

Ron Westrum coined the fallacy of centrality from observing 1940s–50s
pediatricians who failed to diagnose child abuse under the reasoning: "If
parents were abusing their children, I'd know about it; since I don't know
about it, it isn't happening." People entrusted with dangerous technologies
can feel self-important because they are constantly told they are important
— true in part, but not evidence they are all-knowing.

The danger is bilateral: if others assume you're all-knowing, they won't
bother telling you what they know, treating their own observations as
redundant. Self-important people know less than they think, are less curious
than they need to be, and are more vulnerable to surprise.

At Cerro Grande, Paul Gleason's associates assumed he already knew the fire
was unusually volatile (though Gleason himself did not commit the fallacy)
and so did not speak up, leaving him without information they held. This
interacts with [deference to expertise](deference-to-expertise.md): too much
deference to a legendary expert can produce the opposite of the intended
effect — others slacken monitoring, assuming "if Gleason doesn't see it, it's
not happening."

The fallacy connects to [dangerous silence in high-risk
systems](dangerous-silence-in-high-risk-systems.md): higher-ups in position
to listen are often blind to the silencing effects of their presence.
Remedy: treat speaking up as institutional and systemic, not a test of
individual backbone; make knowledge transparent and widely known so problems
in the making are noticed faster.
