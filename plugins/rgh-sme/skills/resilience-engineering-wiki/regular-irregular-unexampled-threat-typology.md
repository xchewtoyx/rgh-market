title: Regular, Irregular, and Unexampled Threats
description: >
  A three-tier typology of threats by how much prior pattern exists to
  respond from — routine, one-off but conceivable, and beyond anyone's
  collective experience — because each tier calls for a structurally
  different kind of preparedness, not just "more" of the same readiness.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts (Hollnagel, Woods, Leveson), ch. 5"
---

Threats to a system's integrity can be classified along three axes —
**predictability** (does the threat recur often enough to recognise a
pattern), **disruption potential** (how much of the system's overall
integrity is at stake), and **origin** (internal, requiring the system's
own quality controls and safeguards, versus external, requiring outward-
facing defence). Crossing predictability and disruption produces three
qualitatively distinct situations, each demanding a different kind of
organisational preparedness:

1. **The regular threat.** Recurs often enough that a standardised response
   algorithm can be formulated and rehearsed in advance: contained,
   low-disruption internal events like routine medication errors, or
   predictable external disturbances like a hospital's response to
   recurring local violence, handled through pre-planned resource
   reallocation. The system's job here is executing a known routine well.
2. **The irregular threat.** A low-probability, high-consequence "one-off"
   for which no pre-formulated algorithm can be written in advance, because
   the space of possible catastrophic scenarios is too large to enumerate —
   Apollo 13's oxygen-tank explosion, or a combat hospital suddenly
   overloaded by a mass-casualty attack. What handles an irregular threat
   is not a bigger playbook but organisational flexibility: self-
   organisation, real-time improvisation, and rapid resource reallocation
   under genuine crisis conditions — see [generic competencies for
   escalating situations](generic-competencies-for-escalation.md) for what
   that flexibility concretely consists of.
3. **The unexampled event.** Pushes responders entirely beyond their
   collective prior experience — 9/11, Chernobyl, the AIDS pandemic's early
   years — such that neither a standard routine nor an ordinary scale-up of
   improvisation is adequate; a fundamentally new mental framework for
   understanding the situation is required before an effective response is
   even conceivable. Success here depends on baseline organisational
   qualities built up long before the event: the capacity to monitor
   broadly, self-organise without instruction, and formulate genuinely
   novel response strategies rather than adapted old ones.

**Risk-stacking makes the boundary between irregular and unexampled
porous.** The Senegalese ferry *Le Joola* (2002, over 1,800 deaths,
exceeding the *Titanic*) shows how an event crosses from "should have been
irregular" into "effectively unexampled": no single factor — loading to
three times design capacity, an unmonitored coast guard radio overnight,
bureaucratic corruption, severely delayed rescue — was individually beyond
the system's collective experience, but their combination produced a
situation nobody had actually prepared a response envelope for, the same
mechanism [choke-point detection](choke-point-detection.md) names for
individually-tolerable factors compounding into an unrecognised whole.

**The practical design implication**: preparedness investment aimed at one
tier does not transfer automatically to the next. Building a better
regular-threat playbook does nothing for an irregular threat, which needs
flexibility instead of a bigger rulebook; and building flexibility for
irregular threats does not by itself prepare an organisation for an
unexampled event, which needs the capacity to reconceive the situation
entirely rather than improvise faster within an existing frame. An
organisation auditing its own readiness has to ask which tier a given
scenario actually belongs to before asking whether it is prepared, because
"prepared" means something structurally different at each tier.
