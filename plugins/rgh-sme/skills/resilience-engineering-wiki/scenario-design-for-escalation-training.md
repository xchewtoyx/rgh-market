title: Scenario Design for Escalation Training
description: >
  Training scenarios that deliberately reproduce escalation's real dynamics
  — cascading, cross-boundary, unfolding over time — and specifically bait
  the cognitive traps a team needs practice resisting, rather than
  rehearsing a single well-defined problem to a known procedure.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering in Practice"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering in Practice (Hollnagel, Pariès, Wreathall), ch. 4"
---

Training [generic competencies for escalating
situations](generic-competencies-for-escalation.md) requires scenarios built
to reproduce escalation's actual shape, not a hard version of an ordinary
bounded problem. Three basic properties a training scenario needs to have
before it can teach anything about escalation at all: the monitored process
must genuinely **cascade**, demanding rising cognitive effort from
participants rather than staying at one difficulty level; the cascade must
**cross boundaries** of responsibility, forcing coordinated multi-role
response rather than something one participant could solve alone; and it
must **unfold dynamically over time**, rather than being presented as a
static problem set with all the relevant information available at once.

On top of those baseline properties, specific design choices deliberately
target the failure modes escalation is known to produce:

- **Role routine breakdowns** — include a problem genuinely unsolvable
  within established procedures, so participants are forced out of
  prescribed-role behaviour rather than able to solve the scenario by
  correctly executing a checklist.
- **Hidden goals** — embed a de-escalation path or escape route that stays
  latent unless the team explicitly articulates and vocalises it, testing
  whether the team's communication surfaces options that exist but are not
  handed to them.
- **Proactive impact decisions** — introduce a high-consequence choice
  whose outcome is genuinely hard to foresee, forcing prospective reasoning
  and explicit expectation-setting rather than reactive, outcome-driven
  play.
- **Garden-pathing traps** — open with strong cues matching a familiar,
  well-known scenario, then quietly depart from it with weaker,
  easy-to-dismiss cues, testing directly whether the team gets caught in
  [the garden path fallacy](garden-path-fallacy-and-snap-back.md) — a false
  early diagnosis that survives past the point it should have been
  abandoned.
- **Thematic vagabonding risks** — generate genuine cognitive noise
  (frequent warnings, competing events) to test whether the team fragments
  its problem-solving by chasing every new data point, the team-scale
  version of [cognitive fixation and thematic
  vagabonding](cognitive-fixation.md).

The common design logic is that a scenario only trains what it actually
exercises: a scenario solvable by correctly applying a known procedure
trains procedure-following, not the generic competencies escalation
requires, however dramatic the scenario looks on the surface. Each of the
five criteria above exists to make procedure-following specifically
insufficient, so that the competencies actually being tested are the ones
[the irony of resilience](irony-of-resilience.md) identifies as eroded by
standard, prescriptive training in the first place.

A separate design choice, orthogonal to which of the five properties a
scenario has, is *which domain* the scenario is drawn from: running the
scenario [outside trainees' own professional
domain](out-of-domain-simulation-for-resilience-training.md) removes the
ego and prestige stakes that otherwise make a poor performance read as a
threat to professional identity rather than as training data.
