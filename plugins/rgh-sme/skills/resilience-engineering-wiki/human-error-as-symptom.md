In the [New View](old-view-vs-new-view-of-human-error.md), "human error" is
not an explanation; it is something that itself requires explanation. Error
is systematically connected to features of people's tools, tasks, and
operating context — it is the visible symptom of trouble deeper inside the
system. An investigation that concludes with "operator error" has stopped at
its own starting line.

The founding demonstration is Fitts & Jones (1947) — the origin of the
scare quotes around 'human error'. Analysing 460 "pilot-error" experiences,
they found nearly all Army Air Force pilots, regardless of experience, made
the same control errors (retracting flaps instead of gear under heavy
workload), because errors were systematically produced by poor cockpit
layout and non-standardised switch locations — not incompetence. They called
the events "experiences", not "failures", and fixed the cockpit rather than
the pilots. Hollnagel & Amalberti (2001) added the observer's half: watching
live ATC operations, psychologists sorted "errors" into cognitive mechanisms
while the controllers themselves explained the same acts as deliberate,
expert strategies for managing operational constraints and preserving safety
margins — markers of the resilience that
[Safety-II](safety-i-and-safety-ii.md) studies. "Error" was in the eye of
the observer.

Operational tests for whether you are treating error as symptom or cause:

- Does the finding name a *what* (confusing interface, conflicting goals,
  missing information, time pressure) or only a *who*?
- Would the countermeasure still work if a different, equally competent person
  had been on shift? If not, the analysis rediscovered the person, not the
  problem (compare the [accident-proneness
  myth](accident-proneness-myth.md)).
- Can you answer [why it made sense](local-rationality-principle.md) for the
  person to act as they did? If not, context is still missing.

Stopping at the error also truncates causal search in the way the
[chain-of-events model](chain-of-events-model.md) encourages — the human is a
convenient "chain start". The same truncation habit generalised is the
[root-cause fallacy](root-cause-fallacy.md): picking any single stopping
point and calling it the cause.

[Threat and Error Management](threat-and-error-management.md) builds this
same reframing into a measurement framework: rather than counting errors in
isolation, it treats professionalism as the management of context and the
recovery of performance within acceptable boundaries, since a raw error
count cannot distinguish a caught, recovered deviation from an uncaught one.
