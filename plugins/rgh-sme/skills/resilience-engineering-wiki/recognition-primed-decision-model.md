---
type: concept
title: Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) Model
description: >
  Experienced practitioners under time pressure do not compare options —
  they recognise a situation as an instance of a familiar pattern, which
  supplies a workable action directly, and check that action with mental
  simulation rather than against alternatives.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power (Klein), ch. 3"
---

Gary Klein's studies of fireground commanders set out to find how experts
compare decision options under time pressure and found, in 80% of the
non-routine, high-stakes decisions studied, that they never compared options
at all. An experienced commander looking at a scene recognises it as an
instance of a **prototype** built from years of exposure, and recognising
the situation supplies four things simultaneously: plausible **goals**
(what matters here), relevant **cues** (what to pay attention to, avoiding
information overload), **expectancies** (what should happen next, so a
violated expectation is noticed as a surprise), and a typical **action**.
The commander is not deliberating and then acting; recognising the
situation and knowing what to do are the same event.

This is **satisficing** (Herbert Simon): take the first option that will
work, rather than **optimising**: search for the provably best one. Klein
names the resulting style **singular evaluation** — options, if more than
one is needed, are considered one at a time and checked via **mental
simulation** (imagining how the action would play out) until one survives,
rather than **comparative evaluation** — weighing two or more options
side by side on shared dimensions, the pattern classical decision theory
assumes. Comparative evaluation did appear in the data, but disproportionately
in exactly the situations where experience was absent (a fire far larger and
stranger than anything any responder had ever handled): deliberate
comparison is what substitutes for recognition when recognition has nothing
to draw on, not a superior method experts forgo under time pressure.

The finding inverts the expected novice/expert pattern: novices are the ones
who need to generate and compare several options, precisely because they
lack the experience base that would let one option present itself as
obviously right; experts more often generate a single workable course of
action and refine it. This is also why classical rational-choice
prescriptions — canvass a wide option set, weight every dimension, score
every option — are better training for novices than for the experienced
practitioners who are actually placed in high-stakes, time-pressured roles:
the prescription assumes a decision-maker without the very pattern library
that makes expert performance fast. See [the rational-choice explanation of
failure](rational-choice-explanation-of-failure.md) and [naturalistic
decision-making conditions](naturalistic-decision-making-conditions.md) for
why judging an RPD-style decision against a rational-choice standard
after the fact is judging it against a process the decision-maker had no
reason, and often no time, to use.

Mental simulation is not foolproof — a rescuer can mentally check an
improvised rig for the failure modes he can imagine and still be defeated by
one he cannot (a harness sized for a bulky, coated firefighter sliding off a
slender, unconscious victim) — but it is generally more reliable than any
alternative available under the same time constraint. RPD's practical
implication for training experienced responders is to build the pattern
library itself (many varied scenarios, including rare ones) rather than
drilling formal comparative-analysis technique, which does not build the
recognitional skill that actually drives expert performance.
