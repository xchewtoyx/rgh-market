title: Safety Culture Is Something an Organization Does, Not Has

  The interpretivist view treats culture as emerging from daily interactions

  culture as an attitude-object management can own, measure, and campaign

Two incompatible views of what a safety culture is:

| Interpretivist (sociology/anthropology) | Functionalist (management/psychology) |
| :-- | :-- |
| Culture is something an organization **does** | Culture is something an organization **has** |
| Emerges from ongoing human interactions | Reduced to individual attitudes and behaviours |
| Influenced only through daily interaction | Controlled top-down via policies and campaigns |
| Evaluated qualitatively (observation, discussion) | Measured quantitatively (surveys, scorecards) |
| Emic — inside-out perspective | Etic — outside-in perspective |
| Assumes diversity of operational viewpoints | Demands homogeneity ("Vision Zero") |

The functionalist premise is a special case of [Newtonian-Cartesian
reductionism](newtonian-cartesian-accident-thinking.md): decompose the
whole into parts (individual attitudes), measure each part, and sum them
into a score, without ever establishing that culture actually adds up that
way. It underwrites most failed safety programmes: if culture is a
possession composed of worker attitudes, then posters, scorecards, behaviour
audits, and slogan campaigns look like levers —
[behaviour-based safety](behavior-based-safety.md) and [Vision
Zero](vision-zero.md) are its flagship products, and demanding attitude
homogeneity suppresses exactly the minority doubt that [chronic
unease](chronic-unease.md) depends on.

The interpretivist view relocates the work: culture changes through how the
organisation actually behaves, daily — what leaders ask about, how bad news
is received ([blame suppresses
reporting](blame-suppresses-reporting.md)), how [goal
conflicts](goal-conflicts-and-production-pressure.md) are settled in
practice, whether accounts after failure are met with [restoration or
retribution](just-culture.md). Survey scores measure at best the climate of
saying, not the culture of doing — which is another instance of the
[work-as-imagined vs
work-as-done](work-as-imagined-vs-work-as-done.md) gap. (Schein's classic
three-level model makes the same point structurally: visible artifacts and
espoused values sit atop the basic underlying assumptions that actually
drive behaviour — and surveys mostly reach the top two levels.)

The change mechanism follows: John Shook, reflecting on the NUMMI
transformation — *"the way to change culture is not to first change how
people think, but to start by changing how people behave — what they do."*
In the Accelerate research this is the observed DevOps mechanism: adopting
concrete technical and management practices changes daily work patterns,
and sustained practice moves organisations toward a [generative Westrum
culture](westrum-typology.md), which in turn predicts performance. Culture
is the residue of practiced behaviour, not a prerequisite to be installed
first.

Weick and Sutcliffe align with the interpretivist view: "we think culture is
something an organization has that eventually becomes something an
organization is. Organizations act their way into what they become." Culture
change can start with symbols and artifacts (easy to change) and work backward
through values to assumptions (hardest). Edgar Schein's counsel: never start
with changing culture — start with a specific business issue, then ask
whether culture aids or hinders it. See [informed
culture](informed-culture.md) for Reason's content model and [mindfulness
in high-reliability organizing](mindfulness-in-high-reliability-organizing.md)
for the practices that institutionalize wariness.

Culture in this sense is deliberately organisation-wide and slow-moving.
Reason (2009) names the faster, local counterpart **climate** — see [error-
likely climate versus safety culture](error-likely-climate-vs-safety-culture.md)
for why a site can carry an acutely dangerous local climate while sitting
inside an otherwise healthy overall culture, and why detecting the two
requires different mechanisms operating at different speeds.

A 1998 wildland firefighter safety report on transforming culture describes
change as a **two-sided coin**: bottom-up changes from those doing the work
and top-down direction and sanction from management — both essential.
"Cultural change is not triggered by a magic bullet or a directive. Rather,
culture is changed by a series of small steps taken by the leading members
of the culture at all levels. Leadership is standing up and leading the way.
It is behavior and it is demonstrable. It is showing, not telling." Nearly
a decade after Cerro Grande, Weick and Sutcliffe judged the wildland fire
community still lacked a culture binding all five HRO principles together
despite individual [small wins for mindful
organizing](small-wins-for-mindful-organizing.md) — illustrating that
interpretivist culture change is slow, partial, and requires both sides of
the coin, not a single campaign.
