---
type: concept
title: Westrum's Three Temporal Horizons of Resilience
description: >
  Resilience shows up as three distinct capabilities relative to when a
  threat occurs — foreseeing and avoiding it, coping while it unfolds, and
  repairing after it — and possessing one does not guarantee the others.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts (Hollnagel, Woods, Leveson), ch. 5"
---

Westrum sorts resilience by its temporal relationship to a threat, rather
than by the continuous abilities a system exercises all the time (contrast
[the four abilities of resilient
performance](four-abilities-of-resilient-performance.md), which names what a
system must keep doing simultaneously; this framework instead names three
separable competencies a system can hold in different amounts). A system
strong in one horizon is not thereby strong in the others — each is trained,
resourced, and can fail independently.

1. **Foresee and avoid (proactive resilience)** — anticipating and
   preventing a bad event from ever materialising. Requires **requisite
   imagination**: the capacity to build a "wily organisation" that actively
   resists groupthink rather than one that only recognises hazards already
   in its historical record (the same term [resilience as
   control](resilience-as-control.md) names under "lack of knowledge").
   Foresight draws on two distinct sources: experiential memory — actively
   retaining and standardising past lessons rather than letting them decay
   (a set of US veterans' hospitals cut mortality up to 40% simply by
   *reinstating* forgotten standard cardiac and pneumonia treatments) — and
   faint-signal processing, catching weak trends and gut-level anomalies
   before they compound. Failure here looks like groupthink, fortress
   mentality, and encapsulation: NASA's Mission Management Team repeatedly
   declined engineers' requests for satellite imaging of Columbia's tile
   damage during its final mission.
2. **Coping with ongoing trouble (concurrent / adaptive resilience)** —
   preventing a crisis already underway from escalating further. This draws
   on dynamic flexibility, adaptive learning, and slack resources, not
   static toughness. Its characteristic failure is operational rigidity and
   departmentalisation surviving past their usefulness: Union cavalry that
   won battles with repeating rifles had them confiscated post-war by a
   bureaucracy optimising for ammunition conservation; British forces held
   back an anti-aircraft gun that could have stopped German tanks because
   deploying it crossed departmental lines the Germans had no equivalent
   barrier to crossing. Communication breakdown between agencies compounds
   this directly — NYPD and FDNY's incompatible radios and absent unified
   command on 9/11 turned a coordination gap into unnecessary on-scene
   fatalities.
3. **Repairing after catastrophe (reactive / recovery resilience)** —
   rebuilding operational capability after severe damage or a genuine
   surprise attack. The Israeli Air Force's mid-war reinvention of its
   doctrine, intelligence flows, and ground-support tactics during the 1973
   Yom Kippur War, in direct response to unexpected surface-to-air missile
   losses, is the studied positive case. Recovery is severely constrained
   when the command and decision centres themselves are destroyed (WTC local
   command on 9/11) or when the scale of the event simply overwhelms
   regional capacity regardless of how well any single actor responds (the
   2004 Indian Ocean tsunami).

The practical use of separating the three: a post-incident review that only
asks "did we cope well once trouble started" is silent on whether the event
should have been foreseen at all, or on whether recovery capacity actually
existed once the acute phase ended — three independent questions, not one.
