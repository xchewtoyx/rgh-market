title: Newtonian-Cartesian Accident Thinking
description: >
  The inherited worldview that systems decompose into parts connected by
  traceable, proportional cause-effect links — adequate for simple systems,
  and the reason chain-of-events and root-cause reasoning feel self-evident
  even when they mislead.
sources:
  - title: "Drift into Failure: From Hunting Broken Components to Understanding Complex Systems"

Dekker traces the [chain-of-events model](chain-of-events-model.md) and the
[root-cause fallacy](root-cause-fallacy.md) to a shared epistemological
ancestor: the vision of the physical world established by Newton and
Descartes. That vision assumes systems are **decomposable** — take them apart,
find the broken piece, and the whole is explained — and that cause and effect
are **linear and proportional**: small causes produce small effects, large
effects require large causes, and effects are in principle foreseeable and
calculable (risk-as-energy-to-be-contained, barriers, failure probabilities).

This worldview is not simply wrong; it is a good approximation for genuinely
simple, decomposable systems. The trap is treating it as the *only* available
lens and applying it by default to systems that are not decomposable — where
a huge number of interacting, diverse, connected parts produce outcomes that
cannot be located back in any single component's properties. In such systems,
analysis can indicate possibilities but cannot predict specific outcomes, no
matter how much a Newtonian risk assessment promises otherwise. This is the
conceptual gap [drift into failure](drift-into-failure.md) exploits: our
technology has become interconnected and adaptive faster than our theories of
failure have kept up, so investigators keep reaching for decomposable-system
tools (a broken part, a root cause, a weakest link) to explain accidents that
were never produced by a broken part at all.

Recognising which worldview is in play is diagnostic: if an investigation's
first move is to ask "which component failed?" it has already assumed
decomposability, before any evidence is examined. The [systems-theoretic
accident model](systems-theoretic-accident-model.md) is the deliberate
alternative — dropping decomposability and treating safety as an emergent,
whole-system property.

The legacy runs deeper than the accident model itself, into the vocabulary
used to judge the people involved. Descartes' dualism — a mind separate from
the material world it perceives — is the direct ancestor of ["loss of
situation awareness"](complacency-and-loss-of-situation-awareness.md) as an
explanation: it pictures an objective reality "out there" against which a
practitioner's mental picture can be graded as an imperfect copy. Newton's
assumption that moral response should be proportional to outcome magnitude —
a bad effect implies a correspondingly blameworthy cause — is what makes a
severe [outcome](outcome-bias.md) pull for a severe verdict on the person
nearest to it, independent of how reasonable their actions were going in;
this is the reasoning [just culture](just-culture.md) has to work against.
Even the [Swiss cheese model](swiss-cheese-model.md), built around
containing hazardous energy behind layers of defence, inherits Newton's
energy-exchange framing rather than escaping it. Investigating [up and out
rather than down and in](go-up-and-out-not-down-and-in.md) is the deliberate
counter-move to all of these at once.

Dekker collects the worldview into a four-point **"Newtonian ethic of
failure"** that quietly governs investigation, management, and law alike:

1. To understand a failure, search for a broken component — the
   component-to-system relationship is treated as unproblematic (the [root
   cause fallacy](root-cause-fallacy.md)).
2. Effects cannot occur without causes, and larger effects imply more
   egregious causes — the proportionality assumption behind [outcome
   bias](outcome-bias.md) and disciplinary schemes that scale punishment to
   harm.
3. With enough effort, people can foresee outcomes, so foreseeable harm
   should have been avoided — the foreseeability logic underlying negligence
   law, which becomes circular once an accident is used as retroactive proof
   that the harm "was foreseeable all along."
4. A causal sequence can be validly reconstructed backward from outcome to
   cause, revealing a pre-existing order, and there is exactly **one true
   story** — the premise that lets a single investigative body claim
   institutional authority to be its arbiter, even though the same event
   supports genuinely contradictory readings (see the [root-cause
   fallacy](root-cause-fallacy.md) on causes as constructions).

This ethic persists not because anyone imposes it top-down but because
institutions, incident-report templates, and legal procedure all expect
people to explain failure in these terms, which reproduces the assumptions
in the explaining. The same reductionist move — decompose the whole into
parts and sum their state to explain it — is what the functionalist view of
[safety culture](safety-culture-is-what-an-organization-does.md) does to
culture: treating it as individual attitudes that can be surveyed and
summed into a score.
