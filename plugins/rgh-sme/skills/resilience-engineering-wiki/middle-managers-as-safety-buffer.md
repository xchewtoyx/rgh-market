type: concept
title: Middle Managers as the Safety Buffer Against Executive Production Pressure
description: >
  Organisational resilience at the managerial level depends on middle
  managers actively buffering top-down production pressure from senior
  leadership against the safety needs of the workforce — a role that fails
  through either failing to notice the threat or noticing it and failing to
  act on it.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts (Hollnagel, Woods, Leveson), ch. 14"
---

Organisational resilience, translated down to the level of an individual
manager's behaviour, is the capacity to handle severe conflict between
safety and production goals when it actually lands on their desk. A
resilient manager makes [sacrifice
judgements](sacrifice-judgements.md): recognising an encroaching threat to
safety and rebuffing or circumventing it while minimising disruption to
production. Managerial resilience breaks down in exactly one of two places
— either the manager fails to detect the threat at all, or detects it but
fails to act on what they detected — and distinguishing which failure
actually occurred matters, because the two call for different fixes
(better detection versus more support for acting on what is already seen).

**Flin's finer-grained breakdown names three non-technical skills**
underneath that detect/act split, and separates "acting on it" into two
distinct sub-skills that fail independently:

1. **Diagnosis** — the cognitive situation-awareness work of actually
   noticing operational drift toward a safety boundary and recognising when
   a tolerance limit has been breached. This is the "detect" half above; a
   manager can suffer outright "risk blindness" even when handed direct
   warnings.
2. **Decision-making** — once a threat is diagnosed, choosing and executing
   the sacrifice judgement itself: trading production or targets for
   safety. A surgeon abandoning minimally invasive laparoscopy for a full
   abdominal incision when the patient's risk profile shifts is the
   individual-practitioner version of the same skill a manager needs at
   organisational scale.
3. **Assertiveness** — persuading *senior* managers and peers that
   production must be halted or costs sacrificed, i.e. managing upward, not
   downward. Standard safety literature is built almost entirely around
   managing subordinates; the gap Flin identifies is that managerial
   resilience specifically requires challenging superior authority, which
   is a different and much less trained skill.

An **offshore rig de-weighting** case shows decision-making and
assertiveness operating together under real stakes: an offshore
installation manager, facing a severe storm, ordered expensive drilling mud
dumped overboard to raise the rig — a call that overrode onshore
management's cost concerns and that the manager himself described as "too
drastic for them but seemed to be the safest thing to do."

**Piper Alpha's Claymore platform supplies the sharpest documented failure
of assertiveness specifically**, distinct from the buffer-failure cases
below: on the night of the disaster, a production supervisor on the
neighbouring Claymore platform asked his own offshore installation manager
to shut down oil production — to stop feeding pipeline flow into the
burning Piper Alpha — six separate times, and was refused each time, out of
fear that a shutdown would cost several days to restart. The diagnosis was
correct and communicated repeatedly; what failed was the *supervisor's*
assertiveness ceiling meeting the *manager's* refusal to be moved by it —
a failure at the assertiveness/authority interface distinct from the
detection failures the cases below illustrate.

**Middle managers occupy a specific structural position**: a buffer between
senior leadership's production drive and the safety needs of the workforce,
plant, and environment underneath them. When that buffer holds, executive
production pressure gets absorbed and translated into locally sustainable
trade-offs before it reaches the sharp end. When it fails, production
pressure passes through unmodified, and safety margins erode directly under
executive demand with nothing in between to resist it.

**The *Vasa* (1628)** is the founding case for what buffer failure looks
like end to end. King Gustavus Adolphus, under military pressure and
provoked by news of a rival Danish warship, repeatedly demanded design
additions to an already-committed ship — a second gundeck, 64 heavy cannons,
elaborate ornamentation — while also compressing the schedule. Master
shipbuilder Hybbertson cautiously tried to dissuade the king, functioning as
the buffer the design needed, but royal authority prevailed. Hybbertson then
died before construction finished, and his successor held nowhere near the
same authority to push back — a **loss of expert buffer** distinct from
ordinary managerial failure, since the person capable of resisting the
pressure was simply no longer present in the role. A deck-stability test
months before launch (30 men running across the deck) produced violently
visible heeling and was halted mid-test — clear, direct evidence of
instability — yet the admiral running the trial did not dare delay the
publicly announced launch date for fear of contradicting the king. The
*Vasa* sailed on 10 August 1628, heeled over, and sank within minutes,
killing 50 of the 150 aboard.

**The same structural pattern recurs across modern disasters**, with
corporate boardrooms and government offices standing in for regal authority.
Before the *Challenger* launch, schedule and political pressure led senior
managers to override engineering concerns about O-ring fragility at low
temperature; a hesitating engineer was told to "take off his engineering hat
and put on his management hat" — a direct instruction to substitute a
production-goal identity for a safety-goal one at the exact decision point
that mattered (Vaughan, 1996). *Columbia* (2003) repeated the identical
managerial trade-off flaw under near-identical schedule incentives. At
*Piper Alpha* (1988), sustained production pressure and cost-cutting
combined with an absence of managerial resistance to produce 167 deaths.
Institutional failure to buffer can also take the form of actively
suppressing a manager who *did* successfully detect and escalate a threat:
anaesthetist Stephen Bolsin's warnings about paediatric cardiac mortality at
Bristol Royal Infirmary were thwarted by medical hierarchy and professional
culture for years before action followed — a case where the individual
buffer held, but the surrounding institution overrode it anyway, which is
part of what motivated the UK's Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998.

The consistent lesson across all these cases: managerial resilience is not a
personality trait some managers happen to have and others lack. It is
determined by whether the organisational structure around a manager
actually lets a sacrifice judgement survive contact with power above it —
Hybbertson tried and was overruled by a king, the Challenger engineer was
explicitly asked to stop trying, and Bolsin succeeded at detection and
escalation but was blocked anyway. Buffer failure is at least as often a
structural failure to support the buffering role as it is an individual
failure to attempt it.

Because managerial resilience is a structural property rather than a fixed
trait, it can be deliberately [measured](measuring-managerial-safety-commitment.md)
and [trained](training-managerial-resilience-skills.md) rather than simply
hoped for.
