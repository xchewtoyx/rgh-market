type: concept
title: Outcome Bias
description: >
  Judging the quality of a decision by the severity of its outcome rather
  than by the information available when it was made — including the illusion
  that bad outcomes must have had bad processes behind them.
sources:
  - title: The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error'
    resource: "The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error' (Dekker), ch. 2"
---

Outcome bias (Baron & Hershey, 1988): observers evaluate decision quality
based on how badly things turned out, not on the information available at the
time of the decision. The identical decision, made on identical grounds, is
rated sound when the outcome is good and negligent when it is bad.

Its structural companion is the **illusion of cause–consequence
equivalence** — the assumption that severe outcomes must be caused by
equally severe flaws. In complex systems this is false in both directions:
good processes can produce bad outcomes, and flawed or scrambling processes
(the rushed approach stabilised just in time) routinely produce successful
ones. This is another face of the [Safety-II](safety-i-and-safety-ii.md)
observation that success and failure flow from the same everyday performance
variability — the outcome, not the behaviour, is what differs.

Practical consequences:

- Never let outcome severity set the depth or tone of scrutiny of the
  *decision*; evaluate against what was known at the time ([local
  rationality](local-rationality-principle.md)).
- Disciplinary schemes that scale sanction with outcome severity punish luck,
  not conduct — one more way [just culture](just-culture.md) schemes drift
  into unfairness and [suppress reporting](blame-suppresses-reporting.md).
- Outcome bias operates alongside [hindsight bias](hindsight-bias.md): one
  inflates the *predictability* of the outcome, the other contaminates the
  *evaluation* of the decision with it.
