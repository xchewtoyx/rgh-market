  Weick and Sutcliffe's term for rich awareness of discriminatory detail —
  the cognitive infrastructure enacted through the five HRO principles that
  lets organizations notice weak signals before they become brutal audits.

Mindfulness is **"a rich awareness of discriminatory detail."** When people
act mindfully they are aware of context, of ways details differ, and of
deviations from expectations. Mindful people have "the big picture," but it
is "a big picture of the moment." The authors distinguish mindfulness from
"situation awareness": mindfulness combines (1) ongoing scrutiny of existing
expectations, (2) continuous refinement of expectations from newer
experience, (3) willingness to invent new expectations for unprecedented
events, (4) nuanced appreciation of context, and (5) identification of new
dimensions of context that improve foresight.

Expectations act like "an invisible hand" guiding toward confirming
perceptions and away from troublesome ones. [High-reliability
organizations](high-reliability-organizations.md) weaken this grip through a
**mindful infrastructure** that continually tracks small failures, resists
oversimplification, remains sensitive to operations, maintains resilience
capabilities, and takes advantage of shifting locations of expertise — the
five principles mapped to facets of attention:

- [Preoccupation with failure](preoccupation-with-failure.md) — notice small
  failures and retain their distinctiveness
- [Reluctance to simplify](reluctance-to-simplify.md) — retain detail rather
  than lose it in categories
- [Sensitivity to operations](sensitivity-to-operations.md) — notice
  symptomatic nuances in ongoing work
- [Commitment to resilience](commitment-to-resilience.md) — locate recovery
  pathways
- [Deference to expertise](deference-to-expertise.md) — locate knowledge to
  implement recovery

Mindfulness is about the *quality of attention*. HROs become more vulnerable
when attention is distracted, unstable, and dominated by abstractions —
predisposing people to misestimate, misunderstand, and misspecify what they
face. Distractions often take the form of associative thinking that pulls
attention from the present.

Less mindful practice normalizes; more mindful practice
[anomalizes](anomalizing-vs-normalizing.md) — capturing unique features that
slow the speed with which details get normalized. Mindfulness involves
strengthening the capability to hold on to the object being perceived,
prevent the mind wandering off it, and render observations more vivid — "the
characteristic of not wobbling, [of] not floating away from the object."

Reliable, safe performance is a [dynamic non-event](dynamic-non-event.md) —
what produces stable outcomes is constant mutual adjustment, not continuous
repetition. When a system runs safely, outcomes look constant and there is
seemingly "nothing to watch," masking continuous compensation.

Mindfulness takes effort: attend to failures more than successes, forgo
recipes and rules of thumb, attend to tactics rather than grand visions,
become more reactive than proactive, and acknowledge someone else may know
more. "High reliability organizations" is too static a phrase; better to
think in terms of high reliability *organizing* — systems unravel and must
be continually redone. See [anticipation versus
containment](anticipation-vs-containment.md) for how mindfulness splits across
preventing and recovering from surprises.

**Mindlessness** is the inverse: following recipes, imposing old categories,
rigidity, automatic pilot, and mislabeling unfamiliar contexts as familiar —
more than inattention alone. Mindlessness probes how often people meet the
unexpected day-to-day, how strongly they expect plans to hold, and whether
they solve or ignore disruptions. Audit 5.2 of the [conscious mindfulness
audits](conscious-mindfulness-audits.md) scores vulnerability; a limited
[action repertoire](commitment-to-resilience.md) is a major source of limited
perception — when people cannot act on what they see, they impose familiar
categories just so they can respond.
