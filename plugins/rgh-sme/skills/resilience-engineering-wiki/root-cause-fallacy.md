---
type: concept
title: The Root-Cause Fallacy
description: >
  Complex system failures have no single root cause — contributing factors
  are individually necessary and only jointly sufficient, and any "root
  cause" is just the arbitrary point where an investigation stopped.
sources:
  - title: The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error'
    resource: "The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error' (Dekker), ch. 3"
---

In complex, well-defended systems, failure requires a conjunction of
multiple factors that are **individually necessary and only jointly
sufficient**. No factor in the set is "the" cause; remove any one and the
outcome changes. "Root cause" therefore names nothing in the world — it is
the arbitrary stopping point of an investigation, usually placed wherever
the [accident model in use](accident-model-shapes-what-you-find.md) says to
stop (for the [chain-of-events model](chain-of-events-model.md), at the last
human act).

Causes are **constructed, not discovered**. After one mountain crash, the
civil aviation authority constructed causes of pilot automation misuse and
loss of situation awareness; the airline constructed non-standard ATC
phraseology, incomplete navigation databases, and absent radar coverage.
Both worked from the same wreckage. Which construction prevails is a matter
of interest and power, not of excavation — one reason blame gravitates to
the [sharp end](sharp-end-and-blunt-end.md).

The boundary between "mechanical failure" and "human error" is itself a
construction. In older jets, spoiler-lever arming failed frequently — a
"pilot slip", until analysis showed hydraulic aging stretched gear extension
to 30 seconds, intruding into checklist tasks, while the handle moved only
one inch, sat obscured behind the power levers, and had no warning light.
The slip was an engineered trap ([error as
symptom](human-error-as-symptom.md)).

The alternative to root-cause hunting is demonstrated by the Air Ontario
Dryden inquiry (Moshansky, 1989), which refused to issue "probable causes"
and instead produced 191 systemic recommendations spanning management
turnover, corporate mergers, regulatory oversight, and aircraft
introduction. Findings should separate [explanatory factors from change
factors](explanatory-vs-change-factors.md): explaining the past event is not
the same task as finding the levers that prevent the next one.

The construction goes deeper than picking a stopping point among causes: even
the higher-level narrative an investigator imposes on a sequence of events —
"this was drift," "this was a sudden stochastic failure with no antecedent" —
is itself a construction (an *eisegesis*, reading meaning in) rather than a
discovery (an *exegesis*, extracting a meaning already present), because no
analyst, model, or agency can be as complex as the whole system it is
studying. Two equally careful investigators can produce genuinely
contradictory, not merely incomplete, readings of the same [drift into
failure](drift-into-failure.md) episode, and neither is the final "true
story."

The same arbitrary-stopping-point problem recurs at the scale of a whole
system's life cycle, not just a single accident chain: [three design phases
of resilient systems](three-design-phases-of-resilient-systems.md) shows how
a deficiency that surfaces during operation can trace back to a
conceptualisation-phase decision made years earlier, and an investigation
confined to the moment of failure will never reach it.
