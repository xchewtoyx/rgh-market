---
type: concept
title: Protocols Require a Speak-Up Culture
description: >
  Emergency procedures and trained reflexes only work when interpersonal risk
  does not crowd out speaking, listening, and acting on them — psychological
  safety is a precondition for protocol use, not a substitute for it.
sources:
  - title: The Fearless Organization
    resource: "The Fearless Organization (Edmondson), ch. 6"
---

Written procedures, checklists, and mandated training do not guarantee the
behaviour they describe. Under time pressure, people continuously perform
**micro-assessments of interpersonal risk** — weighing the tangible, immediate
cost of speaking (status loss, irritation, appearing weak) against benefits
that feel vague or delayed. Where that calculus favours silence, practitioners
fail to recognize that hesitation itself is consequential in the seconds
available.

US Airways Flight 1549 (2009) is the positive case against the catalogue of
[catastrophes from dangerous silence](dangerous-silence-in-high-risk-systems.md):
the crew had CRM and [threat and error management
(TEM)](threat-and-error-management.md) training, but Edmondson's point is
that training alone is insufficient without the culture that lets it be
exercised — the same structural dependency [training managerial resilience
skills](training-managerial-resilience-skills.md) documents for upward
assertiveness. Sullenberger had long trained pilots in CRM; the recorded
exchange shows trained reflexes *used*, not merely known: immediate
[deference to expertise](deference-to-expertise.md) in the "My aircraft / Your
aircraft" handoff, [communication forcing
functions](communication-forcing-functions-for-distributed-authority.md) such
as the controller's repeat-back of the Hudson landing intention, and
disciplined restraint from support roles that did not second-guess the
captain's sacrificing decision.

The inverse cases are familiar: the 1982 Air Florida Potomac crash, where a
copilot could not insist on turning back despite freezing rain; Asiana 2013,
where a copilot would not warn about low approach speed. CRM became mandatory
for pilots partly in response to [Tenerife](dangerous-silence-in-high-risk-systems.md)
and similar failures — institutionalizing speak-up and listen-up — but CRM
spread to medicine for the same reason: one cited study found CRM-like
training improved delivery-room outcomes and satisfaction.

Psychological safety here is not the general team-comfort construct from
knowledge-work research; it is the safety-science requirement that high-risk
systems institutionalize voice and listening so that [procedures as resources
for action](procedures-as-resources-for-action.md) can actually be deployed
when the situation exceeds what automation and policy explicitly cover — as
Flight 1549 did from the first seconds after dual engine failure.
