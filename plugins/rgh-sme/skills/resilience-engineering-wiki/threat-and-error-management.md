Line Operations Safety Audit (LOSA) data — direct observation of normal line
flying, not incident investigation after the fact — shows that procedural
deviations and errors are not occasional lapses by otherwise-compliant
practitioners; they are routinely embedded in ordinary, successful practice.
This finding is what Threat and Error Management (TEM) is built to explain:
rather than treating professionalism as the absence of error, TEM defines it
as the ongoing management of *threats* (external, situation-generated
pressures) and *errors* (departures from an intended action) such that
performance is recovered and kept within acceptable boundaries before either
one becomes consequential.

This reframes error the same direction as [Safety-II](safety-i-and-safety-ii.md)
and [error as symptom rather than cause](human-error-as-symptom.md): an
error observed in isolation, stripped of the recovery that immediately
followed it, looks identical whether it belonged to a near-miss or to a
routine, successful operation — what actually distinguishes them is whether
context was managed and performance was recovered, which is exactly the skill
TEM measures rather than ignores. A LOSA-style audit that only counted raw
error occurrences would therefore be measuring the wrong thing, for the same
reason [counting and targeting human error
misleads](why-error-counting-misleads.md) generally: the count conflates
errors that were caught and recovered with errors that weren't, when the
recovery is where the professionalism actually lives.

In aviation practice, TEM is routinely paired with Crew Resource Management
(CRM) training — interpersonal communication, leadership, and decision-making
under pressure — because managing threats and errors in real time requires
speaking up, listening, and coordinating across roles, not merely individual
technical recovery. The US Airways Flight 1549 crew had both; Edmondson treats
the cockpit recording as evidence that [protocols require a speak-up
culture](protocols-require-speak-up-culture.md) for TEM and CRM to function
when automated failure guidance and written policy explicitly defer to
"common sense and good judgment."
