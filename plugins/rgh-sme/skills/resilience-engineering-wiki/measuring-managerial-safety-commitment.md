title: Measuring Managerial Safety Commitment
description: >
  Three techniques for making a manager's real safety-versus-production
  priority visible and comparable, because self-report from the manager
  alone is the least reliable of the three.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts (Hollnagel, Woods, Leveson), ch. 14"
---

If [managerial resilience](middle-managers-as-safety-buffer.md) is a
structural property of how an organisation supports its managers rather
than a fixed personal trait, it follows that it should be measurable rather
than only inferred after the fact from a disaster. Flin gives three
techniques, each measuring the same underlying thing — whether a manager
actually prioritises safety over production when the two conflict — from a
different vantage point:

1. **Safety climate surveys.** Workforce-wide surveys of how safety is
   perceived to be managed and prioritised against production and cost;
   because they aggregate many subordinates' perceptions, they function as
   a leading indicator of a manager's real (as opposed to stated) priority.
   North Sea oil studies found substantial workforce scepticism running
   under the surface of official policy: 44% of respondents agreed
   production came before safety in practice, and only 23% believed
   managers cared more about safety than production.
2. **Upward appraisal (360° safety assessment).** Subordinates directly
   rate their senior managers' safety commitment, leadership, and priority-
   setting, and the results are mirrored against the same managers' own
   self-ratings in a feedback workshop. Trialled on 70 senior oil-industry
   managers paired with 350 direct reports, the gap the mirroring exposes
   between self-perception and subordinate perception is itself the useful
   signal, and has been shown to stimulate behavioural change in the
   executives confronted with it.
3. **Managerial scripts (scenario-based priority testing).** Leaders are
   run through operational scenarios that pose a concrete conflict between
   mission/production demands and safety, and their choices under the
   scenario are scored — tested, for instance, on Israeli army platoon
   leaders. Unlike a survey question, a scenario forces an actual trade-off
   decision rather than a stated preference.

The three techniques are complementary rather than substitutable: a survey
captures aggregate perception cheaply but not the reasoning behind it; 360°
appraisal exposes the specific gap between a manager's self-image and how
subordinates actually experience their priorities; a scripted scenario is
the only one of the three that measures a decision under simulated pressure
rather than an opinion about past behaviour. None of the three asks the
manager to simply self-report their own priority directly, because that is
exactly the measure most vulnerable to the manager believing their own
stated priorities are what actually governs their behaviour under pressure.
