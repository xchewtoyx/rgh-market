A just culture determines what happens to people after things go wrong — and
therefore what the organisation gets to learn, since fear of unjust treatment
[suppresses reporting](blame-suppresses-reporting.md). Dekker, who coined the
term: "Responses to incidents and accidents that are seen as unjust can
impede safety investigations, promote fear rather than mindfulness in people
who do safety-critical work, make organizations more bureaucratic rather
than more careful, and cultivate professional secrecy, evasion, and
self-protection."

**Why retributive schemes fail.** Popular "culpability tree" schemes sort
behaviour into White ("honest mistake"), Grey ("risk-taking"), and Black
("recklessness"). The categories feel principled but rely on post-hoc value
judgments saturated with [hindsight bias](hindsight-bias.md) — and the lines
are drawn by whoever holds organisational power, which routinely converts
"just culture" into a managerial tool for terminating workers. Judging a
"violation" also presupposes the written rule was workable, ignoring the
[work-as-imagined / work-as-done gap](work-as-imagined-vs-work-as-done.md).

**Accountability as account-giving.** Restorative just culture redefines
accountability: practitioners are held accountable by being enabled to *give
their account* — tell their story — without fear of prosecution or career
jeopardy. The account is where learning lives: one airline facing repeated
logbook procedure violations redesigned the logbook pages together with its
mechanics, and violations dropped to zero. Relatedly, O'Leary & Pidgeon found
formal reports to line management strip away contextual complexity ("flap
failure"), while confidential reports reveal the real operational risks
(distraction from non-standard clearances, terrain proximity, forgotten
checks during a go-around).

Reason's [informed culture](informed-culture.md) model names just culture as
one of four interlocking subcultures (with reporting, flexible, and learning
cultures) required for managers and operators to hold current knowledge of
what determines system safety. At Bristol Royal Infirmary, medical audit was
deliberately insulated from management as "an educational tool, not a
mechanism for accountability" — when the line between acceptable and
unacceptable behavior is unclear, the ~90% of unsafe acts that should be
reportable without fear get concealed instead.

**Five pillars of restorative just culture:**

1. **Ask what is responsible, not who** — focus on tools, tasks, and system
   design ([error as symptom](human-error-as-symptom.md)).
2. **Incorporate the messy details** — involve peer practitioners who
   understand real operational trade-offs in post-incident review.
3. **Restorative over retributive justice** — prioritise healing, dialogue,
   and repair of relationships over punishment.
4. **Forward-looking accountability** — accountability means who takes
   responsibility for fixing the systemic issue, not who pays for the past
   (see [authority–responsibility
   mismatch](authority-responsibility-mismatch.md) for why retrospective
   blame misfires).
5. **Second-victim support** — formal support for practitioners traumatised
   by their involvement in an accident (Dekker, 2013); their recovery is
   directly tied to whether the organisation learns.
