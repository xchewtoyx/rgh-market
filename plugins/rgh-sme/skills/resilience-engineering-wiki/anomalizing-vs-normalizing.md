---
type: concept
title: Anomalizing Versus Normalizing
description: >
  Mindful organizations treat unfamiliar events as anomalies worth
  investigating; mindless ones relabel them as normal, concealing information
  that foreshadows trouble.
sources:
  - title: Managing the Unexpected
    resource: "Managing the Unexpected (Weick, Sutcliffe), ch. 3"
---

Weick and Sutcliffe: "Less mindful practice normalizes; more mindful practice
**anomalizes**." [Mindfulness in high-reliability
organizing](mindfulness-in-high-reliability-organizing.md) weakens the
tendency to simplify occurrences into familiar events ([normalization of
deviance](normalization-of-deviance.md)) and strengthens the tendency to
redefine an event as something less familiar. Anomalizing captures unique
features that slow the speed with which details get normalized; visible
anomalies foreshadow potential problems and reduce the tendency to let events
incubate until unmanageable.

NASA's "in-family" versus "out-of-family" distinction illustrates category-
driven blindness: reporting requires existing words and categories, and
"whatever labels a group uses will color what members of the group think they
see and report." Columbia's foam strike was classified "almost in-family" —
treated as largely understood — partly because of this careless use of
categories (see [reluctance to simplify](reluctance-to-simplify.md)).

HRO people worry about the temptation to treat unexpected events as no big
deal — to normalize them. When unexpected burn marks appeared on Challenger
O-rings, engineers redefined "acceptable risk" step by step until hot-gas
leakage past gaskets was normal. Larry Wear: "Once you've accepted an
anomaly or something less than perfect... you've given up your virginity. You
can't go back."

Aviators call the feeling that something is not quite right "leemers" (from
"leery"). Trust those feelings — they signal your model of the world is in
error. Hold on to them and resist normalizing, because "in that brief
interval between surprise and successful normalizing lies one of your few
opportunities to discover what you don't know." HRO people try to "freeze
and stretch out their unexpected moments" to learn more.

Perceptions of the unexpected are fleeting: after interruption, people are
candid briefly, then "get their stories straight" in ways that justify
actions and protect reputations — and learning stops. The period after a
[brutal audit](brutal-audit.md) follows the same pattern unless culture
institutionalizes candor (see [informed culture](informed-culture.md)).

Treat all unexpected events as information, especially rare ones. Watch for
the temptation to redefine the unexpected as normal — that conceals
information and heightens risk.
