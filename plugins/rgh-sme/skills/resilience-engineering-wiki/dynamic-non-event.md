type: concept
title: Dynamic Non-Event
description: >
  Reliable, safe performance looks like nothing happening because constant
  mutual adjustment among operators compensates for changing conditions —
  masking the mindful work that produces stability.
sources:
  - title: Managing the Unexpected
    resource: "Managing the Unexpected (Weick, Sutcliffe), ch. 3"
---

Reliable, safe performance "is a dynamic nonevent — what produces the stable
outcome is constant change rather than continuous repetition. To achieve this
stability, a change in one system parameter must be compensated for by change
in other parameters."

When a system runs safely and reliably, outcomes look constant and there is
seemingly "nothing to watch" — but this masks continuous mutual adjustment.
Examples from carrier flight decks: the deck gets more slippery, so plane
spacing increases; the radio signal breaks up, so the crew switches to hand
signals; seas get heavy, so aircraft aren't moved on sea-exposed elevators;
armed weapons explode at different temperatures, so one person records which
weapons are on which planes for firefighters.

No one in an [high-reliability organization](high-reliability-organizations.md)
understands operations perfectly — puzzlement is common. It is impossible to
manage solely through mindless control systems (rules, plans, routines, fixed
categories) because no one knows enough to design such a system for a dynamic
environment. Designers must organize to evoke [mindfulness in high-reliability
organizing](mindfulness-in-high-reliability-organizing.md), and people must
adopt continuous learning and refinement of expectations. "Carriers are
guided as much by updated expectations as they are by stable computations."

This parallels health care versus manufacturing: huge patient variability,
rapid adaptation, changing knowledge base, and expert judgment in dynamic
settings — closer to carrier flight-deck operations (48–60 second recovery
intervals with 100–200 people) than to assembly lines. Once-rigid orders have
given way to flexible, less hierarchical methods — junior officers override
the captain when an order risks crew safety.

[Sensitivity to operations](sensitivity-to-operations.md) is what makes these
adjustments visible before they fail: routines treated as mindless miss the
compensations that actually keep the system going while sustaining the
illusion that rule-compliance, not continuing adjustment, produces safety.

Reliability does not mean absence of variation — "It takes mindful variety to
ensure stable high performance." HROs learned this through both successes and
failures.
