Deference to expertise is the fifth [high-reliability
organization](high-reliability-organizations.md) principle and one of two
**containment** practices (with [commitment to
resilience](commitment-to-resilience.md)). HROs create operating dynamics
grounded in deference to expertise — but this is less simple than "assign
the problem to an expert." Hierarchical authority exists in HROs as in
traditional organizations. During routine operations, members defer to the
powerful and senior; during unexpected problems, HROs alter this pattern.

**Positive example — Flight 1549.** Seconds after dual engine failure,
Captain Sullenberger and First Officer Skiles — who had met only hours
earlier — executed an immediate, unambiguous authority handoff: "My aircraft"
/ "Your aircraft," with no hesitancy, apology, or disagreement. The choice
was reasoned, not automatic deference to rank: Sullenberger had far more
hours on the A320 and could see the cityscape from his side; Skiles knew
emergency and landing-equipment procedures better. Support roles showed the
same pattern from the blunt end: air-traffic controller Patrick Harten asked
only essential questions, kept parallel phone lines open so Sullenberger
could hear without repetition, and later wrote that his words conveyed
understanding that "these hard choices were mine to make" — not dictating a
plan to the person with the best situational picture. See [protocols require
a speak-up culture](protocols-require-speak-up-culture.md).

**Migrating decisions.** Karlene Roberts, Susanne Stout, and Jennifer Halpern
identified [migrating decisions](migrating-decisions.md) — decisions pushed
down to the lowest levels when quick action is needed, and migrating around
the organization "in search of a person who has specific knowledge of the
event." Expertise is not necessarily matched with hierarchical position.

**Failures of deference.** NASA's STS-107 (Columbia, 2003): mission manager
Linda Ham asked *who* was requesting damage images rather than the merits;
managers lacked security clearance for national imaging capabilities. CAIB:
"Allegiance to hierarchy and procedure had replaced deference to NASA
engineers' technical expertise." Even when NASA deferred to expertise, it
deferred in ways that foreclosed learning — treating Calvin Schomburg as the
thermal-protection expert when he was not an RCC expert, and welcoming his
minimal-damage opinion while seeking no others.

At Cerro Grande, deference was mixed: Paul Gleason (a legend in the fire
world) received deference and took over as burn boss, but (1) many of his
good decisions were not implemented, and (2) he may have received *too much*
deference — others assumed "if Gleason doesn't see it, it's not happening"
and discounted their own impressions. Earlier, a dispatcher deferred to
authority rather than expertise by waiting for his supervisor at 7:00 a.m.
rather than acting on the burn boss's 3:00 a.m. urgent request.

**Deference downward.** HROs are mindful when command-and-control keeps
engineers at the top and maintenance specialists at the bottom — the
authority hierarchy does not reliably correspond to the knowledge hierarchy.
Expertise is often "disguised by dirty coveralls and greasy hands." At Diablo
Canyon, the plant manager stayed out of the control room during an automatic
shutdown and relied on subordinates' root-cause analyses; Mathilde Bourrier
found outage planning supported by formal delegation to craft personnel plus
top management availability at all times.

**Expertise, not experts.** Expertise is *relational* — an assemblage of
knowledge, experience, learning, and intuitions seldom embodied in one
individual. Gene Rochlin: emerging crises on aircraft carriers are often
contained by informal networks — knowledgeable people self-organize into ad
hoc groups for expert problem solving, then dissolve when the crisis ends.

Paul Schulman's three values at Diablo Canyon — credibility, trust,
attentiveness — with credibility most salient for expertise. Credibility can
come from candid acknowledgment of the limits of one's knowledge. CAIB: "In
highly uncertain circumstances, when lives were immediately at risk,
management failed to defer to its engineers" and failed to recognize that
qualitative, subjective, democratic processes were more appropriate than
protocol and chain of command.

Asking for help when out of one's depth is seen as strength in HROs,
contrasted with "macho" cultures where admitting limits is frowned upon.

silence in high-risk systems](dangerous-silence-in-high-risk-systems.md).
