---
type: concept
title: Out-of-Domain Simulation for Resilience Training
description: >
  Training a team's generic escalation competencies in a domain unrelated to
  their real work strips away the ego and prestige stakes that make trainees
  defend familiar procedures instead of reflecting on how they actually
  handled the unexpected.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering in Practice"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering in Practice (Hollnagel, Pariès, Wreathall), ch. 4"
---

An empirical training design choice, distinct from what a scenario needs to
contain (see [scenario design for escalation
training](scenario-design-for-escalation-training.md)): train
[generic escalation competencies](generic-competencies-for-escalation.md)
in a simulation drawn from a domain the trainees do not work in at all —
fire-safety engineers running a passenger-vessel bridge simulation in an
Atlantic storm, rather than a fire scenario.

**The mechanism is removing ego and prestige stakes, not novelty for its own
sake.** When a scenario sits inside a trainee's own professional domain,
failing at it threatens professional identity directly — a fire commander
who performs badly in a fire-command simulation has failed *at being a fire
commander*, and the natural response is defensive: attack the scenario's
fidelity to real-world conditions, insist implicit judgement was actually
sound, and treat any critique as a challenge to professional competence
rather than useful data. Move the same team into an unfamiliar domain and
that identity threat disappears — nobody's professional self-image rests on
being good at commanding a storm-damaged ship — which frees the debrief to
be genuinely reflective rather than defensive.

**The empirical contrast between the two postures was stark.** Teams
trained out-of-domain later showed, in an unrelated domain-specific
exercise, a reflective stance: recognising that deep operational learning
does not require exact physical or procedural fidelity to the training
scenario, articulating nuanced accounts of their own escalation handling,
and conducting genuinely self-critical performance analysis. Control teams
given only standard in-domain training showed a defensive stance instead:
high confidence in rigid, predefined procedures; using minor discrepancies
between the exercise and "real" operations to dismiss the exercise's value
outright; and incorrectly believing their own implicit, unstated goals had
already been sufficient and proactive when they had not been.

**The transfer claim is that the competency trained is genuinely generic.**
Because escalation dynamics, goal-formulation breakdowns, and team
coordination failures recur in roughly the same shape regardless of the
technical domain they occur in, confronting participants with an
unfamiliar domain's version of escalation still builds the transferable
adaptive mindset the training is actually after, and the confrontation
lands more cleanly for having no domain-specific procedure to hide behind.
