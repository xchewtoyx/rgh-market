type: concept
title: Expectancy Violation as Anomaly Detection
description: >
  Experienced practitioners often detect danger not by recognising a
  familiar threat pattern but by noticing that events are failing to match
  what a recognised situation should produce next — which is why the
  warning is so hard for them to articulate.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power (Klein), ch. 4"
---

The [recognition-primed decision model](recognition-primed-decision-model.md)
holds that recognising a situation as a familiar pattern supplies
expectancies — what should happen next if the read is correct. Those
expectancies do more than confirm a diagnosis; they are also the mechanism
that catches a *wrong* diagnosis. When events stop matching what the
recognised pattern predicts, that mismatch itself is a signal, even before
the practitioner can say what the correct alternative pattern is. A
commander who reads a fire as a small kitchen fire but finds a hose stream
having no effect, an unexpectedly hot living room, and unexpected quiet is
not recognising a specific hidden danger (a basement fire below) — he is
recognising that his current read has stopped being confirmed, which is
enough to justify retreat before the specific danger is ever identified.

This explains why such warnings feel like "a sixth sense" to the people who
issue them and are so hard to convey to others: the practitioner is reacting
to an **absence** (a sound that should be there and isn't, a response that
should have occurred and hasn't) rather than to a positive cue, and the
underlying comparison is against a large number of blended-together past
cases rather than any one retrievable memory. Both properties — reacting to
absence, and drawing on generalised rather than specific memory — make the
judgment feel intuitive and near-impossible to justify on demand, even
though it is a direct product of the practitioner's real expertise, not
mysticism.

The practical consequences:

- **Expectancy violation is a legitimate, trainable form of hazard
  detection**, not a soft substitute for "real" evidence — it is one more
  reason to take [ambiguous threats](ambiguous-threats.md) and unexplained
  unease seriously rather than demanding an articulable cause before acting,
  the discipline [chronic unease](chronic-unease.md) already prescribes.
- **It is fallible in the same direction it is powerful**: because it works
  by pattern-matching, it can mistake one kind of anomaly for another (an
  altitude-driven radar-detection artifact misread as a target
  accelerating) — mental simulation and cross-checking with people who hold
  different local vantage points remain necessary, not optional, once the
  alarm fires.
- **Investigators and managers should not discount a warning just because
  the person raising it cannot explain it.** Demanding an articulated
  mechanism before acting on an experienced practitioner's unease filters
  out exactly the signal this mechanism is best at producing.
