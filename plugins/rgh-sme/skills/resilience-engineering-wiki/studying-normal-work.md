  The proactive practice of learning how daily work actually succeeds — six
  field questions and an engagement posture — because pre-catastrophic risk
  lives in unreported routine adaptations, not in incident data.

In ultra-safe systems, low incident rates erode [chronic
unease](chronic-unease.md) and incident data stops mapping real
vulnerability ([Wald's bomber paradox](walds-bomber-paradox.md)). The
replacement information source is normal work itself — the
[Safety-II](safety-i-and-safety-ii.md) programme made concrete. Six field
questions:

1. **What is the gap between written guidance and actual work?** Dig into
   *why* [the gap](work-as-imagined-vs-work-as-done.md) exists rather than
   citing operators for violations.
2. **What workarounds and improvisations are required daily** to get the job
   done?
3. **What daily frustrations exist** with machinery, technology, or
   contractors?
4. **What do workers consider "dodgy"** about the operation?
5. **How do operators "finish the design" of their tools?** Look for
   physical reminders, jury-rigged covers, post-it notes — each is a
   requirement the design failed to meet.
6. **What informal coaching occurs** ("here's how to actually make it
   work")?
7. **Which stories get retold, and what do they license?** [Permission
   stories](permission-stories.md) teach newcomers the real, unwritten
   boundary of acceptable action more reliably than any policy document.

The cautionary example: Space Shuttle external-tank mechanics routinely
applied unreported chemical foam repairs mixed in cups to meet launch
schedules. Because the workaround was daily routine, no formal reporting
system ever captured it — and it contributed directly to catastrophic
failure. Routine adaptation is exactly what [drifts](drift-into-failure.md)
and [normalises](normalization-of-deviance.md) beneath formal visibility.

The reason this matters more than incident data is Amalberti's **banality of
accidents thesis**: in mature, complex systems, accidents usually occur
without any serious breakdown or error — they result from a combination of
factors none of which alone would cause even a minor incident, so incident
reporting becomes progressively less predictive of major disasters as a
system matures. This rejects the "common-cause hypothesis" — the assumption
that incidents and accidents share the same causal signature and an incident
is just an accident that stopped one step short. What actually precedes
catastrophe in these systems is not a legible trail of near-misses but
ordinary, successful, unremarkable normal work: **incidents do not precede
accidents; normal work does.** This is also why [decrementalism](decrementalism.md)
is so corrosive to detection — each step away from the original norm is
individually too small to report, so the reporting stream stays clean while
the margin disappears.

This preference for normal work over accident data has a learning-theory
grounding, not just an empirical one: see [three conditions for effective
learning](three-conditions-for-effective-learning.md) for why accidents
structurally fail the conditions effective learning requires, while everyday
operations satisfy them.

Posture matters as much as questions. Field visits focused on "safety"
become paternalistic inspection — treating workers like children and
producing performances of compliance. Focusing on **people's work** treats
operators as colleagues and opens the channel for bad news and trade-off
talk. That requires a genuinely inquisitive mindset: wanting to learn what
is frustrating or dodgy, listening without ego or instant judgment, and
being willing to be proven wrong about how you imagined the work — all of
which collapses the moment candour is punished ([blame suppresses
reporting](blame-suppresses-reporting.md)).
