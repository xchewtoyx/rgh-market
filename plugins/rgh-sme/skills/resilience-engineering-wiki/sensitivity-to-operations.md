Sensitivity to operations is the third [high-reliability
organization](high-reliability-organizations.md) anticipation principle. It
means being responsive to the messy reality inside a system — monitoring
"expectable interactions with a complicated [and] often opaque system" and
responding promptly to unexpected ones.

It differs from the first two principles: [preoccupation with
failure](preoccupation-with-failure.md) detects small discrepancies anywhere;
[reluctance to simplify](reluctance-to-simplify.md) concerns the concepts
used to detect; sensitivity to operations is about the work itself — seeing
what people are actually doing regardless of intentions, designs, and plans
([work-as-imagined vs work-as-done](work-as-imagined-vs-work-as-done.md)).

At Cerro Grande, sensitivity failed three ways on budgets: (1) insensitive to
instant activation of standby resources; (2) conflicting definitions of
"standby" (available in the area vs. unassigned and immediately available);
(3) the budgeting operation itself poorly understood, so bottlenecks in its
own functioning went unseen. Gleason: "If someone phones and needs help,
don't talk budget. This is fire! Do the money thing later." A policy stalemate
at dispatch — refusing contingency resources until the fire was declared
escaped — starved frontline workers of help.

Diablo Canyon control room operators have "a bubble," a 3-D mental picture of
the plant; frequent interdisciplinary briefings during outages build
credibility and deepen understanding of interdependent workings. Admiral
Rickover complained too much attention went to R&D and too little to "the
daily drudgery of seeing that every aspect... is in fact properly handled
every day."

**Threats to sensitivity:**

1. **Engineering culture** — valuing quantitative, context-free formal
   knowledge over experiential knowledge operators need to fulfill engineers'
   intentions.
2. **Routines becoming mindless** — treating customary work as automatic,
   forgetting to ask "What if...?" Mindful operators rework routines to fit
   changed conditions; these small adjustments are "the bane of a
   command-and-control system" yet are what actually keeps the system going.
3. **Overestimating soundness from misread close calls** — NASA reinterpreted
   growing O-ring burn marks as "safety in the guise of danger" rather than
   danger in the guise of safety, expanding acceptable risk limits.

On carriers, officers from captain down stay in continuous communication
during flight ops. Counter-example: a carrier at high speed in heavy seas did
not slow for an elevator move; seven men and an aircraft were washed
overboard.

Sensitivity ties to relationships: people who won't speak up starve the
system of what it needs to know. "If managers refuse to examine what happens
between heads, they'll be eternally puzzled by what appears to happen inside
individual heads."

Gary Klein's **STICC** briefing protocol keeps clearer expectations from
increasing confirmation bias: Situation, Task, Intent, Concern ("tell me if
you see something I don't"), Calibrate.
