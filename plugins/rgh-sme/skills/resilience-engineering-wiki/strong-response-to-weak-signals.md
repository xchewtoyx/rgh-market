title: Strong Response to Weak Signals
description: >
  Managing the unexpected requires amplifying response to small, ambiguous
  clues — the opposite of the normal instinct to treat weak signals with weak
  action until they become visible crises.
sources:
  - title: Managing the Unexpected
    resource: "Managing the Unexpected (Weick, Sutcliffe), ch. 1"
---

Unexpected failures often follow a recurring pattern: small discrepancies
give off weak clues that are easy to treat if spotted early, but much harder
to treat once they become visible. The Cerro Grande prescribed burn (May
2000) is the book's anchoring case — a one-inch band of fire creeping across
a fireline produced roughly $1 billion in damage. Each preceding signal was
weak and each organizational response was weak: four nearby prescribed fires
had escaped in the prior two weeks; the blackline's inner edge could not be
extinguished; most personnel were released at midnight leaving a skeleton
crew; a dispatcher refused a fresh hotshot crew at 3:00 a.m.; budget
negotiations delayed contingency resources.

Managing the unexpected therefore requires **strong responses to weak
signals** — counterintuitive and "not very heroic," since the normal instinct
is [weak response to weak signal, strong response to strong
signal](seeking-weaker-failure-signals.md). The overwhelming behavioral
difference between [high-reliability organizations](high-reliability-organizations.md)
and others shows up at this early stage, when [mindfulness in high-reliability
organizing](mindfulness-in-high-reliability-organizing.md) preserves the
capacity to see significance in weak signals and respond vigorously.

Reliability lapses in HROs typically do not emerge as full-blown issues;
small clues accumulate and suggest that unexpected things are happening and
are not going away. A [brutal audit](brutal-audit.md) — an unexpected event
testing structures built before chaos arrives — is mild when those clues are
acted on early and brutal when they are not.
