title: Generic Competencies for Escalating Situations
description: >
  Four team-level, non-domain-specific competencies — information
  management, communication and coordination, decision-making, and effect
  control — that let a team keep functioning once role-specific procedures
  stop applying.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering in Practice"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering in Practice (Hollnagel, Pariès, Wreathall), ch. 4"
---

These are the competencies specifically needed for [irregular
threats](regular-irregular-unexampled-threat-typology.md) — one-off events
outside any pre-formulated response algorithm, but not yet beyond the
team's collective frame of reference entirely.

Standard preparation for high-risk work leans on two tools that both fail
against a genuine [escalation](escalation.md): rigid emergency procedures,
which only cover contingencies someone already anticipated, and
domain-specific technical training, which builds individual skill but not
team-level adaptability. What handles a genuine escalation is a different
kind of competency entirely — **generic**, in the sense of not being tied to
any particular domain's technical content, and belonging to the *team*
rather than to any individual role. Bergström, Dahlström, Dekker, and
Petersen group these into four interdependent categories:

**Information management.** Escalation typically floods a team with more
incoming data, not less — the paradox is that greater data access makes
extracting the informative signal from background noise *harder*, not
easier. The countermeasure is goal-driven filtering: a team with shared,
explicit goals can prioritise, distribute, and share information by
strategic relevance, rather than triaging by raw urgency alone, which is
what a noise flood otherwise forces.

**Communication and coordination.** Effective coordination starts from
clear role understanding, but escalation changes each member's workload
dynamically, so the team also has to monitor for overload and reformulate
tasks or inject resources in real time rather than holding the original
role assignment fixed. Predefined, bureaucratic response structures rarely
fit an escalation's actual shape, so resilience here means continuously
checking organisational fit and trading off predefined structure against
real-time flexibility, rather than picking one stance in advance and
holding it.

**Decision-making.** Both of the two default modes fail under escalation
for opposite reasons: pure consensus decision-making drowns the team in
data-processing overhead and forces purely reactive management, while pure
hierarchical decision-making creates a single point of failure by
overloading the leader with every decision. The alternative is
**distributed decision-making**, where authority to decide sits with
whichever team member has the relevant information, held together by shared
explicit goals plus active, proactive sharing of what was just decided —
distribution without that sharing just recreates uncoordinated [cross-
purposes](working-at-cross-purposes.md) instead of solving it.

**Effect control.** A team under escalation has to keep questioning its own
operating picture actively — "what could be wrong in our understanding of
this situation right now?" — rather than treating the current understanding
as settled once formed, and keep renegotiating goals and task assignments
as the situation's actual dynamics continue to change rather than holding
the original plan. This is the team-level discipline underneath [preserving
margin for future response](preserving-margin-for-future-response.md): not
spending the team's adaptive capacity on defending an understanding that
needs to be revised.

**A controlled experiment gives this framework its clearest single
observable marker: the "proactive switch."** Teams trained on generic
escalation competencies and teams given only standard emergency-response
training were compared on an unrelated domain-specific crisis exercise.
Control-group briefings stayed **reactive** — consensus-seeking discussions
trying to agree on what had already happened — while trained teams executed
a "proactive switch": shifting the same briefing moment from reactive
consensus-building to forward-looking, goal-setting communication that
disseminated decisions and updated the team's shared goals going forward.
The shift is a concrete, observable instance of the decision-making and
effect-control competencies above, not a separate fifth competency: a team
able to make the proactive switch is a team whose information management is
actually goal-driven and whose effect control is actively revising the
operating picture rather than re-litigating it. Trained teams also differed
sharply in self-assessment afterward — offering nuanced, self-critical
analysis of their own escalation handling — while control teams defended
their performance and dismissed the exercise's low domain-fidelity as the
reason it "didn't count," missing the generic-competency gap the exercise
was built to expose.

**Two individual-cognition failure modes this framework specifically
targets** are already documented elsewhere in this bundle and recur at the
team scale under escalation: getting anchored on an early, plausible
reading of an ambiguous situation is [cognitive
fixation](cognitive-fixation.md), and a chain of individually-reasonable
explaining-away steps carrying understanding steadily off course is [the
garden path fallacy](garden-path-fallacy-and-snap-back.md) — both describe
exactly the traps information management and effect control are designed to
catch before they compound. How to deliberately train a team against these
traps, rather than only describe them, is covered in [scenario design for
escalation training](scenario-design-for-escalation-training.md).
