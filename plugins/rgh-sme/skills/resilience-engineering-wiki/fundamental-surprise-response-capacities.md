type: concept
title: Responding to Fundamental Surprise
description: >
  The specific real-time cognitive capacities that let a team move from the
  shock of a genuinely unanticipated event into effective action — distinct
  from the capacities that handle anticipated abnormal situations.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering in Practice"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering in Practice (Hollnagel, Pariès, Wreathall), ch. 2"
---

A genuinely unanticipated event — a "fundamental surprise" (Lanir, 1986) —
does not just present a hard problem; it first inflicts a cognitive shock:
disbelief, acute physical distress, an initial denial that this is really
happening. On Flight 1549, both the captain and the air traffic controller
handling the flight report exactly this reaction in the first seconds after
the bird strike. Overcoming fundamental surprise, not just solving the
problem underneath it, is the first real-time demand [the boundary of
potential variability](boundary-of-potential-variability.md) places on a
team once it accepts the boundary has actually been crossed.

Six capacities recur across accounts of teams that made it through this
transition effectively:

1. **Rapid operational comprehension** — recovering from the initial shock
   fast enough to build an accurate mental model of what is actually
   happening, rather than staying stuck on the model that just broke.
2. **Single-shot options assessment** — recognising explicitly that this
   decision cannot be retried, which changes what counts as an acceptable
   option compared with an ordinary decision that tolerates a second
   attempt (see [sacrificing decisions under
   crisis](sacrificing-decisions-under-crisis.md), which this capacity feeds
   directly).
3. **Balance of experience and opportunism** — drawing on relevant past
   experience outside the immediate domain (glider-piloting skill, in the
   Hudson case) while simultaneously exploiting whatever the specific
   environment happens to offer (choosing a landing site near active ferry
   traffic specifically because it would speed rescue).
4. **Dynamic replanning** — revising the actual intention repeatedly and
   quickly as the situation develops, rather than committing early and
   holding: the plan moved from returning to LaGuardia, to a specific
   runway there, to a different airport, to the river, in a matter of
   minutes.
5. **Procedure bricolage and improvisation** — executing partial procedures
   and running steps out of the order they were written in, because the
   normal sequencing assumes more time than a single-shot emergency
   provides. This is [procedures as resources for
   action](procedures-as-resources-for-action.md) under the most extreme
   time pressure the concept describes: relight and ditching checklists
   were both started and never finished in their written order.
6. **Hyper-focused stress control** — managing extreme stress through
   narrowed, intense concentration rather than through calm in the ordinary
   sense; the goal is functional focus, not the absence of stress.

**The psychological cost of the event does not fall where the physical
danger fell.** Post-event trauma often affects blunt-end and supporting
personnel more severely than the sharp-end operators who were directly
acting — the air traffic controller handling Flight 1549 needed 45 days off
to recover from persistent traumatic imagery, more than the flight crew
required. Three factors are identified as buffering trauma for the people
who do recover well: active involvement in the response rather than
watching helplessly, a sense of altruistic purpose in what they were doing,
and group solidarity with the people they were doing it alongside — all
three describe the sharp end's typical experience and are specifically
absent from a purely observing or coordinating role at the blunt end.
