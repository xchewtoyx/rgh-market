Resilience is the ability to recognise, adapt to, and absorb disturbance
without a serious drop in performance, and **diversity supplies the raw
material for that ability** — a richer set of perspectives means a richer
set of candidate responses when something unexpected happens, and protection
against over-rehearsed routines that only work for the situations they were
rehearsed against. Wildavsky's "decentralised anticipation" names the same
finding from [high-reliability organization
theory](high-reliability-organizations.md): dispersed, entrepreneurial
safety effort outperforms centralised top-down policy, because the
authority (and duty) to act on a bad feeling is distributed to whoever is
closest to it, including the most junior person present.

**Diversity is not the same as headcount.** Adding people to a decision does
not automatically add perspectives if their influence is unevenly weighted —
one dominant voice with high status can make a five-person room functionally
a one-person decision. Concentration of decision-making influence can be
measured the way market concentration is measured (an index summing the
square of each participant's share of influence): a room where two roughly
equal peers make the call is diverse; the same room becomes concentrated the
moment a high-status expert's opinion effectively decides the outcome
regardless of headcount, and concentrates further if a still more senior
authority is then brought in and simply defers to. Training that explicitly
flattens hierarchy and rewards raising minority views (crew-resource
management style) is what actually moves this number toward diversity — a
room's *nominal* seniority mix says nothing about its *effective* diversity
without knowing how it was trained to use it. This concentration pattern is
directly implicated in group decisions where a senior figure's confidence
effectively silenced dissenting technical voices before a launch or
go/no-go call.

**Two concrete levers for keeping diversity real:**

- **Deliberately engineered, protected dissent.** A voice explicitly licensed
  to say the unwelcome thing, and protected from the normal social cost of
  disagreeing with authority, counters the tendency of an aligned group to
  suppress exactly the input it most needs.
- **Rotating outsiders through for short tours.** Fresh eyes recalibrate
  insiders' sense of what counts as "normal," and having to explain routine
  practice to a newcomer forces insiders to articulate tacit assumptions
  they would otherwise never examine — the same mechanism [studying normal
  work](studying-normal-work.md) uses, applied to personnel rotation instead
  of interview technique.

**Diversity has a failure mode of its own: the dominant-logic trap.**
Promotion systems that reward whoever best embodies the organisation's
current priorities systematically select, cycle after cycle, for people who
already think alike — each promotion round further concentrates the
organisation's culture around the same trait, producing what looks like
diversity of people but is actually convergence of judgement (the political
version of this is called groupthink). Recruiting for a *type* of person —
even a type prized for "independence" or "creativity" — can produce this
same convergence if everyone recruited shares the same underlying
temperament and incentives. Diversity therefore has to be actively defended
against an organisation's own selection and promotion pressure, not just
established once.

Diversity also has a limit: in a genuinely time-critical moment, endless
exploration of alternatives prevents a system from ever settling on and
using an already-good option. The lever is for building [adaptive
capacity](resilience-as-adaptive-capacity.md) between crises, not for every
decision under time pressure.

**A regulatory inspection of Swedish nuclear power plants shows what
building diversity into a forum concretely requires**, beyond simply
inviting more departments. A daily operational meeting relied on as the
main defence against diffuse, ambiguous problems was dominated by a single
department's viewpoint; other relevant departments' attendance was
optional, and the representatives who did attend (including independent
safety staff) mostly sat as passive listeners rather than actively
contesting what was raised — a forum diverse on paper but concentrated in
practice, the same [concentration-versus-headcount
distinction](diversity-as-a-safety-resource.md) above. The fix implemented
after the finding had three concrete parts: making attendance from key
departments *mandatory* rather than optional; re-engineering the agenda so
representatives are explicitly required to surface vague symptoms and
unresolved anomalies rather than leaving raising them to individual
initiative; and **institutionalising the "opponent" role** — mandating that
some attendees function as explicit, structural devil's advocates whose job
is to challenge proposed decisions and ongoing analyses, rather than
relying on any individual choosing to volunteer for that socially costly
role unprompted. The underlying lesson generalises past nuclear-plant
meetings: establishing a forum is not the same as building diversity into
it — what determines whether the forum works is what information is
required to be raised, how discussion is structured to force engagement
with it, and how deliberately the diversity of competencies present is put
to use, not the forum's existence on an org chart.
