---
type: concept
title: Unfalsifiable Bias Attribution
description: >
  A bias label that could equally have been applied had the opposite outcome
  occurred explains nothing about the actual decision — it only redescribes
  a bad outcome in psychological-sounding language.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power (Klein), ch. 6"
---

After the USS Vincennes shot down a civilian airliner it had misidentified
as an attacking fighter, official reviewers concluded the crew had suffered
"scenario fulfillment" and **expectancy bias** — seeing what they expected
to see rather than the actual data. Klein's objection is not that expectancy
effects are unreal, but that the label was applied in a way no possible
outcome could have avoided: crews at the time were operating on a base rate
in which the overwhelming majority of similarly challenged aircraft turned
out to be hostile military aircraft. Had the crew instead *not* fired, and
been struck by a genuine attacker, the same reviewers would just as readily
have accused them of **ignoring known base rates**. Acting on expectancies
and being wrong is "expectancy bias"; ignoring expectancies and being wrong
is "failure to use base rates" — there is no decision that survives both
verdicts, which means the verdict is being generated by the outcome, not by
anything analysable in the decision itself.

**The test for whether a bias attribution is doing real explanatory work**:
ask whether the opposite decision, followed by the opposite (bad) outcome,
would have drawn a *different* bias label from the same critique. If any
outcome can be retrofitted with some bias name, the label is not identifying
a flaw in the reasoning — it is [hindsight bias](hindsight-bias.md) wearing
technical vocabulary. This is the same failure mode as [pseudo-explanations
like "loss of situation awareness" and
"complacency"](complacency-and-loss-of-situation-awareness.md): a
construct invoked after the fact that restates the bad outcome rather than
explaining the reasoning that produced it.

A concrete, traceable mechanism was in fact available for the misread
altitude: [team preconscious knowledge](team-preconscious-knowledge.md) —
some crew members held the corrected track number while others queried the
stale one, and the mismatch never surfaced into the crew's shared
understanding before the fatal decision.

A related caution applies to the underlying research base for these labels:
many classic decision-bias findings were produced by lab tasks specifically
designed so a heuristic would fail, using deliberately unrepresentative
stimuli, and a number of the most-cited biases (including confirmation bias)
shrink or disappear once real context is added or once the subjects are
actual domain experts rather than novices on an artificial task. A bias
label imported wholesale from that literature onto an experienced
practitioner's field decision is borrowing more authority than the
underlying research supports.

Before accepting a post-incident bias diagnosis, check it against a concrete
alternative account of the same event that traces the belief to a specific,
traceable mechanism — a genuinely broken instrument reading, a mislabelled
data track, a display with no trend indicator — rather than a psychological
label with no independent evidence behind it. A traceable mechanism is
falsifiable and actionable (fix the instrument, add the indicator); a bias
label usually is neither.
