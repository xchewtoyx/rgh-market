---
type: concept
title: Epistemic Distance and the Sweet Spot for Organisational Learning
description: >
  How much an investigator can actually learn from an accident depends on
  their distance from it along a spectrum from emotionally overwhelmed
  proximity to sterile, decontextualised remoteness — with a "second
  stories" sweet spot in between that neither extreme reaches.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering: Concepts and Precepts (Hollnagel, Woods, Leveson), ch. 20"
---

Immediately after a high-consequence failure, normal production goals
pause, the lines dividing stakeholders thin, and resources refocus on
inquiry. Where a given reviewer sits relative to that inquiry — their
**epistemic distance from the epicentre** — determines what kind of
knowledge they can actually extract, and the relationship is not "closer is
always better":

- **At the epicentre**: the workers and participants directly involved are
  emotionally devastated and overwhelmed by the immediate consequences.
  Their access to what happened is unmatched, but their capacity to analyse
  it dispassionately in the moment is compromised by exactly that access.
- **Far distant from the epicentre**: reviewers arrive divorced from the
  complex technical context, dependent on skeletal accounts — Cook et al.'s
  "**first stories**" — and default to oversimplified, sterile
  explanations that fit the little they were given.
- **Near the epicentre — the sweet spot**: personnel with enough detailed
  technical context to understand the work, but enough distance from the
  immediate emotional and operational fallout to examine it. This position
  is what makes it possible to dig past the first story into the
  "**second story**": how safety was actually being created, day to day,
  before the event that made it visible.

**Both extremes fail for reasons beyond distance alone.** Systemic
complexity plus [hindsight bias](hindsight-bias.md) makes it easy for a
reviewer working from a first story to stop investigating the moment a
single individual or group can be selected as culprit — a "stop-and-blame"
outcome that looks like closure but forecloses the second story entirely.
Reviewers closer to the epicentre face a different, non-technical set of
pressures pulling them toward the same premature closure: their own
emotional distress, stakeholder demand for a fast resolution that lets
operations resume, the organisation's need to manage financial and legal
exposure, and the genuine psychological cost of confronting cognitive
dissonance by revising a mental model of how the operation works that the
event just proved wrong.

**What a genuinely second-story-level investigation surfaces looks nothing
like what a first-story investigation reports.** A formal internal
investigation constrained to a short mandated turnaround produced only
superficial, local findings (adding a reactant label inside a machine) and
treated safety as a dedicated department's problem, disconnected from daily
clean-room realities, with errors framed as originating in workers rather
than in the conditions those workers worked under. A slower, second-story
review of the same event surfaced two systemic traps entirely invisible to
the first: **procedural overload**, where detailed checklists forced
workers to juggle multiple concurrent procedures with no guidance on how to
sequence them under real time pressure — a live instance of the
[predictability and controllability assumptions procedures quietly rest
on](procedures-as-resources-for-action.md) — and a **buddy-system
breakdown**, where a mandated two-worker safety check failed not because
either worker was careless but because the machine's physical layout put
them on opposite sides of it, and the task itself required one to watch a
screen while the other executed steps, making the direct observation the
procedure assumed structurally impossible to perform as written.

The practical implication for who should investigate: deliberately
positioning reviewers at the epistemic sweet spot — technically fluent
enough to understand the work, distant enough from the immediate emotional
and political fallout to examine it — is a design choice about who
conducts an investigation, not an accident of who happens to be available.
