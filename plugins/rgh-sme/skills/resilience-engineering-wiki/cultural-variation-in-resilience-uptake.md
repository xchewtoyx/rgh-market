type: concept
title: Cultural Variation in Resilience Uptake
description: >
  Acceptance of resilience-engineering ideas over traditional safety
  assumptions varies systematically by national and occupational culture,
  and practitioners hold both paradigms simultaneously and inconsistently
  rather than committing to one — so RE cannot be rolled out as a
  one-size-fits-all message.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering in Practice"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering in Practice (Hollnagel, Pariès, Wreathall), ch. 18"
---

A 705-respondent survey of European and Americas aviation professionals
(the Safety Assumptions and Resilient Attitudes, or SARA, instrument) gauged
agreement with traditional safety assumptions to indirectly locate
respondents on a spectrum between [the Old View and New View of human
error](old-view-vs-new-view-of-human-error.md) — named in this study
**technological optimism** (humans as a liability whose variability must be
constrained; things go right because systems, procedures, and training were
correct) versus **technological realism** (humans as the asset that makes
technical systems work at all; things go right because operators overcome
design flaws, adapt performance to context, and catch anomalies the system
itself would have missed).

**Two independent factors drove significant variation, and neither
interacted with the other**: country of residence (societal culture) and
profession (occupational culture).

- **By country**: Northern Europe showed the strongest rejection of
  traditional assumptions; Latin America and Southern Europe (Italy, Spain,
  Portugal) showed the strongest agreement with them, with disagreement
  roughly tracking latitude across European nations — though the pattern is
  not purely geographic (Spanish respondents scored closer to France than
  to Italy, despite the two countries' geographic proximity).
- **By profession**: human-factors and safety specialists rejected
  traditional assumptions most strongly; aircraft maintenance engineers
  agreed with them most strongly, with pilots, air traffic controllers, and
  general engineers falling between the two.

**Practitioners do not hold one coherent paradigm — they hold both, and
switch between them without noticing.** Overall agreement scores clustered
near neutral with high internal variance, and individual interviewees
routinely voiced directly contradictory positions within a single
conversation — asserting that following rules assures total safety in one
breath and that following rules does not guarantee safety in the next.
Rather than a single theoretical commitment, practitioners opportunistically
retrieve fragmented folk wisdom, traditional assumptions, and resilient
concepts depending on immediate context, goals, or desired social
positioning — an organisational-scale version of the same pattern shows up
when airlines revise incident-investigation manuals by simply combining
multiple published methodologies on the unexamined assumption that using
more expert methods makes a finding "more right," without checking whether
the underlying models are even compatible with each other (see [your
accident model shapes what you find](accident-model-shapes-what-you-find.md)
for why they usually are not).

**The practical implication for anyone trying to move an organisation
toward resilience engineering**: because acceptance varies by both national
and occupational culture, and because even receptive practitioners apply
concepts eclectically rather than systematically, a single message or
training programme delivered uniformly across a multinational,
multi-occupational workforce will land completely differently in each
subgroup it reaches — the intervention has to be tailored to both job type
and societal culture, not authored once and broadcast everywhere. The
finding is not that aviation practitioners resist resilience thinking
(overall openness was closer to neutral than hostile); it is that RE
advocates cannot assume consistent application even where openness exists,
which puts the harder burden on translating theory into memorable, usable
tools rather than on winning the argument for the theory itself.
