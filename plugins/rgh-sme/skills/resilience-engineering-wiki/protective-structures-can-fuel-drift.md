---
type: concept
title: Protective Structures Can Fuel Drift
description: >
  Regulators, auditors, and internal risk functions are embedded in the same
  web of local pressures as the operation they oversee, so they can actively
  contribute to drift rather than merely fail to catch it.
sources:
  - title: "Drift into Failure: From Hunting Broken Components to Understanding Complex Systems"
    resource: "Drift into Failure (Dekker), ch. 1"
---

A structure built to protect against [drift into
failure](drift-into-failure.md) — a regulator, a ratings agency, an internal
audit or risk function — is itself part of the complex system, subject to the
same [local rationality](local-rationality-principle.md) and [goal
conflicts](goal-conflicts-and-production-pressure.md) as the operation it
watches. It does not stand outside the system looking in; it is one more node
whose local, sensible decisions can add to the drift rather than arrest it.

This happens through mechanisms that look reasonable at each step, not through
capture or corruption alone:

- **Mandate conflict**: a regulator tasked with both promoting an activity and
  policing it (e.g. an agency that both encourages an industry's output and
  collects revenue from it while also enforcing safety) carries the same
  [authority-responsibility
  mismatch](authority-responsibility-mismatch.md) it is supposed to prevent
  elsewhere.
- **Thin oversight capacity relative to scope**: when the ratio of inspectors,
  reviewers, or auditors to the systems they cover is small, oversight
  degrades from direct verification to paperwork review and self-reporting —
  a change usually made incrementally, each step locally defensible.
- **Delegated self-certification**: letting the regulated party draft its own
  compliance or risk documentation (which the overseer then approves with
  minimal independent rework) transfers the safety-relevant judgment back to
  the party with the strongest local incentive to normalise the deviant
  practice — see [normalisation of
  deviance](normalization-of-deviance.md).

The practical implication for anyone assessing an organisation's safety
posture: the existence of a named oversight function is not evidence that
drift is being resisted. Ask instead whether that function's own resourcing,
mandate, and incentives are drifting in the same direction as the operation
it watches.

A further complication is that the protective structure itself usually has
no clean boundary: rule-making bodies, review boards, and the industry they
oversee blend together (personnel move routinely between regulator and
regulated; industry steering committees, often regulator-chaired, write the
guidance that oversight bodies then apply), so it can be genuinely
impossible to say where "the system that makes the rules" begins or ends.
Rules in this setting are not fixed reference points that incoming data gets
checked against; they are themselves the constantly renegotiated product of
the same web of relationships, meaning that when field data conflicts with
an existing rule, the *meaning of the data* is what gets renegotiated, not
necessarily the rule — a specific instance of [unruly
technology](unruly-technology.md)'s data-reinterpretation move operating at
the institutional level. In Wynne's summary: practices do not follow rules;
rules follow evolving practices.

A regulator or auditor also faces a structural bind that pure enforcement
framings ignore: it must be enough of an **outsider** to keep critical
distance and credibility, yet enough of an **insider** to have the domain
knowledge needed to notice weak signals and be taken seriously by the
operator. A purely external inspector misses what matters; a fully
co-opted, insider-only oversight body loses the [diversity of
perspective](diversity-as-a-safety-resource.md) that oversight exists to
supply. The more workable reframing is a **co-evolver / counter-evolver**
role rather than an enforcer of static compliance: continuously updating its
own understanding alongside the operation's evolving practice (co-evolving),
while actively generating plausible alternative outcomes to the small steps
the operation is currently taking, so risk can surface before it locks in as
accepted practice (counter-evolving) — rather than auditing current practice
against a rulebook that practice has already outrun.
