---
type: concept
title: Bounded Rationality and Role Perspective
description: >
  People occupying a position in a system reliably adopt that position's
  characteristic, locally rational view of the world — which is why
  blaming the individual who currently holds the role rarely improves
  outcomes and redesigning the system usually does.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking in Systems: A Primer"
    resource: "Thinking in Systems (Meadows), ch. 4"
---

Herbert Simon's bounded rationality: people make reasonable decisions given
the information they actually have, which is always incomplete, especially
about distant parts of the system they sit inside. A fisherman doesn't know
the total fish population or every other boat's same-day catch; a business
doesn't know competitors' investment plans or the total market size. People
are not omniscient optimisers but "satisficers" — they find a locally
tolerable choice within their limited view and stick with it until forced
to reconsider, compounded by systematically misjudging risk, over-weighting
recent experience over long-term history, and discounting the future.

The generalising move beyond any one decision: **the bounded-rational view
is a property of the *position*, not the person occupying it.** Swap the
person and the same position produces the same characteristic view —
management sees labour as a cost to minimise regardless of who manages;
a mortgaged fisherman overfishes regardless of who holds the mortgage;
classroom role-play games (a fishery, a development ministry) and the
Stanford Prison Experiment all demonstrate people rapidly absorbing the
outlook their assigned position supplies, largely independent of their
prior views or character. The practical conclusion: "blaming the individual
rarely helps create a more desirable outcome... change comes first from
stepping outside the limited information that can be seen from any single
place in the system and getting an overview" — putting a new actor into an
unchanged system reliably reproduces the old system's behaviour, because the
position, not the person, is doing the work.

This is the systemic, forward-looking analogue of the [local rationality
principle](local-rationality-principle.md): local rationality asks an
investigator to reconstruct why a specific action made sense to the
specific person who took it, after the fact; bounded rationality asks a
designer to anticipate, before the fact, that *anyone* who occupies that
position will reason from the same restricted view — so the fix for a
systematically bad decision is changing what information, incentives, and
constraints the position supplies, not replacing the person in it. Both
converge on the same conclusion reliability engineering keeps arriving at
from different directions: [the authority-responsibility
mismatch](authority-responsibility-mismatch.md) exists because a role's
information and incentives, not its occupant's competence, are usually the
actual constraint on what "the right decision" could have been.
