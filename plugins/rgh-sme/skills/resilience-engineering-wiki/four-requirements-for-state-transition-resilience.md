title: Four Requirements for State-Transition Resilience
description: >
  Managing a transition between organisational functional states requires
  recognising the trigger, having a defined transition routine, having the
  receiving state actually ready to operate, and being able to rebound back
  — and the 2004 Swedish Foreign Department response to the Sumatra tsunami
  shows what happens when the first requirement fails.
sources:

---

Moving successfully between the functional states in [the state-space model
of organisational functioning](state-space-model-of-organizational-functioning.md)
requires four capabilities together, not any one alone:

1. **Recognition of triggering conditions** — the capability to diagnose,
   assess, and anticipate that the environment has actually changed enough
   to warrant a state transition at all. Recognition can be blocked by
   organisational bureaucracy, cognitive biases, vested interests in the
   current state, or a genuine data deficit.
2. **Defined transition rules and routines** — clear change-management
   processes, established command lines, roles that can actually be
   reassigned, and scripts specifying how the system shifts modes, prepared
   in advance rather than improvised at the moment of transition.
3. **Readiness in the receiving state** — the physical facilities,
   communication channels, human capital, and operational reserves the new
   state requires have to already exist and be available on entry, not be
   assembled after the transition has nominally happened.
4. **Rebound and reversion capability** — the procedures and structural
   capacity to dismantle emergency operations and return to normal (or a
   deliberately revised normal), including absorbing or idling the
   resources an emergency mobilisation pulled in once they are no longer
   needed.

**The 2004 Sumatra tsunami** shows a real-time failure at the first
requirement specifically. The Swedish Foreign Department received initial
disaster notifications early on 26 December 2004 and remained stuck in
**regular reduced functioning** — its scheduled holiday-staffing state — for
over 24 hours, because it lacked a transition routine, its staff had no
geographic familiarity with the affected region (Phuket among the
locations), and the department failed to register the disaster's actual
scale despite ongoing public news coverage. Frozen inside its own procedures
and with roles not adapted to the situation, the department initially
turned down offers of external assistance, including from national police —
readiness and rebound capability never came into play because recognition
never triggered the transition that would have activated them. Charter
travel companies, police, and emergency rescue agencies, by contrast,
transitioned into high-alert operating modes within hours, suspending
normal functions to concentrate resources on the response — the same
triggering information reached every organisation roughly simultaneously,
so the divergence traces specifically to which organisations had a working
recognition capability and a rehearsed transition routine ready to fire.

**Transition timing has its own trade-off.** Reactive transitions wait for
post-hoc detection of an environmental shift — resilient organisations work
to minimise the unavoidable lag between the shift occurring and it being
detected, but cannot eliminate it. Proactive transitions act on early cues
to anticipate a shift before it is confirmed, reducing or eliminating that
lag — at the cost of operating under genuinely higher uncertainty, with a
real risk of triggering a premature or simply wrong state change. Neither
mode dominates the other; which one an organisation should favour for a
given trigger depends on how costly a false-positive transition would be
relative to how costly the detection lag itself would be.
