---
type: concept
title: Explanatory Factors vs Change Factors
description: >
  Explaining why a past event happened and finding the levers that prevent
  the next one are different analytic products — the best change factor is
  often not among the explanatory factors at all.
sources:
  - title: The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error'
    resource: "The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error' (Dekker), ch. 3"
---

- **Explanatory factors** account for why a specific historical event
  occurred: the lighting, the time pressure, where a hand was placed.
- **Change factors** are the systemic levers that prevent recurrence and
  improve the operation going forward.

The two are not the same list, and treating explanation as automatically
yielding prevention is a quiet form of the [root-cause
fallacy](root-cause-fallacy.md). The haul-truck case makes the distinction
concrete: mechanics' hand injuries were "explained" by carelessness and poor
lighting — but the change factor emerged from pattern analysis: 80% of
injuries occurred during *unscheduled roadside repairs*. Investing in
preventive maintenance so work happened in sheds eliminated the injury
pattern. No amount of lighting improvement or carefulness exhortation at
the explanatory level would have done that.

Implications:

- After explaining the event ([why it made
  sense](local-rationality-principle.md)), run a second, separate analysis:
  where does the *pattern* live, and what structural lever moves it? The
  lever frequently sits at the [blunt end](sharp-end-and-blunt-end.md) —
  scheduling, resourcing, design — far from the event's proximal details.
- Recommendations aimed only at explanatory factors reproduce the classic
  weak fixes — retrain, remind, [add a
  checker](social-redundancy-fallacy.md) — the signature of the [quick-fix
  fallacy](fallacy-of-the-quick-fix.md).
- A change factor that adds a component (a barrier, a checklist step, a
  dedicated team) is not automatically safe: it can [increase overall
  complexity risk](redundancy-can-increase-complexity-risk.md) by creating
  new relationships elsewhere in the system that nobody is watching.
- Recommendations should still be actionable: specific about the
  organisational unit and action, measurable, agreed with operational
  realities, realistic within constraints, and time-bound (SMART) — vague
  "improve safety culture" recommendations are change factors in name only.
