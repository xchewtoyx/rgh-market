title: The Accident-Proneness Myth
description: >

  method-of-percentages fallacy — statistics that ignore unequal exposure to
  risk — and was statistically discredited by the 1940s–50s.
sources:

Early 20th-century safety literature (Tolman & Kendall 1913; Marbe 1925;
Farmer 1925) held that certain workers were inherently "accident-prone" and
recommended psychometric screening or termination — the ancestor of [Bad
Apple theory](old-view-vs-new-view-of-human-error.md).

Its statistical foundation is the **method-of-percentages fallacy**: claims
like "27% of Boston drivers cause 55% of accidents" or "3% of doctors
generate 49% of complaints" fail to control for **exposure to risk**. Drivers
assigned dense urban routes face far higher risk than rural drivers;
paediatric oncological surgeons face vastly higher baseline complication and
mortality rates than general practitioners. Skewed outcome distributions are
what unequal exposure *looks like*, no personal trait required.

Arbous & Kerrich (1951) showed accident-proneness lacked predictive validity,
and WWII-era technology made the deeper point unmissable: error rates are a
function of machine interface, task design, and environment — not personal
traits. The productive question shifted permanently from *who* is responsible
to *what* is responsible.

The modern residue: when a practitioner repeatedly struggles, treat it as a
**mismatch between person, task, and system**. Resolving that mismatch —
through selection, task redesign, and continuous retraining — is an
organisational responsibility, not an individual moral failure. Reaching
instead for termination re-runs the myth and [suppresses
reporting](blame-suppresses-reporting.md) besides.
