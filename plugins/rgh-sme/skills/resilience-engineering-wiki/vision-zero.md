  rather than the systemic inputs that produce it, driving data falsification,
  moral stigmatisation, and misallocated investigation effort.

"Vision Zero" (zero harm, zero accidents) is an ethical commitment mistaken
for an achievable, manageable target. Its scientific error is categorical:
safety outcomes are a **dependent variable**. Management can only manipulate
independent variables — resourcing, equipment, staffing, design, [goal
trade-offs](goal-conflicts-and-production-pressure.md) — and outcomes follow.
Setting a target on the dependent variable itself invites managing the
number instead of the system:

- **Falsification and numbers games**: a power-plant contractor's safety
  manager was sentenced to 6.5 years in prison for hiding over 80 severe
  injuries to collect $2.5M in safety bonuses. (The incentive mechanics are
  those of [behaviour-based safety](behavior-based-safety.md).)
- **Moral stigmatisation**: declaring all error morally unacceptable turns
  practitioners involved in incidents into second victims, isolated and
  guilt-ridden without support (see [just culture](just-culture.md)).
- **Investigation waste**: if all harm is preventable, all harm must be
  investigated — paper cuts and rolled ankles drain the resources needed to
  analyse complex process risks.

The contrast case that sharpens the critique is Alcoa under Paul O'Neill
(1987–1997). O'Neill also declared a zero-injury goal — but implemented it
as learning infrastructure, not as a target on the metric: personal
notification of every injury within 24 hours, explicitly *not* to punish
but to capture and incorporate learnings. Injuries fell 95% in a decade,
and reporting then *expanded* to close calls. The difference from the
Vision Zero failure mode is exactly which variable was managed: O'Neill
attached leadership attention to the flow of bad news (independent-variable
work — see [blame suppresses reporting](blame-suppresses-reporting.md)),
where bonus-and-target schemes attach consequences to the number itself.

Underneath the target-on-the-metric version sits the assumption that
suppressing small negatives prevents
large ones — the [Heinrich triangle myth](heinrich-triangle-myth.md), whose
empirical refutations show low reported-incident rates *co-occurring with
higher* fatality rates. A zero-harm scoreboard reading zero is at least as
consistent with suppressed reporting as with safety; treating it as safety
evidence is exactly the overconfidence [chronic
unease](chronic-unease.md) exists to resist.
