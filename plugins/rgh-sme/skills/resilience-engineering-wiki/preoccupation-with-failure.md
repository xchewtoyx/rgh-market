title: Preoccupation with Failure
description: >
  The first HRO anticipation principle: treating any lapse, near miss, or weak
  signal as a possible symptom of deeper system trouble rather than as noise
  to be smoothed over after success.
sources:
  - title: Managing the Unexpected
    resource: "Managing the Unexpected (Weick, Sutcliffe), ch. 1, ch. 4"
---

Preoccupation with failure is the first of five [high-reliability
organization](high-reliability-organizations.md) principles and one of three
**anticipation** practices (with [reluctance to simplify](reluctance-to-simplify.md)
and [sensitivity to operations](sensitivity-to-operations.md)). HROs embrace
failure in two senses: they pay close attention to weak signals that may
symptomize larger problems, and they spell out mistakes people must not dare
make — building ongoing attention to both into practice.

At Cerro Grande, preoccupation was violated when small failures piled up
without strong response: escaped prescribed burns nearby, an active inner
blackline edge, skeleton crew after midnight, dispatch delays, and budget
stalemates over contingency resources. At Davis-Besse (2002), maintenance
staff found rust clogging AC and water filters so badly that filters were
changed every two days for two years — versus an industry norm of once a
month — yet no one framed this as "a mistake we can't make." Wildland fire
foreman Mark Linane: "The pucker factor goes up when you get out of camp
late" — a late start means hotter, drier, windier conditions by arrival.

The key variable is how long problematic conditions persist: the longer they
persist, the less predictable and controllable system interactions become.
Earlier discrepancies are harder to spot but offer more options. Detection
can start with a feeling, a checklist of shaky expectations (recent
supervisory changes, missed procedure steps, staff spread thin), or James
Reason's three interface questions: which activities involve the most direct
human contact, which pose the greatest risk if performed inadequately, and
how often are they performed.

Reporting is as important as detection — people need to feel safe reporting
or they cover up incidents ([blame suppresses
reporting](blame-suppresses-reporting.md)). Amy Edmondson found the
highest-performing nursing units had *higher detected* adverse drug events,
not more errors — a climate of openness. Wernher von Braun sent champagne to
an engineer who confessed a possible Redstone short-circuit; a seaman on the
Carl Vinson was commended after reporting a lost deck tool that grounded all
aircraft until it was found.

HRO interest differs from the [Swiss cheese model](swiss-cheese-model.md):
the concern is the *process* of defense holes lining up — each moment one
hole aligns with another is a failed expectation and an opportunity to stop
progression toward a [brutal audit](brutal-audit.md). Success has its own
liabilities — overconfidence, narrowed perception, entrenchment of "the one
way we do things," reduced acceptance of opposing views — which [chronic
unease](chronic-unease.md) and preoccupation with failure counteract.
Bill Starbuck and Frances Milliken on Challenger: "Success breeds confidence
and fantasy."

On aircraft carriers, every landing is graded ship-wide; near misses are
debriefed within the hour with written accounts of what each person saw
beforehand. Small failures (a plane out of position, repeated failure to
snag the third wire) are treated as signs of potential larger problems.
