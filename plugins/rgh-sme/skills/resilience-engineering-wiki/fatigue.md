Fatigue is driven by circadian desynchronisation, sleep deprivation, time on
task, and sustained exertion. Its cognitive signature:

- **Vigilance lapses** — slower reactions, missed signals.
- **Cognitive slowing** — impaired arithmetic, reasoning, and option
  generation.
- **Lapsing / micro-sleeps** — unresponsive periods of ten seconds or more.
- **Impaired self-assessment** — fatigue degrades the very faculty needed to
  judge one's own impairment, so "I'm fine to continue" is unreliable
  precisely when it matters.

The self-assessment point makes fatigue a *systemic* responsibility: an
organisation cannot delegate fatigue management to the fatigued. Effective
remediation is roster and schedule redesign and sleep-opportunity management
— structural [blunt-end](sharp-end-and-blunt-end.md) decisions — not
vigilance slogans or caffeine, which are the classic try-harder
countermeasure of the [Old View](old-view-vs-new-view-of-human-error.md).

For investigations, fatigue is a context to establish (hours awake, shift
history, circadian phase), not a label to apply: it shapes what was
noticeable and thinkable inside the tunnel ([local
rationality](local-rationality-principle.md)), and schedules that produced
it usually trace back to [production
pressure](goal-conflicts-and-production-pressure.md).

**The relationship between alertness and actual operational risk is
U-shaped, not linear** — high alertness is not simply "safer" than
moderate alertness. Low alertness directly impairs cognitive capability and
reaction time, raising risk as expected. But *high* alertness carries its
own elevated risk, through overconfidence and reduced self-monitoring —
feeling sharp lowers the felt need to double-check. Risk is lowest at
*medium* alertness specifically, where operators are alert enough to
process information well but still uncertain enough of their own state to
actively self-monitor and engage controlled, deliberate processing rather
than confident, fast processing. In team-based automated environments —
commercial cockpits are the studied case — crews partially compensate for
this by deploying deliberate countermeasures under fatigue: increased
cross-checking and heavier reliance on automated systems, both of which are
explicit attempts to substitute external verification for degraded
internal self-assessment.

This is why static, regulated duty-hour limits routinely fail to track real
risk: a fixed cap treats a roster as safe or unsafe based on hours alone,
blind to where on the U-shaped curve a given crew actually sits at a given
moment. See [managed safety vs regulated
safety](managed-safety-vs-regulated-safety.md) for the alternative
regulatory framework built specifically to evaluate fatigue risk on its own
non-linear terms instead of against a blanket threshold.
