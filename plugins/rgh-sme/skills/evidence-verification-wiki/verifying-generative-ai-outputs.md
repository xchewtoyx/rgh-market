# Verifying Generative AI Outputs

Generative AI tools can usefully stimulate exploration — surfacing search terms, framings, or candidate leads a researcher hadn't considered — but their outputs are not themselves evidence and require the same verification any other unsourced claim would need before being relied upon.

## The "Magic Box" Framing
A useful way to calibrate how much verification an AI output needs: treat any tool whose behavior is neither understood nor controlled nor otherwise known to be safe as a "magic box," and scale scrutiny to how strongly magical it is. Generative AI is strongly magical — even its own developers cannot reliably predict or explain a given output, its behavior is stochastic (the same prompt can yield different answers on different runs), and it can be radically fragile (small, seemingly unrelated changes to a prompt or the underlying model produce unforeseeable shifts in output). This argues for treating every output as needing independent verification proportional to how much the claim matters, not for trusting a system merely because it "usually" performs well.

## Why This Is True by Construction, Not Just in Practice
The need for independent verification isn't a limitation current models happen to have and might outgrow — it follows from what a generative language model is trained to do. Training rewards a model for producing the statistically most likely continuation of a document given everything trained on (books, articles, forum posts, code), not for producing verified truth; in this sense the model is a training-data mimic, not a fact-lookup engine. Nothing in that training objective gives the model an internal category for "things I actually know" versus "things that are merely plausible-sounding" — from the model's own perspective, a fabricated citation and a real one, or a fabricated URL or identifier and a real one, are generated by the identical process and can come out equally confident and equally well-formed, because emulating the surface patterns of real facts, sources, and identifiers is exactly what the training process optimized for. This is why a directive like "don't make stuff up" has limited effect: the model has no internal signal distinguishing the case where it's complying from the case where it isn't. It is also why fluency and confident tone carry no evidential weight (see the bullet above) — confidence is a stylistic feature of the completion, not a report on how the underlying claim was produced.

Because hallucinations are indistinguishable from other completions from the model's own perspective, the practical mitigation is to demand outputs that carry their own checkable structure — a stated chain of reasoning, a calculation that can be redone, a source link, or specific searchable details — rather than a bare conclusion. "There was an English king who married his cousin" is nearly impossible to verify as stated; "George IV, who married Caroline of Brunswick" gives a reviewer somewhere concrete to check. See [defining checkable claims](defining-checkable-claims.md) for the general discipline this is an instance of. A related, distinct hazard is [truth bias in LLM completions](truth-bias-in-llm-completions.md): a model will typically continue rather than flag a false premise embedded in its own prompt, which is a separate failure from confident fabrication and needs separate handling. For the human-cognition analog — confident, coherent explanations generated without the source realizing they're inventing rather than recalling — see [confabulation in testimony](confabulation-in-testimony.md).

## Verification Action
- Treat every factual assertion, citation, or quotation produced by a generative AI system as an unverified claim requiring independent confirmation against a real primary or reliable secondary source — never cite the AI output itself as the source for a factual claim.
- Be specifically alert to fabricated citations and quotations: generative systems can produce plausible-looking references to sources that do not exist or do not say what's attributed to them.
- Disclose honestly and transparently where and how AI assistance was used in producing a document, rather than presenting AI-assisted work as if it were produced and verified entirely independently.
- Form your own answer *before* consulting the AI output ("me first"), so your judgment isn't anchored by whatever the system produced.
- Don't let expressed confidence or agreeable tone stand in for evidence — a system can be fluent and flattering while wrong; look for the actual data or reasoning backing an answer, not how assertively it's stated.
- Check stability: ask the same question a different way and see whether the substance of the answer holds up or drifts — instability under rephrasing is itself a signal the original answer wasn't well grounded.
