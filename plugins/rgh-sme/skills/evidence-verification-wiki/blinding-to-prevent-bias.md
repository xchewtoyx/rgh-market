# Blinding to Prevent Bias

**Blinding** removes a source of contamination in a test by preventing the subject (or the experimenter, in a double-blind design) from knowing which condition is in effect, so neither party's expectation can influence the outcome or its recording.

## Worked Example: Emily Rosa's Therapeutic Touch Test
A 9-year-old's science-fair experiment tested practitioners' claim that they could feel a patient's "energy field" by hand. Design: a screen with hand-sized holes separated experimenter and practitioner; the practitioner placed both hands through the holes, out of sight; the experimenter randomly chose (coin flip) which hand to hover over, and the practitioner had to guess which hand was selected, unable to see. Across 280 trials, practitioners scored 44% — statistically indistinguishable from the 50% chance baseline (95% CI of ±6%). The design isolated exactly the one claim under test (can they detect the field at all) without needing to adjudicate the broader healing claim, and cost about $10 total. It was later published in JAMA.

**Retroactive-excuse countermeasure**: because a losing subject may claim after the fact that the test conditions were unfair (wrong distance, wrong protocol), a rigorous blinding design has subjects sign an affidavit *before* the test affirming the conditions are fair and that they won't dispute a failing result afterward — removing the option to relitigate methodology only once the outcome is known.

## Why This Matters for Verification
Blinding is not a specialist, expensive technique reserved for clinical trials — the elements (a plausible mechanism for randomization, concealment of the assigned condition from whoever is being tested or whoever is recording the result, and pre-commitment to the test's fairness) can be assembled cheaply for many claims that would otherwise be taken on faith. A claim that "could be tested but hasn't been" because testing sounds expensive or technical is worth checking against this bar before accepting the claim as unverifiable in practice.

## Blinding the Analyst, Not Just the Subject
Blinding applies just as directly to whoever *analyzes* results as to whoever generates them. Physicists have found that researchers who know which hypothesis their analysis is meant to support unconsciously bias analytic choices (which data points to exclude, which statistical method to prefer) toward confirming it, even when trying to be objective — a phenomenon observable even in a field with a strong culture of rigor. The corrective, increasingly used in particle physics and cosmology, is **outcome-blind analysis**: the analyst works from data with the true result masked or randomized until the analysis method is fully locked in, so no methodological choice can be steered, even unconsciously, toward a preferred answer. The same discipline can be applied informally to any review: describe a decision, plan, or case to an evaluator without revealing its outcome or the reviewer's own prior verdict on it, and ask for an assessment of the process alone — this is markedly more disorienting than it sounds, precisely because people are used to letting the known outcome color their evaluation of the process that produced it.

## Verification Action
When a claim rests on a subject's or evaluator's own unblinded judgment (an expert who knows which product/version/candidate they're evaluating, a self-assessed outcome), check whether a blinded test was possible and, if not run, treat the claim as weaker for the missing methodological safeguard rather than fully accepted.
