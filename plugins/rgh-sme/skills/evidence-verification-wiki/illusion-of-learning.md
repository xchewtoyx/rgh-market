# Illusion of Learning

People generally feel their judgment improves with experience — but rarely track their own hit rate over time, and selectively remember flattering anecdotes over disconfirming ones, so the *feeling* of improved judgment can be entirely disconnected from actual improvement. Robyn Dawes termed this the "illusion of learning."

## Documented Cases Where Confidence Rose While Accuracy Fell or Stayed Flat
- Horse-racing experts given progressively more data grew more confident in their picks, while actual prediction accuracy plateaued and then degraded as data volume increased.
- Collaborating with more people improved group decisions only up to a point, beyond which decision quality slightly worsened — yet confidence kept climbing regardless of group size.
- As individual investors gathered more market information, their confidence rose while portfolio performance worsened, plausibly from overreacting to each new piece of news.
- Subjects given formal lie-detection training became more confident in their judgments of truthfulness while performing *worse* at actually detecting lies than untrained subjects.

## Why This Matters for Verification
A process that looks more structured, analytical, or data-intensive is not automatically more accurate — the added structure or data volume can produce a pure confidence effect (a kind of placebo) with zero or negative accuracy benefit. This generalizes beyond individual judgment to organizational "measurement" methods: an evaluation process that makes participants feel more rigorous (an elaborate weighted-scoring rubric, a longer committee discussion, more source documents reviewed) is not evidence the resulting judgment is any better, unless the process's actual track record has been checked against outcomes. The correct diagnostic question is not "does this feel more rigorous" but "has anyone actually measured whether this process's outputs are more accurate than a simpler alternative?" — see [statistical model track record vs. expert judgment](statistical-model-track-record-vs-expert-judgment.md) for the class of studies that actually run this check.

## Verification Action
When a draft or source cites the amount of data, experience, effort, or process formality behind a judgment as if that alone were evidence of its reliability, treat that as an unsupported inferential leap. Ask instead whether the judgment or process has ever been checked against a tracked outcome record — absent that check, more inputs or more elaborate process are not evidence of more accuracy, and can specifically correlate with unwarranted overconfidence.
