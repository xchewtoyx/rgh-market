# Claim Type Taxonomy

A claim's type determines what kind of evidence can actually support it. Verifying a claim starts with identifying which type it is, because evidence appropriate to one type does not automatically support another:
- **Factual**: asserts something is or was the case. Needs direct, checkable evidence (see [defining checkable claims](defining-checkable-claims.md)).
- **Definitional**: asserts something falls into (or should be understood as) a category. Needs evidence that the definition's criteria are actually met, and that the definition itself is the appropriate/accepted one to apply.
- **Causal**: asserts one thing produces or influences another. Needs evidence appropriate to causal inference, not mere association (see [correlation vs. causation](correlation-vs-causation.md)) — a document that supports a causal claim with only correlational evidence has an [evidence quality dimensions](evidence-quality-dimensions.md) mismatch (inappropriate evidence for the claim type), even if every individual fact cited is accurate.
- **Evaluative**: asserts something is good, bad, better, or worse relative to some criteria. Needs the evaluative criteria to be stated explicitly, plus evidence that the thing being evaluated actually meets or fails those criteria — an evaluative claim with unstated criteria can't be verified at all, only asserted.
- **Practical**: asserts what should be done. Needs evidence of feasibility and of the claimed consequences of acting (or not acting), not just evidence that a problem exists.

## Verification Action
Before checking a claim's supporting evidence, first identify its type. A well-sourced factual premise does not automatically license a causal, evaluative, or practical conclusion drawn from it — check that the type of evidence actually offered matches the type of claim being made, and flag a mismatch (e.g., causal language resting only on factual/associational evidence) even when every individual fact is independently accurate.
