# Cargo Cult Science

Richard Feynman's **cargo cult science** label describes work that looks scientific on the surface but lacks what makes science actually work. The metaphor comes from Pacific islanders who built fake runways and control towers hoping to lure back WWII supply planes — reproducing the *form* of aviation infrastructure without the underlying system that made planes land.

Pre–randomized-controlled-trial medicine is a canonical example: impressive titles, Latin lectures, and case reports created an aura of knowledge while most treatments were useless or harmful — historians could describe what physicians *believed* they were doing far more easily than whether those beliefs were *right*. The missing ingredient was not ceremony but **doubt**: treating conclusions as provisional until controlled tests could falsify them.

## Why Doubt Is the Operational Requirement
Feynman argued that scientific progress depends less on the rate of observations alone than on the rate at which new things are created *to test*. A field full of theories, assertions, and famous confident figures — but little experimentation — can look like nineteenth-century medicine or twenty-first-century forecasting: plenty of narrative authority, little independently reviewable evidence.

## Verification Action

When a document cites prestigious credentials, technical vocabulary, or voluminous case material as if that were verification, check whether any claim could actually have been **falsified** by the evidence shown. A pattern where every outcome is reinterpreted as confirming the authority's prior belief (recoveries prove the treatment works; deaths prove the case was incurable) is cargo cult reasoning, not evidence. Prefer sources that report what was tested, what would have counted as failure, and what independent checks were still pending — see [precommitted evaluation criteria](precommitted-evaluation-criteria.md) and [experimental vs. observational causal evidence](experimental-vs-observational-causal-evidence.md).
