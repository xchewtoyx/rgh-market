# Measurement as Uncertainty Reduction

Hubbard's operative definition: **measurement is a quantitatively expressed reduction of uncertainty based on one or more observations.** Only a *reduction* in uncertainty is required, not its elimination. This matters for verification because it resets the bar a reviewer holds a quantitative claim to — a figure doesn't have to be exact to be genuinely evidence-based, but it does have to actually derive from observation and come with some expression of its own uncertainty.

This framing draws on Claude Shannon's information theory (information as the amount of uncertainty a signal removes for a receiver who already holds some prior state of knowledge) and applies even to non-numeric-seeming (nominal/ordinal) questions, provided the *uncertainty itself* is expressed quantitatively — "85% chance we win the dispute" is a measurement of a yes/no question, not a violation of the scale.

## The No-Error-Bars Red Flag
A reported number presented with no range, confidence interval, or acknowledgment of its own uncertainty is a warning sign that it may not be a genuine empirical measurement at all — genuine measurements normally come with a stated range (e.g., "10%–18%, 95% confidence"). A figure that instead follows a fixed procedure disconnected from any observation of reality (the worked example given is pre-2008-crisis mark-to-model asset valuations that technically followed accounting procedure but weren't checked against real market behavior) can look measurement-like while carrying none of measurement's actual evidentiary content.

## Verification Action
When a draft states a quantitative claim as a bare point figure, check whether it derives from an actual observation and ask for the uncertainty that should accompany it. Treat the total absence of any error range, confidence interval, or hedge as a signal to trace the number back to how it was actually produced — see [sampling and measurement bias](sampling-and-measurement-bias.md) — rather than accepting it as more precise or more empirically grounded than it is.
