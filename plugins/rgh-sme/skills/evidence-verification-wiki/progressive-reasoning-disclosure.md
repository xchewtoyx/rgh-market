# Progressive Reasoning Disclosure

Presenting a conclusion first — a recommendation, a final claim, a decision already made — and only then offering supporting reasoning invites a reviewer to react to the conclusion as an *opinion* to agree or disagree with, rather than to independently evaluate the *evidence* that produced it. Once a conclusion is on the table, a reviewer's own pre-existing opinions about the subject compete with it directly, and whoever has more authority in the room tends to win that competition regardless of which position the evidence actually supports (the mechanism behind "the highest-paid person's opinion wins" dynamics). Walking a reviewer through the same sequence of evidence and intermediate findings that produced the conclusion — in the order they were actually discovered or reasoned through, not compressed into a final answer — lets the reviewer reach the same conclusion themselves, or catch a specific step where they'd have concluded differently.

## The Curse of Knowledge
The reason authors default to conclusion-first presentation is the **curse of knowledge**: once you know something, it is difficult to remember what it was like not to know it, so your own conclusion feels self-evidently obvious to you. A reviewer who wasn't present for the underlying investigation does not share that context and is being asked to accept a conclusion that skips the steps that made it feel obvious to the author. This is a structural, not a communication-style, problem — no amount of confident delivery substitutes for actually walking the reviewer through the missing steps.

## Practical Structure
- Restate the question or goal the work was meant to answer, checking that this framing is still agreed before presenting anything built on top of it.
- Present the evidence-gathering process and what it surfaced, at the level of intermediate findings — not just a summary count, but what was actually found and how it narrowed the space of possible conclusions.
- Present how competing explanations or options were assessed and why one was preferred, explicitly inviting the reviewer to point out where they would have weighed something differently — see [objection anticipation in argument construction](objection-anticipation-in-argument-construction.md) for testing this step against the objections a reviewer is likely to raise.
- Only then state the conclusion, framed as what follows from the steps just walked through, not as a freestanding assertion needing separate defense.
- Tailor the amount of intermediate detail to the specific reviewer's needs, but do not skip the *structure* (question → evidence → reasoning → conclusion) even in a compressed version — a shortened walkthrough that still moves through all four steps is different from a walkthrough that jumps straight to the conclusion with supporting points listed underneath it.

## Verification Action
When reviewing a document that presents only a conclusion with supporting points, treat this as a structural gap, not just a style preference — ask the author to reconstruct the sequence of evidence and intermediate reasoning that led to the conclusion, and check each step, rather than evaluating only whether the final claim sounds plausible.
