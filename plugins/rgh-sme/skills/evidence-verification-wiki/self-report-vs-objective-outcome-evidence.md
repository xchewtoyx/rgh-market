# Self-Report vs. Objective Outcome Evidence

A claim that an intervention worked is often supported by two different kinds of evidence: objectively measured outcomes (e.g., complication or mortality rates recorded independent of anyone's opinion) and participants' own self-reported perception of whether it worked (e.g., a survey asking whether people felt it helped, or whether they recall it preventing a problem). These are not interchangeable evidence for the same claim, even when they point the same direction.

## Why Self-Report Is Weaker
Self-reported perception data is subject to failure modes objective outcome data isn't: recall bias (a respondent's memory of a specific prevented incident may be unreliable or influenced by having just been asked), social desirability bias (a respondent may over-report satisfaction with something they were involved in implementing), and the absence of any counterfactual (a respondent has no access to what would have happened without the intervention, unlike a measured outcome rate that can be compared before and after). None of this makes self-report worthless — it's useful evidence of buy-in, usability, and perceived value — but it is a distinct, weaker evidence tier for the specific causal claim "this intervention reduced the target outcome."

## Worked Example
A checklist rollout was supported by both kinds of evidence: objectively measured complication and mortality rates fell after implementation, and a separate clinician survey found large majorities reported the checklist was easy to use, improved safety, and had personally prevented an error, with the vast majority saying they'd want it used on themselves. The survey results are strong evidence of adoption and perceived value, but the *causal effectiveness* claim rests on the objective outcome-rate comparison, not on how many clinicians recalled it preventing an error.

## Corroborating Evidence: Objective Tracking Contradicts Self-Report at Scale
Research comparing self-reported social behavior (how much time people recall spending with whom) against objective location/activity tracking data has found the two are highly inconsistent — people are simply not reliable narrators of their own routine behavior, independent of any motive to mislead. This is general evidence that self-report should be treated as a distinct, weaker tier even for purely descriptive (not just effectiveness) claims about behavior, not only for causal claims about an intervention's effect.

## Verification Action
When a draft supports an effectiveness claim with both self-reported and objectively measured evidence, check that the causal claim itself is attributed to the objective outcome data, and that self-report figures are labeled and used only for what they can actually support (perceived value, adoption, usability) rather than substituted as if they were independent confirmation of the causal effect — see [evidence quality dimensions](evidence-quality-dimensions.md) for the general "appropriate kind of evidence for the claim" check this is an instance of.

A stated reason behind a self-report can itself be an on-the-spot fabrication the reporter believes — see [confabulation in testimony](confabulation-in-testimony.md) for why "why" questions are especially unreliable and what to ask instead.
