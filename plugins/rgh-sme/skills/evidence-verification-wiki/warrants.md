# Warrants

A **warrant** is the general principle that authorizes moving from a reason to a claim — it is neither evidence (externally checkable information) nor the reason itself (a proposition in the argument), but the licensing rule that explains *why* the reason is relevant support for the claim at all. Most arguments leave their warrant unstated because the author assumes the audience already accepts it; verification breaks down exactly where that assumption is wrong.

## When to Surface a Warrant Explicitly
- The audience may not share the same disciplinary assumptions the author is relying on.
- The inference itself is controversial, not just the underlying facts.
- The argument is explaining specialist reasoning to a non-specialist audience, who cannot be assumed to already hold the specialist's background principle.

## Testing a Warrant
When reviewing an argument, test its warrant (stated or not) against three questions:
1. **Reliability**: is the general principle actually true, or only usually true?
2. **Sufficient generality**: does the principle hold broadly enough to license this specific inference, or is it too narrow/context-specific to transfer here?
3. **Applicability to this case**: even a reliable, sufficiently general warrant can fail to apply to the specific case at hand — check for limits, exceptions, or a competing warrant that would license a different inference from the same reason.

## Backing a Warrant
A warrant that isn't self-evidently accepted needs its own support ("backing") — via appeal to recognized authority, genuinely shared common knowledge, or independent evidence for the principle itself. An argument's evidence can be flawless and its reason can be true, and the argument can still fail if the warrant connecting them is unsupported or false.

## Why This Matters for Review
Warrants frequently encode values or methodological assumptions the author isn't consciously aware of stating. Making a warrant explicit turns a disagreement that looks like dueling assertions ("this claim is wrong" / "no it isn't") into a disagreement about a specific, checkable principle — which is where genuine resolution becomes possible. A reviewer who only checks a document's cited facts and reasons, without surfacing the warrant connecting them to the claim, can miss the actual point of disagreement entirely.
