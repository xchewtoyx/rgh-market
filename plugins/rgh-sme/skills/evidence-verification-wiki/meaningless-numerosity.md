# Meaningless Numerosity

A number can be entirely accurate and still be uninformative, because it omits the comparison a reader needs to interpret it. Precision creates a false sense of rigor even when the claim permits no actual evaluation.

## Worked Examples
- A product claiming "25% Less Fat Than the Average of the Leading Brands" is unfalsifiable and uninterpretable as stated: the comparator brands aren't named, and other nutrients aren't addressed.
- A "99.9% caffeine-free" claim on a low-caffeine product is true but vacuous — the same arithmetic makes a strong cup of coffee "99.9% caffeine-free" too, by weight. The claim permits no comparison to the product's actual competitor (coffee) at all. A genuinely informative version would state the relative comparison directly (e.g., "1% as much caffeine as coffee").

## Verification Action
When a draft states a percentage, ratio, or comparative figure, check whether the comparison baseline is actually specified and whether it's the *relevant* comparison for the claim being made. If the number is technically true regardless of which real-world alternative it's compared against, it is not doing the persuasive work the document is using it for, and should be flagged as unsupported context, not corrected as a factual error.
