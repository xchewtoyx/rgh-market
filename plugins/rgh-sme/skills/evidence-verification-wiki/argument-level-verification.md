# Argument-Level Verification

**Argument-level verification** (often framed as the "forest vs. trees" problem) is the practice of evaluating whether the verified individual claims in a document collectively justify its overarching argument, conclusions, or recommendations. Verifying individual facts ([defining-checkable-claims](defining-checkable-claims.md)) is necessary, but a document can still be fundamentally incorrect if its factual foundation is cherry-picked or mischaracterized to support an unsound thesis.

## The Forest vs. Trees Distinction
A document's accuracy exists at two levels:
1. **The Trees (Micro-level)**: The individual data points, quotes, measurements, and references.
2. **The Forest (Macro-level)**: The logical structure, conclusions, and narrative that these individual facts construct.

If a reviewer verifies every individual fact but fails to check the logical connections between them, they risk approving a document that is factually accurate in its details but logically fallacious or misleading in its conclusions. Conversely, if a key fact supporting the central thesis is disproven or removed, the reviewer must reassess whether the remaining "forest" can still stand.

A **fact** is a single indisputable data point; **truth**, in this sense, is a fact or set of facts placed in context — including which facts were selected, in what order, and from whose perspective. Two journalists working from the same recorded interview once produced two substantially different published stories, each built entirely from real, individually verifiable quotes and facts drawn from that one recording. Every fact in each story could pass a line-by-line check, yet the two stories left listeners with different impressions of the same underlying event — demonstrating that fact-checking the trees does not, by itself, guarantee two independently "checked" tellings of the same material will agree on the forest.

## Verification Prompts for Reviewers
Reviewers should constantly query the relationship between facts and arguments by asking:
- *How do the individual facts build the central claim?*
- *Is the source of this fact positioned to support this specific conclusion?*
- *If this specific data point were removed or corrected, does the overarching recommendation still hold?*
- *Are there other, contradicting facts that have been omitted?* (See [omission-detection](omission-detection.md))

## Reasons Are Not Evidence
Keep two layers of an argument distinct when verifying it: **reasons** are the propositions doing the arguing (claims about why the thesis holds), while **evidence** is the externally checkable information cited to support those reasons. A reviewer checking only whether the cited evidence is factually accurate, without separately checking whether it's [sufficient, representative, and the right kind](evidence-quality-dimensions.md) to actually support the reason it's attached to, can pass a document whose facts are true but whose argument doesn't follow from them. A well-built argument makes this bridge — evidence supporting a reason, and reasons supporting the claim — explicit and visible, rather than leaving the reader to construct the connection themselves. The unstated principle that licenses the reason-to-claim step specifically is the argument's [warrant](warrants.md); a document can have accurate evidence and a true reason and still fail if the warrant connecting them doesn't hold.

## The Five Audience Questions
A well-formed argument answers five predictable questions a skeptical audience will raise, each corresponding to a component with its own verification technique:
1. *What should I believe?* — the **claim** (see [claim type taxonomy](claim-type-taxonomy.md) for matching it to the evidence it needs).
2. *Why?* — the **reasons** offered in its support.
3. *How do you know?* — the **evidence** backing those reasons (see [evidence quality dimensions](evidence-quality-dimensions.md)).
4. *How does that follow?* — the **warrant** licensing the reason-to-claim inference (see [warrants](warrants.md)).
5. *But what about…?* — **acknowledgment and response** to predictable objections and alternatives (see [objection anticipation in argument construction](objection-anticipation-in-argument-construction.md)).

Working through all five in order, for a document's central argument, is a fast way to locate which specific component (not just "the argument feels weak") is under-supported.
