# Negation Test for Vacuous Claims

Some prose is unclear not because the underlying idea is genuinely difficult, but because it's constructed to be **unclarifiable** — convoluted enough that it resists being pinned down to a specific, checkable meaning at all. This is a distinct failure mode from a claim that's merely false or under-sourced: a vacuous claim can't be verified or refuted because it was never precise enough to say anything specific in the first place.

## The Diagnostic
Take the sentence and negate it (replace its central assertion with its opposite). If the negated version doesn't clearly mean something different from the original — if both versions feel equally plausible or equally empty — the sentence isn't making a checkable claim, regardless of how sophisticated or fluent it reads. A sentence like "Prospero is ultimately the fulcrum of an epistemic tragedy of self-knowledge" fails this test: its negation doesn't obviously assert the opposite of anything, because the original never specified a concrete, checkable claim to begin with.

## Verification Action
When a sentence in a draft is hard to verify not because the underlying fact is obscure but because the sentence itself resists being pinned down to a specific meaning:
- Try negating the sentence's central assertion. If the negation doesn't clearly contradict the original, treat the sentence as not yet making a checkable claim — push the author to state precisely what is being asserted before attempting to verify it.
- Distinguish this from [defining checkable claims](defining-checkable-claims.md)'s broader question of whether a claim has a checkable source: this test targets claims that fail even earlier, before sourcing is relevant, because they don't have a pinned-down enough meaning to check against anything.
