# Citation Completeness Check

Citation serves two distinct purposes: ethical attribution, and a service that lets a reader trace a claim back to its source and check it independently. A document can state a fact accurately and still fail the second purpose if it omits the citation that would let a reader confirm it — the fact being true doesn't make it checkable by someone other than the author.

## What Requires Citation
Citation obligations extend beyond direct quotations and statistics — every borrowed idea, distinctive wording, data point, or structural approach taken from a source needs attribution, not just the material that's obviously "a fact from somewhere." A reviewer auditing citation completeness should look for unmarked material that clearly originated elsewhere, not just check that every number already has a footnote.

## Patchwriting
**Patchwriting** — closely imitating a source's phrasing or sentence structure while substituting synonyms — is plagiarism even when the source is cited. A citation credits the *idea*; it does not license copying the source's *expression*. A reviewer should treat "the source is cited" as necessary but not sufficient: the wording itself must also diverge substantially from the source's own phrasing wherever it isn't presented as a marked quotation.

## Verification Action
When auditing a draft's use of sources, check three things independently: (1) is every borrowed idea, data point, or distinctive structure cited, not just quoted material; (2) does paraphrased material use genuinely independent wording rather than patchwriting the source's phrasing; (3) could a reader, given only the citation, actually locate and check the specific material being relied on.
