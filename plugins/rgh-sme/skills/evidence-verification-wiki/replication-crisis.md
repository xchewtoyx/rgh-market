# Replication Crisis

Large-scale efforts to reproduce published research findings have repeatedly found that a substantial share of results do not replicate. Documented figures include: a commercial lab reproducing only 6 of 53 "landmark" cancer biology studies; a coordinated replication effort reproducing only 39 of 100 high-profile social psychology experiments; and an economics replication effort reproducing only 11 of 18 top-journal experimental papers.

## Fraud Is Not the Main Cause
Outright fraud is a real but rare contributor — plausibly explaining a small fraction of failures, not the scale seen across these efforts. The dominant drivers are instead structural: weak statistical practices, [publication bias](publication-bias.md) toward positive results, and the [base rate fallacy](base-rate-fallacy.md) applied at the level of an entire field's literature (testing many hypotheses that were unlikely to be true a priori).

## Verification Action
Because a nontrivial fraction of any field's published findings fail to replicate, a single study — even one that is well-conducted and legitimately published — should be treated as one data point that incrementally shifts belief, not as a definitive settled fact. When a draft treats one study's finding as conclusive, check whether it has been independently replicated, and prefer systematic reviews or meta-analyses that aggregate across multiple studies where available. See [evaluating scientific literature](evaluating-scientific-literature.md).
