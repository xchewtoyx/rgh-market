# Cross-Statement Consistency Check

A single document or statement can be misleading without containing any individually false line, if it's read in isolation from other documents or sections that describe the same underlying reality. Professional investors who formalized due-diligence checklists converged on the same insight: "it's easy to hide in a statement; it's hard to hide between statements." A misleading picture that survives scrutiny of any one financial statement, section, or footnote in isolation often falls apart once two or more are checked against each other for consistency.

## What to Cross-Check
- **Headline figures against supporting detail**: check a summary statement's top-line numbers (e.g., reported revenue growth) against the more granular detail that should be consistent with it (e.g., cash flow statements, cost figures) — a mismatch between what a headline claims and what the underlying detail actually shows is a specific, checkable red flag.
- **Footnotes and fine print against the main narrative**: footnotes, disclosures, and fine print are often where a fact inconsistent with the main narrative surfaces (e.g., a disclosure of insider selling alongside an otherwise bullish presentation) — these sections need to be checked as carefully as the primary claims, not skipped as boilerplate.
- **Current performance against its own drivers**: check whether a period of strong reported results depended on conditions specific to that period (a cyclical boom, an unusual one-time event) that a claim implicitly assumes are durable and general — a claim built on a favorable-conditions-dependent track record needs its own scope calibrated to those conditions, not stated as if it reflects underlying, repeatable quality (see [claim scope calibration](claim-scope-calibration.md)).

## Verification Action
When verifying a claim backed by multiple related documents or sections (financial statements, technical reports, multi-part disclosures), don't stop at confirming each section is individually accurate — explicitly check whether the sections are consistent with each other, and treat unexplained inconsistency between them as a stronger red flag than any single section's content, since it's specifically the place a misleading claim is hardest to construct convincingly across every section at once.
