# Incentive Bias in Verification

**Incentive bias** in verification is the tendency to under-scrutinize a claim specifically because accepting it would produce a highly desirable outcome — a large financial return, a career-making result, a solution to an urgent problem. Multiple professional investors independently identified the same mechanism (nicknamed the "cocaine brain problem"): the prospect of a big win activates reward-anticipation circuits similar to those involved in substance dependence, and that state of excitement measurably degrades the critical judgment a reviewer would otherwise apply.

## Distinct From Related Biases
This is a different mechanism from [confirmation bias in verification](confirmation-bias-in-verification.md) (under-scrutinizing a claim because it matches existing beliefs) and [knowledge bias in verification](knowledge-bias-in-verification.md) (under-scrutinizing a claim because it feels familiar). Incentive bias can strike on a completely novel, unfamiliar claim that the reviewer holds no prior belief about at all — the trigger is the desirability of the outcome the claim implies, not agreement with a prior or a sense of familiarity.

## Verification Action
- Treat a reviewer's own heightened excitement, urgency, or anticipation about a claim's implications as a specific trigger for *more* procedural rigor, not less — the more desirable accepting a claim would be, the more that desirability itself should prompt deliberate, checklist-driven verification rather than intuitive judgment.
- Build and use a standing checklist specifically for moments of high excitement about a prospective decision, so the verification steps run the same way regardless of how appealing the outcome looks in the moment — a checklist run consistently is a systematic counter-pressure to a state that reliably degrades in-the-moment judgment.
- Where checklist items are derived from cataloguing past mistakes (see [verification checklist design](verification-checklist-design.md)), include mistakes specifically caused by excitement or urgency overriding a check that would otherwise have been run.

This is the reviewer-side version of a broader pattern; see [engagement incentive distortion](engagement-incentive-distortion.md) for the same incentive-driven distortion occurring on the *source* side, where a publisher's revenue model (not the reviewer's excitement) skews what gets produced.
