# Confabulation in Testimony

When a person is asked to explain their own past behavior or motives, they are not reliably reporting a remembered reason — they are frequently constructing a plausible-sounding story on the spot, and experience that constructed story with the same confidence as an actual memory. This is not dishonesty; it's how self-explanation works. The classic demonstration comes from split-brain experiments: when only the brain's non-verbal hemisphere had the actual information behind a choice, the verbal hemisphere — which had no access to that information — still confidently invented a coherent, plausible-sounding reason with no basis in what had actually happened. Follow-up research found this "confident confabulation" mechanism operates in everyone, not only split-brain patients. Confidence in an explanation is a feeling about how coherent and easy-to-process the explanation feels, not a signal of its accuracy — so a witness's certainty about *why* they did something is not itself evidence that the stated reason is correct.

A related, narrower failure mode: people asked what they want or value tend to answer with an idealized, identity-driven version of themselves ("what I should want") rather than a description of what actually drives their behavior — a documented case had someone name "fit" as their top purchasing criterion for a product category while their own most recent purchase in that category was actually driven by entirely different factors (price, convenience, brand loyalty) that didn't match their stated criterion at all.

## Why This Matters Beyond Product Interviews
This applies to any testimonial evidence gathered by asking someone to explain their own reasoning, motive, or preference — witness interviews, user research, survey free-text responses, retrospective self-assessments. Direct "why did you..." or "what matters most to you..." questions invite confabulation. This compounds the general weakness of self-report evidence discussed in [self-report vs. objective outcome evidence](self-report-vs-objective-outcome-evidence.md): it's not just that self-report lacks a counterfactual and is subject to social-desirability pressure, but that a stated *reason* can be a confident, coherent fabrication even when the reporter is trying to be fully honest.

## Verification Action
- Treat a source's stated reason for their own past action as a hypothesis to corroborate against independent, behavioral evidence (what they actually did, records, other observers), not as settled fact simply because it was stated confidently and coherently.
- When eliciting testimony directly, prefer asking about a specific, recent, concrete instance ("tell me about the last time you did X, what happened first, what happened next") over direct or hypothetical questions ("what factors matter to you when you do X") — recall of a specific episode is far less prone to confabulation and idealized self-narrative than a generalized self-explanation.
- If a respondent's account drifts from a specific episode into a generalization ("I usually...", "in general I..."), treat that drift itself as a cue: redirect back to whether the generalization actually held in the specific instance being discussed, since generalizations are exactly where confabulated, identity-driven answers hide.

## Worked Example: Mistaking a Real Tacit Cue for "ESP"
A skilled performer's own explanation of a snap judgment can be confidently wrong about the *mechanism*, even when the underlying perception was genuinely well-founded. A naval air-defense officer identified an incoming radar contact as a hostile missile within seconds, before any formal identification data existed, and could only describe the experience afterward as an inexplicable hunch — even privately concluding it must have been something like ESP. Careful physical reconstruction after the fact, cross-checking the raw radar data against known equipment behavior, found a real, physical explanation the officer had never consciously noticed: coastal radar ground-clutter masks a low-flying object longer than a high-flying one, so the missile's radar track first appeared at an unusually large distance from shore compared to the higher-flying aircraft the officer was used to tracking — an anomaly that registered as alarm without ever being consciously identified as "altitude." The specific mechanism the officer *did* consciously report noticing (the target appeared to be accelerating) was independently checked against the raw data and found to be physically impossible, since detecting acceleration requires more radar sweeps than had occurred. The lesson: a confident, coherent account of "how I knew" can be entirely wrong about the actual mechanism even when the underlying snap judgment itself was well-calibrated and correct — the two are separate claims requiring separate verification, and debunking the stated mechanism does not, by itself, mean the judgment was mere luck or unfounded.

## A Structured Protocol for High-Stakes Debriefing
For higher-stakes testimony (e.g., a participant in an incident being investigated), a four-stage debriefing protocol further reduces confabulation and distortion from leading questions:
1. Let the participant tell the story unassisted and uninterrupted, without replaying recordings or feeding them cues from other sources first — external cues introduced before their own account risks contaminating their memory with information they didn't actually have at the time.
2. Have the investigator read the story back, to confirm accurate comprehension of the participant's own account before probing it further.
3. Jointly identify the critical junctures (key decision points) in the story.
4. At each juncture, probe specifically for what the participant actually perceived and knew *at that moment* — what cues were noticed, how the situation was interpreted, what knowledge or prior experience was invoked, what competing goals or constraints were active, and what outcome was expected — rather than asking generally "why did you do that."

This structure keeps the account anchored to the participant's actual moment-to-moment awareness rather than to a reconstructed, outcome-informed narrative; see [hindsight bias in review](hindsight-bias-in-review.md) for why the reviewer's own outcome knowledge is a separate distorting force to guard against even once a clean account has been elicited.
