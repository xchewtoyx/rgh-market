# Relative Risk vs. Absolute Risk

Health, safety, and performance reporting commonly conflates **relative risk** (the proportional change between two values) with **absolute risk** (the actual real-world magnitude of the change). A reviewer must check which framing the underlying data supports, because the two can tell very different stories from the same study.

## Worked Example
A study shows a factor raises *relative* risk of an outcome by 25%, which generates an alarming headline. But if the *absolute* risk only moved from 0.001% to 0.00125%, the real-world effect is tiny despite the large relative-percentage framing. Both statements are numerically true; only one is misleading without the other alongside it.

## A Relative Risk Number Also Needs a Baseline
A relative-risk figure alone (e.g., "1.44x risk") is uninterpretable without the baseline rate it's multiplying. A widely-covered "no safe level" headline about a substance's health risk can be built on a relative risk increase of well under 1% per typical use — meaning the *baseline*-adjusted absolute risk moves by only a few additional cases per 100,000 people, even though the qualitative headline claim ("no safe level exists") is technically consistent with the data. The dose-response relationship can be real and worth reporting while the absolute magnitude at typical/moderate exposure levels remains genuinely small — both facts need to be present for the claim to be honestly represented.

## Verification Action
When a draft states a risk or effect size as a percentage change, the reviewer must:
- Trace the claim back to the primary study's raw numbers (the underlying counts or base rates), not just the press release's percentage.
- Confirm whether the draft presents relative or absolute risk, and whether that framing matches the emphasis in the source data.
- Require both figures be surfaced together when the relative framing alone would materially mislead a reader about practical significance.
