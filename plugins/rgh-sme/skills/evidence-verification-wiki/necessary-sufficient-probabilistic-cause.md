# Necessary, Sufficient, and Probabilistic Cause

A claim that "A causes B" can mean three different things, and arguments frequently equivocate between them:
- **Probabilistic cause**: A increases the *chance* of B occurring.
- **Sufficient cause**: A *always* produces B.
- **Necessary cause**: B *cannot occur* without A.

## Two Named Fallacies
- **Conflating sufficient with probabilistic**: arguing that because not everyone exposed to A develops B, A doesn't really cause B — this demands a sufficient-cause standard to refute what was only ever claimed as a probabilistic effect. A public claim that "most people exposed to A don't get B" can be true and can still radically understate harm if the actual claim under dispute was probabilistic risk elevation, not certainty.
- **Conflating necessary with probabilistic**: arguing that because some people who get B were never exposed to A, A doesn't cause B — this demands a necessary-cause standard (B is impossible without A) to refute what was only ever claimed as a probabilistic effect. Other causal pathways to B can coexist with A being a genuine probabilistic cause.

## Verification Action
When a draft disputes or defends a causal claim, identify which of the three senses the original claim was actually making, and check that the counter-argument or supporting argument addresses that same sense rather than a stricter or looser one substituted in its place.
