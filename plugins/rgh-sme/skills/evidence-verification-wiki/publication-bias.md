# Publication Bias

Journals and researchers systematically favor positive/significant results over negative ones: negative results feel inconclusive, may reflect a failed technique rather than a real finding, and are statistically "cheap" to produce (most tested hypotheses are false, so failing to reject one is unsurprising). The result is the **file drawer effect** — negative results are disproportionately left unpublished, sitting in a metaphorical file drawer, while positive results reach print. A reader who only sees the published literature sees a body of evidence dominated by positive findings and concludes an effect is real and robust, when the full picture (including the unpublished negatives) may not support that at all.

## Named Case: FDA-Registered Antidepressant Trials
Because US law requires clinical trials to be *registered* with the FDA even when publication isn't required, one researcher could compare the full universe of registered trials against the smaller set that were actually published. Of 74 registered trials, 38 were positive, 12 questionable, and 24 negative (~51% raw "success" rate). Of the trials that were actually published, 48 were positive and only 3 negative — most of the questionable/negative trials had been recast as positive findings in publication. The registered 51% success rate became a published 94% success rate. This is visualized as an iceberg: the published positive results are the visible tip, while the unpublished or recast negative results are the submerged mass a reader never sees.

## Amplification by Secondary Reporting
Science journalism compounds the effect further: studies of press-covered disease-risk papers found that initial positive findings generated far more news coverage than the later disconfirming follow-up studies did — a reader following news coverage alone sees almost none of the corrections.

## Verification Action
- Do not treat a single published positive study, or even a cluster of them, as the full evidentiary picture — check whether trial registries, preprint servers, or systematic reviews reveal a larger, less favorable body of unpublished or under-reported results.
- **Meta-analysis** across multiple studies (published and, where accessible, registered-but-unpublished) is the best available tool for estimating the size of the unseen "submerged" portion of evidence.
- Be specifically skeptical of secondary/press coverage that reports only the positive finding without checking whether a registry or prior literature shows a less favorable base rate.
