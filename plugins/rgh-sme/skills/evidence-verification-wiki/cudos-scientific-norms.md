# CUDOS Scientific Norms

Sociologist Robert K. Merton identified four norms that make a community of investigators trustworthy at converging on truth, summarized by the acronym **CUDOS**:

- **Communism** (in the sense of communal ownership): data and findings must eventually be shared with the community rather than held back — secrecy is the norm's direct opposite. A rule of thumb for individuals: an urge to omit an inconvenient detail is itself a signal that the detail is relevant and should be shared, not suppressed.
- **Universalism**: a claim is evaluated against fixed, impersonal criteria regardless of who made it — status, reputation, or affiliation of the source must not determine whether a claim is accepted or rejected. Violating this norm is "shooting the message" based on the messenger.
- **Disinterestedness**: active vigilance against conflicts of interest, financial or otherwise, that could distort evaluation of a claim — including the often-unconscious conflicts built into ordinary cognition (motivation to confirm existing beliefs, take credit for good outcomes, externalize blame for bad ones).
- **Organized Skepticism**: the community structurally encourages engagement and dissent rather than treating disagreement as a threat, embedding the practice of actively asking why a claim might *not* be true rather than only looking for reasons it is.

## Why This Matters Beyond Science
These norms function as a general checklist for whether any group's process for evaluating claims deserves trust — a corporate review process, an editorial team, a technical review board — not only a formal scientific community. A group that hoards information, credits or discredits claims based on who raised them, tolerates undisclosed stakes in the outcome, or treats dissent as disloyalty is violating one or more CUDOS norms regardless of how much it otherwise resembles a rigorous process.

## Historical Failure Case: Undisclosed Sponsorship
A widely influential 1967 scientific review blaming dietary fat (rather than sugar) for heart disease was later found, decades afterward, to have been funded by the sugar industry — a violation of disinterestedness that went undetected for years because the field's journals did not yet require conflict-of-interest disclosure. National dietary policy was built on the resulting research for a generation before the funding conflict came to light.

## Verification Action
When assessing whether a body of findings, a review process, or an institution's conclusions deserve trust, check each norm separately: is underlying data available for others to check (communism)? Would the same claim be accepted or rejected if it came from a different, less credentialed or less liked source (universalism)? Are all funding sources and stakes in the outcome disclosed (disinterestedness)? Does the process include a structural mechanism for dissent, or does it only reward confirming the prevailing view (organized skepticism)? A process failing on any one norm is a specific, nameable weakness worth flagging on its own, not just a vague impression that something feels off.
