# Poll Methodology Assessment

Polls are **primary sources** (direct data collection), but a poll result is not itself "capital-T Truth" — it is one snapshot from one point in time, and the real question for a reviewer is whether the poll is systematically biased. A single poll should be situated within the broader "polling ecosystem": compare it against other recent polls asking the same question rather than citing it in isolation.

## Assessing the Pollster
- Is the organization a known, track-recorded entity, or unknown/new (requiring independent reporting to rule out a scam pollster)?
- Is there transparent contact information, and will the pollster discuss its methodology?
- Do they disclose verbatim questions, question order, demographic breakdown, and response rate? A missing response rate alone is not disqualifying, but more transparency is a positive signal.
- Distinguish independent/news/university polls from candidate-commissioned polls (which may reuse biased contact lists) and internal campaign polls (the most prone to leading questions or cherry-picked release of favorable results — see [cherry-picking](cherry-picking.md)).

## Assessing an Individual Poll
- **Date range**: when was the data collected, and what was happening at that time that could shape opinion?
- **Sample size**: roughly 2,000 respondents is often sufficient for a representative national sample; scaling to 10,000 doesn't meaningfully increase accuracy. Be suspicious of unusually small or undisclosed sample sizes.
- **Sample composition and contact method**: probability-based polling (phone/mail lists) must guard against contact-method skew (e.g., landline-only bias) corrected via weighting; nonprobability/opt-in online polling has no true random-sampling guarantee (no master list of internet users exists) and must instead be assessed by the pollster's track record.
- **Question wording and order**: leading questions and question order can prime responses; sensitive-topic phrasing affects honesty; long surveys risk late-question response fatigue.

## Specific Wording Defects to Check For
Beyond generic "leading question" concerns, check survey instruments for these named, specific defects:
- **Loaded terms**: words carrying unintended positive or negative connotation baked into the question itself (e.g., asking about a politician's "liberal" policies without naming the specific policies).
- **Leading questions**: wording that signals the answer the question-writer wants (e.g., "should the underpaid, overworked sanitation workers get pay raises?"). If the survey author already knows the desired answer, the instrument cannot be doing genuine uncertainty reduction.
- **Compound questions**: two distinct things bundled into one question (e.g., asking about satisfaction with "the seat, steering wheel, and controls" together) — a respondent dissatisfied with only one element has no way to express that, and the resulting answer can't be attributed to any single element.
- **Response set bias**: respondents mechanically picking the same scale position regardless of content, mitigated by periodically reversing which end of a scale represents the positive answer.
- **Partition dependence**: the same underlying question can yield different answer distributions purely from how the answer scale is bucketed — e.g., a coarse 3-option scale versus a fine 5-option scale changes how often respondents pick the option with identical wording present on both. This is a property of the instrument, not the respondents, so a poll's reported breakdown should be checked against how its scale was partitioned before comparing it to a differently-scaled poll on the same question.

## Verification Action
Also check whether the document *using* the poll did so responsibly — dropped in as unqualified fact vs. contextualized against other polls, with the underlying question's wording accurately represented. When comparing two surveys on the same underlying question, check whether they used the same answer-scale partitioning before treating a difference in reported results as a real difference in opinion rather than an artifact of the instrument.
