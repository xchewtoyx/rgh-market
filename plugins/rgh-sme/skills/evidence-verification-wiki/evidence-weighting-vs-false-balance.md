# Evidence Weighting vs. False Balance

**Evidence weighting** is the practice of calibrating the space, prominence, and authority given to a technical viewpoint to match the strength of the evidence supporting it. Failing to weight evidence correctly leads to **false balance** (or false equivalence), where a marginal, unverified, or thoroughly debunked claim is presented as having equal validity to established technical consensus.

## The Pitfall of False Balance
In general news reporting, journalism often values presenting two opposing views with equal weight. However, in scientific and technical reviews, this approach is a major failure mode:
- **Misleading the Reader**: Giving equal space to a fringe contrarian and an established consensus creates the false impression that the technical community is divided when it is actually in broad agreement.
- **Validating Unsound Claims**: It allows unsupported opinions to stand on the same footing as claims backed by rigorous, peer-reviewed, or empirical data.

## Best Practices for Evidence Weighting
- **Weight by Consensus and Evidence**: When a topic is technically settled (e.g., the safety of a verified engineering standard or a scientific fact), do not introduce dissenting fringe views without clearly contextualizing them as minority or non-standard positions.
- **Check Affiliations and Motives**: Evaluate the funding, institutional backing, and potential conflicts of interest of dissenting sources. A single dissenter funded by an industry advocacy group does not outweigh independent peer-reviewed consensus.
- **Represent the Consensus Scale**: Ensure the document's tone and structure reflect the proportion of agreement in the field. For example, do not give equal word count to a standard design and a highly experimental, unverified proposal without highlighting the risk and lack of peer adoption of the latter.

## Disclosing Interested-Party Claims
When a claim originates from a party with a stake in it being believed (a company marketing its own product, a spokesperson), two techniques keep the weighting honest:
- **Attribute explicitly**: state the claim as the company's/spokesperson's assertion (e.g., "the manufacturer states...") rather than presenting it as the document's own established fact.
- **Seek independent corroboration**: interview an expert with no financial ties to either the company or its competitors to assess the claim's plausibility. For outrageous or potentially harmful claims (especially health-related), question whether the claim belongs in the document at all, or ensure it is framed with clear skeptical context rather than passed through uncritically.
