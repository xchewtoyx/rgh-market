# Source Authority Scope

The same person, interview, or document can be a strong primary source for one claim and a weak secondary source for another, depending on whether the specific claim falls within that source's direct firsthand knowledge or expertise. Evaluating a source once and applying that judgment to everything they say is a verification error — authority does not transfer across topics.

## Worked Examples
- **An oncologist interviewed about cancer** is a strong primary source within their clinical expertise, but the same oncologist interviewed about the *history* of cancer treatment is a secondary source outside their firsthand expertise — a medical historian, or primary archival sources, would be the stronger source for that specific claim.
- **A farmer whose crops were destroyed by a flood** is a primary source for the bare fact that the crops were destroyed, but only a secondary source for any causal claim about *why* the flood happened — if the farmer attributes the flood to climate change, that specific causal claim needs corroboration from a qualified scientist, even though the farmer is a legitimate authority on the damage itself.
- **A politician speaking about a scientific topic** is not a strong source on the science, even if their remarks happen to be accurate, unless they hold relevant scientific credentials or research experience of their own — and even then, an independent, less potentially biased source is preferable. A politician's position of authority on policy does not extend to authority on the underlying science.
- **A bar bouncer describing an ejection for drug use** is a primary source for the fact of the ejection, but the reliability of the drug allegation specifically depends on whether the bouncer directly witnessed or confiscated drugs versus merely inferred use from behavior — the same testimony splits into different reliability tiers depending on which specific claim within it is being checked.

## Verification Action
For any source cited in support of multiple claims, check each claim against that source's *specific* firsthand knowledge or expertise rather than treating the source's credibility as a single blanket rating. A source can be simultaneously an excellent primary source for one claim in a document and an inadequate one for another claim two sentences later.
