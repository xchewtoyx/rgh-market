# Premortem Technique

Asking someone to review their own completed plan for flaws typically produces only half-hearted scrutiny, because they are emotionally invested in the plan succeeding — reviewing your own work for weaknesses is at odds with the confidence needed to commit to it. The **premortem** sidesteps this by changing the framing entirely: participants are told to imagine it is months in the future, the plan has been carried out, **and it has failed** — and their task is to explain why, identifying the causes that would justify "of course it wasn't going to work, because...". This reframes the exercise as a creativity and competence challenge (who can find the smartest failure mode) rather than a defense of the plan, which removes the emotional resistance to finding real weaknesses.

## Why It Works
The technique doesn't ask anyone to doubt the plan directly — doubt is exactly what a plan's authors are least willing to generate about their own work. Instead it presupposes failure as a given fact and asks only for the mechanism, which lets participants engage their analytical skill without having to first overcome their own optimism about the plan. This is measurably faster than it sounds: eliciting an initial round of imagined failure causes typically takes under ten minutes, though the resulting discussion can run much longer.

## Effectiveness
The technique has been found to reliably reduce unwarranted overconfidence in a plan compared to a standard flaw-review request, and is used as a routine part of project-kickoff meetings for exactly that reason. A documented cautionary case for why it matters: in a military mission-rehearsal exercise, of 20 crews rehearsing a precisely timed operation, only one crew actually hit its target window — yet during rehearsal, not a single crew had thought to ask what they'd do if they arrived early or late. No spontaneous contingency planning occurred without a structured prompt to imagine failure first.

## Pairing With Its Positive Counterpart: Backcasting
**Backcasting** (also called "prospective hindsight") is the premortem's positive-space complement: imagine the goal has already been *achieved*, and work backward to identify the path that must have led there. A controlled experiment found imagining a definite future outcome (positive or negative) and reasoning backward from it improved correct identification of the actual causal factors by about 30% compared with reasoning forward from the present. Because unaided planning is subject to an optimism bias that over-represents the positive-outcome space, running a premortem *after* a backcasting exercise corrects for that skew — separate research on "mental contrasting" found that people who vividly imagined only a positive outcome (with no accompanying obstacle-focused exercise) performed measurably worse at achieving it than people who also confronted concrete obstacles, across domains including weight loss, exam preparation, and job seeking. The premortem alone is valuable; paired with a prior backcasting pass, it corrects a bias the backcasting exercise would otherwise leave unaddressed.

## Verification Action
When reviewing a plan, recommendation, or proposal that has only been checked by its own authors, ask whether that review included a premortem-style step or was instead a straightforward "does this look right" pass — the latter is systematically weaker at surfacing real weaknesses precisely because it doesn't neutralize the authors' investment in the plan succeeding. Where a premortem hasn't been run, treat the absence of any identified failure modes as a gap in the review, not as evidence the plan is actually free of them.
