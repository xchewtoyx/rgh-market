# Single Sample Majority Rule

Also called the "Urn of Mystery" rule. Setup: a population is split into two categories (e.g., red/green marbles) in a proportion that is, a priori, uniformly likely to be anywhere from 0% to 100%. Guessing which category is the majority with zero observations gives 50% odds — but drawing and observing just **one single random sample** raises the odds of correctly guessing the majority to **75%**.

> Given maximum uncertainty about a population proportion (uniformly likely between 0% and 100%), there is a 75% chance a single randomly selected sample matches the majority category.

## Why This Matters for Verification
This is the extreme case of a general pattern: when starting from near-total ignorance about a proportion, the *first* observation delivers the largest single jump in confidence of any observation in the sequence — later observations still help, but with rapidly diminishing marginal uncertainty-reduction per unit of data. A reviewer facing a claim built on "just one data point" should not automatically treat that as equivalent to no evidence; under high prior uncertainty, one data point already shifts the odds substantially. It also gives one data point a countable evidentiary weight rather than a purely qualitative "well it's only one example" dismissal.

## Verification Action
When a draft's claim rests on one direct observation about a genuinely uncertain proportion (rather than zero observations), don't equate "only one sample" with "no evidence" — quantify roughly how much that single observation should move confidence given the prior uncertainty, rather than either fully crediting or fully dismissing it by default. Still check separately whether the one sample was drawn *randomly* from the relevant population — the 75% figure assumes true random selection, not a cherry-picked or convenience example.
