# Calibrated Probability Assessment

A person (or an estimate) is **calibrated** when their stated confidence level actually matches their real hit rate: of all the times they say "90% confident," the true value falls in their stated range about 90% of the time; of all the times they say "70% chance," the event happens about 70% of the time. Calibration is directly testable with a scored quiz — 90% confidence-interval range questions plus true/false questions with a stated confidence per answer — and compared statistically against what a truly calibrated scorer's results should look like.

## The Dominant Failure Mode: Overconfidence
Untrained subjects are overwhelmingly overconfident, not underconfident. In a dataset of 927 subjects and 100,000+ test items, people's stated 90% confidence intervals contained the true value only about 53% of the time on average, and just over 15% of answers given at "100% confident" were actually wrong. Rule of thumb from this data: scoring 7-10 out of 10 range questions inside your stated 90% CI suggests plausible calibration; scoring 6 or fewer suggests overconfidence; 5 or fewer suggests substantial overconfidence. Variance across professions is itself evidence that this is a learned skill rather than an innate trait — professional odds-makers calibrate noticeably better than executives, while physicians are notoriously poorly calibrated on diagnostic odds.

## It Is a Trainable Skill That Transfers
Calibration training (repeated testing with feedback, plus the [equivalent bet test](equivalent-bet-calibration-test.md) and the [absurdity test for range estimation](absurdity-test-for-range-estimation.md)) reliably improves scores within just a handful of test rounds — too fast to be explained by improving general-knowledge trivia skill. In a controlled comparison (Giga Information Group, 1997), calibration-trained analysts and an untrained control group of client executives were given the same 20 real industry predictions to call with a stated confidence; the trained analysts landed close to the ideal calibration line while the untrained group was badly overconfident (of 58 "90% confident" predictions from the untrained group, under 60% came true). Critically, the trained group did not get more predictions *right* — they were simply more honest about when to claim high confidence. This is evidence the skill transfers from a training exercise to real professional judgment calls, not just to quiz questions of the same type practiced during training.

## A Probabilistic Forecast Cannot Be "Wrong" Solely Because the Less-Likely Outcome Occurred
A forecast that assigns anything other than exactly 0% or 100% to an outcome is not falsified merely by the less-probable side coming true. Election forecasters who gave a losing candidate a 30-40% chance, or bookmakers who priced a referendum outcome as an underdog that went on to win, were not "wrong" in any sense a single data point can establish — a well-calibrated 30% forecast should, by construction, see that outcome occur roughly three times in ten. Treating a single divergent result as proof the forecast itself was bad is the mirror image of the overconfidence problem: it demands a forecaster behave as if every stated probability must resolve in the majority direction, which is only possible for someone who is not actually distinguishing degrees of uncertainty at all. Evaluating a probabilistic claim's quality requires checking its calibration across many such forecasts (see the calibration-testing approach above), not adjudicating any single instance by its outcome alone — see also [hindsight bias in review](hindsight-bias-in-review.md)'s "resulting" pattern, the same error applied to decisions rather than forecasts.

## Why This Matters for Verification
When a claim, forecast, or expert judgment comes with a stated confidence level, that number is itself worth scrutiny — treat an unqualified "I'm 90% sure" or "I'm confident that..." from someone with no known calibration track record with the same skepticism you'd apply to an uncalibrated instrument, since the base-rate finding is that most people's stated high confidence overstates their real reliability by a wide margin. Conversely, don't dismiss a stated wide range or modest confidence level as unhelpfully vague — an honest wide range is a legitimate, well-calibrated report of genuine uncertainty, not a failure to know the answer.

## Verification Action
When a document states a confidence level or probability alongside a claim, check whether it comes from anyone with a demonstrated calibration track record. Absent that, treat high stated confidence as a claim needing its own support, not as self-validating — and prefer language and reasoning that make the actual evidentiary basis for the confidence level explicit (see [hedge words for uncertain claims](hedge-words-for-uncertain-claims.md)) over a bare confidence percentage asserted without grounding.
