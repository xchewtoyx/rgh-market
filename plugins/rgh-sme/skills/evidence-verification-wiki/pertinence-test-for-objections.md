# Pertinence Test for Objections

Not every technically-true objection is worth raising. An objection that is correct but irrelevant to the point actually being made (the "well, actually..." interruption — e.g., correcting "no mammals lay eggs" with an aside about monotremes when the actual point under discussion doesn't depend on that exception) resembles bullshitting more than genuine correction: neither is optimizing for the audience's understanding, only for the objector's own appearance of cleverness.

## Six Criteria
1. **Relevance**: does the objection actually undermine the speaker's core claim, or only a peripheral technicality that leaves the main point intact?
2. **Speaker's intent**: is the target actually attempting to deceive, or engaging in good-faith, if imprecise, conversation?
3. **Objector's motivation**: is the goal to protect the audience from being misled, or to demonstrate the objector's own cleverness?
4. **Audience**: is there an actual audience at risk of being misled, or is this a private aside with no stakes?
5. **Power dynamics**: does the objection challenge someone with more power/authority (closer to legitimate accountability), or does it just needle someone with less (closer to score-settling)?
6. **Judiciousness**: has the objector weighed whether interrupting or derailing the discussion is worth the social/conversational cost, or do they raise every eligible technicality regardless of cost?

## Verification Action
Before raising a correction, run it against these six criteria. A finding that fails most of them — technically true, but peripheral, aimed at a good-faith speaker, with no real audience at risk, punching down, and raised reflexively — is better left unsaid or folded into a private note rather than surfaced as a review comment.
