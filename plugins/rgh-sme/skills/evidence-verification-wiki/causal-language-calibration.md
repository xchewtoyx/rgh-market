# Causal Language Calibration

A document can smuggle in a causal claim through wording alone, without ever stating "causes" outright — a reviewer must check the underlying evidence type (observational vs. interventional) against how strongly the prose implies causation, not just against explicit causal verbs.

## Grammatical Markers
- **Indicative mood** ("If she *is* Canadian, she *is* more likely to be bilingual") states a mere association.
- **Subjunctive/counterfactual mood** ("If she *were* Canadian, she *would be* more likely...") smuggles in a causal claim about what an intervention would do — a materially stronger claim than the indicative version, even though both could be paraphrasing the same underlying correlational data.
- The same subtlety appears in policy claims: "Where a factor is higher, an outcome is lower" (a cross-sectional association) is not equivalent to "If the factor were increased, the outcome would decrease" (an interventional claim) — a document must not casually convert one into the other without evidence for the second.

## Structural Cues in Graphics
Convention places the presumed causal/independent variable on a chart's x-axis and the outcome on the y-axis — this framing choice suggests a causal story to the reader even when no causal language is used anywhere in the accompanying text.

## Verification Action
When a source study or dataset is observational, check the draft for language that has upgraded it into a causal or prescriptive claim — a title, headline, or summary sentence often overclaims even when the body text of the same document correctly hedges the finding as correlational. This is a common site of press/media distortion: a responsibly-hedged primary report can be turned into an outright causal assertion purely through a follow-up article's or headline's wording, with no new evidence added.
