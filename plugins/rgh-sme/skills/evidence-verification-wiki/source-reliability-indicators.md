# Source Reliability Indicators

**Relevance** (does this source actually bear on the claim at hand) and **reliability** (can this source be trusted) are separate axes and should be evaluated separately — a source can be highly relevant and unreliable, or highly reliable and irrelevant to the specific claim being checked.

## Reliability Indicators
No single indicator below is conclusive on its own; weigh them together:
- Reputable publisher or institutional sponsorship.
- Peer review or an equivalent editorial vetting process.
- Author's expertise in the specific subject, and disclosed interests or affiliations that could bias their account.
- **Currency**: how recent the source needs to be depends on the field — a claim in a fast-moving field needs a much more recent source than a claim in a field where foundational work stays valid for decades.
- Transparent citations that let a reader trace the source's own claims back further.
- Judicious treatment of alternative views or counter-evidence, rather than one-sided advocacy.
- Editorial care (absence of avoidable errors, typos, inconsistencies).
- External reviews or citation impact, where available.

## Verification Action
Run a candidate source through this checklist before relying on it, and weigh the indicators together rather than accepting or rejecting a source on any single one. Separately confirm the source is actually relevant to the specific claim it's being used to support — a reliable source cited for something it doesn't actually address is still a sourcing error.
