# Evaluating Scientific Literature

Academic and scientific papers are valuable sources of primary and secondary evidence, but they require careful evaluation. A reviewer must know how to navigate a paper's structure and identify potential threats to credibility.

## Structure-Based Reading Strategy
An academic paper is structured to serve expert readers, but a reviewer checking claims can target specific sections for rapid verification:
- **Abstract & Discussion**: Usually contain the high-level findings and context. Start here to check if the document's characterization of the study matches the authors' own summary.
- **Introduction**: Functions as a [secondary source](primary-vs-secondary-sources.md) summary of prior work. If a background claim in the draft cites a paper's introduction, follow the citations further back to the primary paper that actually produced the data.
- **Methods**: Critical for verifying methodology claims (e.g., sample sizes, system configurations, or statistical models).
- **Results**: Contains the raw data and figures. Check this to confirm that the numbers cited in the draft match the actual data in the paper, rather than a rounded or exaggerated press release summary.

## Research Quality Indicators
When evaluating academic sources, look for these quality indicators and potential red flags:
1. **Peer-Review Status**: Peer-reviewed journals offer higher assurance than **preprints** (papers published before formal peer review). If citing a preprint, explicitly note its un-reviewed status.
2. **Author Stated Limitations**: Read the limitations section (usually near the end of the discussion). Stated limitations often contain essential caveats that require proper [claim scope calibration](claim-scope-calibration.md) to avoid broad, sweeping assertions.
3. **Conflicts of Interest**: Check for disclosures of funding and affiliations. A study showing a product's safety funded by its manufacturer requires more skepticism than an independent study.
4. **Predatory Journals**: Avoid open-access journals that publish work for a fee without rigorous peer review. Consult verified directories of predatory publishers if a journal's reputation is unknown.
5. **Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses**: These represent high-quality secondary sources that synthesize multiple studies. They are excellent for identifying the overall scientific consensus on a topic.

## A Single Study Is Not a Settled Fact
Popular coverage often misportrays science as a "collecting process," where each new study is a definitive fact added to a permanent record. In reality a single study only marginally shifts belief within a body of evidence still being weighed — it doesn't settle a question on its own, especially given the field's [replication crisis](replication-crisis.md) and [publication bias](publication-bias.md) toward positive results. Selectively citing only the studies that support a preferred narrative while ignoring the rest of the literature (sometimes called "cafeteria science") is a form of [cherry-picking](cherry-picking.md) specific to scientific sourcing.

## Check Whether the Stimulus or Task Was Selected to Produce the Effect
A classic finding can rest on materials chosen specifically because they were expected to produce the demonstrated effect, without the paper foregrounding that selection. A famous study demonstrating a reasoning "bias" (subjects wrongly judging a letter more common in a word's first position than its third) used a specific letter chosen because it was one of a minority of consonants where that judgment happens to be wrong — most consonants studied would have given the opposite, correct answer. The effect was real for the chosen stimulus, but the stimulus itself wasn't a representative or random draw from the relevant space of cases; it was selected because it would work. This is a subtler cousin of [cherry-picking](cherry-picking.md): the selection bias sits in the study's *design* (which materials, tasks, or examples were chosen) rather than in which of several studies or results gets cited afterward.

## Verification Action (Stimulus Selection)
When a study's finding rests on a specific example, word, image, or task, check whether that specific item was representative of the broader category it's presented as illustrating, or whether it was seemingly chosen because it was one of the few instances that would produce the reported effect. Ask what fraction of the plausible alternative stimuli would have produced the same result — a finding that depends on a minority, specially-selected case is narrower than a finding demonstrated to hold across a representative sample of cases, even if both get reported with the same confident framing.

## Journal and Paper Legitimacy Heuristics
- **Legitimate is not the same as correct**: a paper can be judged legitimate (good-faith effort, appropriate methodology, taken seriously by the field) while still turning out to be wrong — even prestigious authors and top journals have published work that was later refuted. Peer review filters for *reasonableness*, not correctness: reviewers generally don't rerun experiments or re-derive the underlying math themselves.
- **Match claim magnitude to venue prestige**: an extraordinary or field-upending claim published only in a low-tier, obscure journal (rather than a leading venue in the field) is a red flag, even though a mundane, narrow finding is perfectly normal to see in an obscure journal.
- **Check for retractions directly** via the publisher or a database such as PubMed rather than assuming a citation is still valid as published.
- These heuristics compound with the predatory-journal red flags above (an obscure publisher taking author fees without real review is even less trustworthy for an outsized claim).
