Studying experienced fireground commanders making life-and-death decisions in
under a minute, Gary Klein's research team expected to find a fast version
of **comparative evaluation** — weighing two or more options against each
other, the classic "rational choice" model taught in decision-making
courses. They found almost none of it: 80% of the decision points studied
showed no evidence of comparing options at all. Instead, commanders used
what Klein calls the **recognition-primed decision (RPD) model**: an
experienced decision-maker recognizes a situation as an instance of a
familiar pattern, and that recognition simultaneously supplies plausible
goals, the cues worth attending to, expectations about what should happen
next, and a typical course of action — all at once, not derived
step-by-step from first principles.

When more than one option comes to mind, they are evaluated one at a time
via **singular evaluation**, not against each other: take the first
option that seems workable, mentally check it for flaws (see [mental
simulation as an evaluation tool](mental-simulation-as-evaluation-tool.md)),
and either act on it, adjust it, or discard it and move to the next most
typical option — never comparing two live candidates side by side. This is
Herbert Simon's **satisficing** (pick the first option that works) rather
than **optimizing** (find the best of all options), and it is markedly
faster: the everyday analogy is stopping at the first acceptable gas
station when running low on fuel in an unfamiliar area, rather than
comparing every station in town.

**When comparative evaluation reappears**: the pattern reverses when
experience is genuinely absent. In a case where volunteer firefighters
faced an oil-tank-farm fire completely outside their prior experience, their
first two days were disorganized improvisation; only once they gave up
trying to act from pattern-matching and switched to explicitly listing
options with pros and cons — the closest thing to classic comparative
evaluation Klein's team ever recorded — did they make their first genuinely
effective decision. **Comparative, deliberate analysis is what people fall
back on when they lack the experience for recognition to work at all** —
it substitutes logical reasoning for a pattern library that isn't there.
This directly reverses the assumption that novices act impulsively on a
single idea while experts deliberate carefully among several: the data
show experts converging quickly on one workable option while novices
generate and compare multiple options because they have no faster way to
tell which one is any good.

## Implications for incident response

This is a direct explanation for the [incident commander's](incident-command-system.md)
practical behavior during [STAR's](star-incident-action-plan.md) Act phase:
an experienced commander presented with a familiar-looking incident is not
failing to "properly weigh alternatives" by moving straight to a plan — they
are doing exactly what expertise is for. The corollary cuts the other way
too: when a responder (or a whole team) is facing a situation with no
real precedent, forcing an explicit, comparative options-and-tradeoffs
discussion is the right move, not a sign the response is going badly —
see [unified command](unified-command.md)'s Planning Wheel and the [plans
group](incident-command-system.md) function, which exist specifically to
run this kind of deliberate comparison off to the side while the main
response keeps moving.

Training implication: formal training in comparative decision-making
procedures does not build the pattern-recognition ability recognition-primed
decisions depend on, and can even slow its development by encouraging
reliance on slow deliberation over fast, expertise-driven recognition.
What builds RPD capability is exposure to many realistic scenarios,
mixing common cases (to build a library of typical patterns) with rare
ones (so an unusual incident isn't the first time a responder has seen
something like it) — see [preparedness drills](preparedness-drills.md).
