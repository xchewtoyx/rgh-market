Safety management relies on different models of how accidents occur:

| Accident Model | Definition of Risk | Major Threat to Safety | Primary Safety Management Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Chain of Events** | Weakest link in a linear sequence | Unreliable human operators | Remove/replace weakest link; introduce linear rules |
| **Barriers (Swiss Cheese)** | Trajectory of uncontained risk | Weak/porous defense layers | Plug holes; add redundant defensive layers |
| **Systems Theory** | Lack of control over component interaction | Complexity, opacity, and goal conflicts | Enforce safety constraints; make goal trade-offs visible |
| **Drift into Failure** | Gradual erosion of safety margins | Success breeding overconfidence & deviance normalization | Cultivate "chronic unease"; study normal work |

* **Chain of Events / Domino Theory**: Heinrich's classic model that assumes failures are linear and proportional. It is flawed because it cannot capture interactive complexity.
* **Barriers / Swiss Cheese Model**: Reason's model dividing failure into active failures (sharp-end) and latent conditions (blunt-end). It is limited because it still treats human error as the necessary final trigger.
* **Systems Theory**: Perrow and Leveson's model showing that safety is an emergent property. Failures result from an erosion of control over safety constraints rather than individual component failures.
* **Drift into Failure**: Turner, Vaughan, and Dekker's model showing that disasters incubate over time as small departures from rules accumulate. See [Rasmussen's safety boundary model](rasmussen-safety-boundary-model.md) for the specific formulation of the boundaries a system drifts toward.

Understanding these models is key to shifting from [safety-I vs safety-II](safety-i-vs-safety-ii.md) and managing [practical drift](practical-drift.md).
