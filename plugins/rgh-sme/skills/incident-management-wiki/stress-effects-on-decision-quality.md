Contrary to the common assumption that stress directly "warps" judgment,
Gary Klein's review of studies across fireground commanders, pilots, and
nurses found no strong evidence that stress corrupts decision quality given
the information at hand. What stress actually does is narrower and more
specific:

- **It limits how much information gets gathered** — under time pressure,
  people sample fewer cues, not necessarily worse ones. An experienced
  responder adapts by deliberately focusing on the most relevant cues and
  discarding the rest, which is a skill, not a failure.
- **It disrupts working-memory rehearsal**, which particularly costs tasks
  that depend on holding a sequence in mind, such as [mental
  simulation](mental-simulation-as-evaluation-tool.md).
- **It captures attention directly** — coping with noise, fear, or physical
  discomfort is itself a competing task alongside the actual decision.

The practical implication is that stress hits **comparative** decision
strategies (explicitly weighing multiple options against each other) far
harder than [recognition-primed
decisions](recognition-primed-decision-model.md), because comparison
depends on holding several option sets in mind at once — exactly the
working-memory capacity stress erodes. An experienced responder relying on
pattern recognition is comparatively stress-resistant; a novice trying to
compare options from first principles is not. This is one more argument
for building deep [preparedness](preparedness-drills.md) in advance rather
than trying to teach analytical rigor for the moment of the incident
itself.

## Recognizing and precommitting against decision spirals

A bad outcome earlier in a long incident can degrade every decision that
follows it — poker players call this **tilt**: a setback triggers a strong
emotional reaction, the same physiological response that shuts down
higher-level reasoning under threat, and the responder's judgment keeps
degrading in a self-reinforcing spiral for as long as the emotional state
persists. It's recognizable from the outside before the person experiencing
it necessarily notices it themselves — a responder who becomes visibly
frustrated, starts making sharper or more clipped statements, or begins
repeating "again?" or "seriously?" is showing the same warning signs as a
player on tilt, and is a candidate for a [transfer of
command](transfer-of-command.md) or a rotation off the task rather than
being pushed to power through.

Because a person on tilt often can't reliably catch their own state in the
moment, the more robust fix is a **precommitment made in advance**, while
calm — the same logic as Ulysses having his crew bind him to the mast
before the Sirens' song could affect his judgment. A pre-agreed rule set
before an incident starts (a fixed threshold for escalating to [Unified
Command](unified-command.md), a maximum number of hours before mandatory
[transfer of command](transfer-of-command.md), an explicit rule for when
to stop attempting a risky mitigation and fall back to a known-safe state)
survives the moment stress actually degrades judgment, in a way that
relying on someone's in-the-moment self-discipline does not — by the time
the pressure is on, the decision has already effectively been made.
