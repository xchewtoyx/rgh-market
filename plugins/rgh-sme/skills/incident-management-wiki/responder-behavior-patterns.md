title: Responder Behavior Patterns
description: A field guide of recurring, non-clinical behavior patterns an incident commander encounters in responders, each paired with a tactic for keeping the response on track.
sources:
  - title: "Incident Management for Operations"
    resource: "Incident Management for Operations (Rob Schnepp, Ron Vidal, Chris Hawley), ch. 3"
---

Managing an incident is tracked along two axes at once: the situational,
time-stealing issues on the channel, and the personalities present — a group
of entirely competent responders can still underperform from interpersonal
friction or fatigue. The source offers fourteen recurring, informally
observed (explicitly not clinical) responder patterns an [incident
commander](incident-command-system.md) learns to recognize, each with a
tactic:

- **The quiet one** holds valuable input but won't offer it unsolicited —
  ask them direct, name-targeted questions rather than assuming disinterest.
- **The naysayer** dissents loudly and often, citing past history against
  the current plan — demand specifics and data rather than sparring, but
  don't dismiss them outright since they aren't always wrong.
- **The overbearing one** and **the gunslinger** both use forceful
  confidence to dominate the channel; the tactic for both is the same —
  don't mistake confidence for correctness, cross-check their claims against
  other SMEs, and keep the relationship transactional rather than engaging
  them one-on-one.
- **The over explainer** answers a yes/no question with a full history
  lesson — give them a time box up front ("explain that in a minute or
  less") and interrupt once you have what you need.
- **The joker** and **the interrupter** both disrupt the channel's focus —
  name the behavior directly and redirect attention rather than indulging
  it, since it licenses others to do the same.
- **The uncertain contributor** hedges indefinitely rather than commit — ask
  yes/no-forcing questions or a numeric certainty scale rather than open
  questions that invite more hedging.
- **The grenade thrower** and **chicken little** both manufacture doubt with
  "what if" framing and worst-case scenarios — refocus on verifiable facts
  via a [CAN report](can-report.md), and separate *possible* from *likely*
  by asking for a probability estimate rather than a yes/no.
- **The lurker** joins without identifying and may drop off unnoticed —
  require every arriver to identify by name and function, and on longer
  incidents run a roll-call-based accountability check of who remains
  engaged.
- **The jumper to conclusions** decides fast from pattern-matching and may
  or may not be right — keep the group anchored to the distinction between
  fact and opinion, and ask them to produce supporting evidence.
- **The tunnel rat** fixates on one position and resists contrary evidence,
  sometimes questioning others' competence to discredit them — it's
  acceptable for the commander to agree to disagree and move the response
  forward rather than getting pulled into circular argument.

The recommended remediation path when one of these patterns actually harmed
the response is twofold: surface it constructively in the [after action
review](after-action-review.md) if others on the channel noticed it too
(silence here undermines the commander's own authority), or have a private,
behavior-focused conversation with the individual or their manager
afterward — never a public confrontation during the live incident.

These patterns interact with a broader leadership discipline the source
calls **TIME** — Tone (the demeanor the commander's own conduct sets, which
responders mirror), Interaction (are people participating fully and
respectfully), Management (tracking situations and personalities together,
since neither alone predicts performance), and Engagement (getting the
right people involved through disciplined [dispatch, not
notification](dispatch-vs-notification.md)).
