title: Procedural Heroism
description: Redefining heroism in high-stakes response as disciplined adherence to procedure and reliance on a team, not solo improvisation.
sources:
  - title: "The Checklist Manifesto"
    resource: "The Checklist Manifesto: How to Get Things Right (Atul Gawande), ch. 8"
---

A persistent cultural belief holds that using a checklist or runbook is
"beneath" a truly capable practitioner — that the greats improvise under
pressure rather than following a protocol. This belief is the primary
obstacle to [runbook](runbooks-and-checklists.md) adoption, not ignorance
of the evidence for their effectiveness: it runs directly against a
professional identity built on individual skill and autonomy.

The Hudson River ditching (US Airways Flight 1549) is the counterexample
usually cited to overturn it. Captain Sullenberger repeatedly rejected the
press narrative crediting his individual instinct and gliding experience,
insisting instead: "this was a crew effort." What actually saved the flight
was disciplined adherence to procedure under extreme pressure (the first
step of the engine-failure checklist is literally "fly the airplane,"
because pilots have been observed becoming so absorbed in restart
procedures that they stop flying), deliberate [team
formation](crew-resource-management.md) before the crisis rather than
reactive improvisation, and willingness to let the checklist and the
aircraft's automation carry part of the cognitive load instead of holding
it all personally.

The practical implication for incident response: praising and promoting
individual firefighting heroics — the engineer who single-handedly
untangles a crisis from memory — reinforces exactly the wrong model.
Treating disciplined runbook use and team coordination as the mark of
skill, not its absence, is what [sustainable on-call
design](sustainable-on-call-design.md) and blameless learning depend on.
