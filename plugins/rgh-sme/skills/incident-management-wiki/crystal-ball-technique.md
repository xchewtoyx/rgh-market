Reviewing a sample of self-identified genuine errors across field studies,
Klein found most traced to inadequate experience or missing information —
but a distinct minority were cases where the decision-maker actually
noticed several individually-weak warning signs and explained each one away
separately rather than recognizing them as a single converging pattern
(the same mechanism as the [garden path
fallacy](plan-continuation-bias.md)). The countermeasure is the **crystal
ball technique**: practiced during calm periods, not live incidents —
deliberately imagine being told your current read of a situation is wrong,
and force yourself to construct a different explanation for the same
evidence. The value isn't the specific alternative it produces; it's
learning to recognize the internal *feel* of having fixated on one
hypothesis, a recognition skill that transfers to real time pressure once
practiced.

A related system-design point worth carrying into [preparedness
work](preparedness-drills.md): layering more automated safeguards on top
of a system (to catch small errors before anyone notices them) can make the
rare error that does slip through *harder* to diagnose, because by the time
it surfaces, responders must untangle the original problem plus the
effects of every automated correction that already tried and failed to
fix it invisibly. The alternative design principle is making malfunctions
visible as they happen rather than silently absorbing them — giving
responders an earlier, clearer signal to reason from, instead of trusting
ever-more-elaborate automation to catch everything before a human ever
needs to look.
