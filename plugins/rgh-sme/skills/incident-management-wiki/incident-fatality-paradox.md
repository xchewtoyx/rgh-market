title: Incident-Fatality Paradox
description: The empirical finding that organizations reporting fewer minor incidents often suffer from higher fatality rates.
sources:
  - title: "The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error'"
    resource: "The Field Guide to Understanding 'Human Error' (Sidney Dekker), ch. 7"
---

The **Incident-Fatality Paradox** directly refutes the traditional **Heinrich's Safety Triangle**, which assumes that suppressing minor incident reports proportionally reduces the risk of major fatalities.

Scientific research shows the opposite:
* **Finland Construction Study (Saloniemi & Oksanen 1998)**: A 14-year study demonstrated a strong negative correlation ($r = -0.82$, $p < 0.001$) between non-fatal incidents and fatalities. Sites with the lowest reported incidents suffered the *highest* fatality rates.
* **Aviation Study (Barnett & Wang 2000)**: Showed that airlines with higher rates of serious non-fatal accident and near-miss reporting had *lower* passenger mortality risk ($r = -0.34$).

These findings prove that minor incidents and catastrophic failures are driven by completely different causal mechanisms. Suppressing minor incident reports (either through fear, punishment, or rate-based incentives) creates a false sense of safety, isolating the organization from learning. Encouraging high reporting of near misses is a vital indicator of system safety, which must be supported by [blameless postmortems](blameless-postmortems.md) and avoiding [survivorship bias in safety](survivorship-bias-in-safety.md).
