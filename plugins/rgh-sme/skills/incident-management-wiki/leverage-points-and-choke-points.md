A **leverage point** is a small difference that makes a large difference —
the detail a response gets built around rather than a fully-formed plan
arrived at all at once. A physician facing a newborn with an obstructed,
invisible airway had no established procedure for the situation; a distant
memory of an EMT locating an airway by watching for **bubbles** supplied
the leverage point, and he had to improvise a way to produce a bubble
(pressing on the infant's chest to force out trapped air) before he could
use it. The leverage point wasn't a solution — it was the one idea the rest
of the plan got built around. This is a different move from [recognition-
primed decision making](recognition-primed-decision-model.md): it's not
retrieving a known typical response to a familiar situation, it's
constructing a genuinely new one from a fragment of related experience,
the way a rock climber finds a route by noticing which holds are usable
from where they currently stand, not by reading the whole route off the
rock face in advance.

Recognizing that a documented constraint doesn't actually apply is a
related expert skill: knowing not just what a runbook or capacity limit
says, but how it was derived and what assumptions it baked in, sometimes
reveals that a supposedly "impossible" option was only impossible under
conditions that no longer hold. This is a sharper, situation-specific
version of the general caution in [runbooks and their
limits](runbooks-and-checklists.md) — a limit worth trusting most of the
time, and worth questioning by someone who understands why it was set.

## Choke points — the same skill turned proactive

A **choke point** is a leverage point working against you: a small,
currently-quiet vulnerability that would compound badly if left alone.
Noticing one and acting before it becomes an emergency — lining up a
backup supplier on a rumor of a strike, rather than after the delivery
actually fails — buys lead time an emergency response never gets.

The harder part of this skill isn't perceiving the individual risk
factors, it's treating their *accumulation* as something worth acting on.
In one observed case, a pilot and the flight's captain had access to the
same set of individually minor risk factors (worn brakes, unexplained
extra fuel weight, a flickering indicator on equipment used during
landing, wet weather, and finally the shortest runway at the destination)
— but only the pilot treated the combination as requiring a nonroutine
response (asking for a runway change), while the captain seemed to
passively accept the default assignment until prompted. Both had the same
facts; only one of them added them up. This is the same discipline
[failure scenario mapping](failure-scenario-mapping-workshop.md) tries to
build deliberately, in advance, rather than relying on someone to notice
the accumulation live under pressure.
