---
type: concept
title: Walk Stakeholders Through the Reasoning, Not Just the Conclusion
description: >
  Present a decision to stakeholders as a step-by-step
  reasoning chain they can question at each point, instead
  of leading with the conclusion and inviting a pure
  opinion contest.
sources:
  - title: "Continuous Discovery Habits"
    resource: "Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres), ch. 13"
---

The default habit when presenting to stakeholders is to lead
with the conclusion — the roadmap, the recommendation, the
chosen option — because that is usually literally what was
asked for. This backfires: stakeholders already hold their
own opinions about the same territory, and a bare conclusion
invites a contest between opinions rather than a shared look
at the reasoning. When that contest happens, whoever
outranks the room tends to win by default, regardless of
whose reasoning was actually sounder — the "Highest Paid
Person's Opinion" (HiPPO) effect. The team creating this
dynamic is often itself the cause, by presenting the
destination without the route.

The fix is to walk the reasoning chain in order — problem or
outcome, then how the option space was mapped, then how it
was narrowed, then the evidence for the option chosen —
inviting questions and additions at each step rather than
only at the end. Two things make this work better than
presenting the conclusion alone:

- It surfaces stakeholder objections and knowledge at the
  point in the chain they actually apply, rather than as an
  undifferentiated rejection of the ending. A stakeholder who
  knows something the team missed can add it into the option
  space or the evidence directly, becoming a contributor to
  the reasoning instead of an opponent of the result.
- It gives the stakeholder room to reach the same conclusion
  themselves, rather than being told it — which matters because
  of the **curse of knowledge**: once you know your own
  reasoning, it feels obvious, and it is easy to forget the
  stakeholder does not share that context and is arriving with
  an equally-obvious-feeling conclusion of their own.

Tailor the *depth* of the walkthrough to the audience — a
close collaborator may want every step, a senior executive
only the outcome, the top few options considered and why one
won, and one supporting piece of evidence — but do not
collapse the *structure* itself even in a compressed version;
a shortened walkthrough still moves outcome → options
considered → why this one → supporting evidence, it just
skips volume, not steps.

This extends
[recommendations-begin-conversation](recommendations-begin-conversation.md)
and
[observation-interpretation-implication](observation-interpretation-implication.md)
with a specific mechanism for the walkthrough itself: move in
order through the reasoning chain and pause for input at each
link, rather than presenting the finished
observation-interpretation-implication chain as a single
block. It also gives
[compare-and-contrast-framing](compare-and-contrast-framing.md)
its natural presentation shape — stakeholders see the
alternatives that were compared, not just the survivor.

When a senior stakeholder still overrides the process despite
this, that is their prerogative; arguing for the process
itself in that moment is usually unwinnable — see
[managing-stakeholder-resistance](managing-stakeholder-resistance.md).

This assumes a reasoning chain and a recommendation already
exist. When stakeholders instead hold an outdated mental model of
the system itself — one that would reject any recommendation built
on the new reality — walking them through this chain too early can
fail for a different reason: they filter each step through the old
model. See
[decision-scenarios-to-shift-mental-models](decision-scenarios-to-shift-mental-models.md)
for the earlier-stage move of updating what stakeholders believe is
possible before a specific recommendation is presented at all.
