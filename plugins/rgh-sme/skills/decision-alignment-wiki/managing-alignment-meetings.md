An alignment meeting's main purpose is to obtain a
commitment to act, learn, or conduct further discovery.
Presenting analysis is only a minor part of the meeting.

### Prepare the Meeting as a Decision Forum
Before the meeting, plan who must attend, what authority
they have, how data and dialogue will be sequenced, which
choices are on the table, and what commitment or next step
is sought. A useful minimum attendee check: those who
contributed to the decision so far, those with the expertise
to evaluate or diagnose the option, those who will be
affected by the outcome, and any other party with a real
stake in it — a decision made without one of these groups in
the room tends to surface their objection later instead of
now. That preparation keeps
[recommendations-begin-conversation](recommendations-begin-conversation.md)
oriented toward a real decision, not a briefing.

To steer meetings toward action, facilitators should apply
the following techniques:

### 1. Control the Process, Not the Outcome
The facilitator must aggressively manage the discussion flow while preserving stakeholder ownership of the final choice. If recommendations are forced, compliance is superficial and lacks authentic commitment. 

### 2. Prevent "Escape" Tactics
Under anxiety, groups will attempt to escape the discomfort of decision-making. The facilitator must name these behaviors and steer the group back to the core decision when they attempt to:
- Lose themselves in low-level data details.
- Blame absent people or past events.
- Debate the facilitator's competence or the validity of the research.
- End the meeting with vague, non-binding approval.

### 3. Force the Decision Explicitly
A successful meeting must shift from checking understanding and addressing [managing-stakeholder-resistance](managing-stakeholder-resistance.md) directly to defining the next steps:
- **What** will be done.
- **Who** is the owner.
- **By when** it will be completed.
- **What support** is required.

By enforcing process control, the meeting itself becomes a diagnostic tool, exposing the team's operational patterns and driving them toward coordinated action.

### 4. Track Progress on a Shared Live Artifact
Run the session against one shared, visible record — a whiteboard or
its digital equivalent, not a slide deck — holding the goals, the
open questions, and a running sketch of the emerging decision.
Presentation slides read as a broadcast the room is meant to receive;
a live shared artifact reads as something the room is building
together, and checking items off it as they're resolved makes
progress toward the decision visible in real time rather than
announced only at the end. Off-topic-but-legitimate points get
written to the same artifact rather than dropped — see
[parking-lot-technique](parking-lot-technique.md). This is the
concrete mechanism behind building consensus continuously at each
step, described below.

### 5. Checkpoint Against the Agenda at the Midpoint
At roughly the halfway point, stop and compare progress against the
agenda and the goals stated at the start. If the session is behind,
say so plainly rather than quietly dropping items, and ask the room
to reprioritize what's left by importance. Stakeholders generally
accept a shorter list they helped choose over a full list rushed
through badly; naming the shortfall out loud keeps the choice of
what to cut with the room instead of the facilitator.

### Why a Well-Built Process Survives Going Off Script
Real alignment meetings rarely run exactly as planned — an architect
resigns mid-review, the thing under discussion turns out to have
already changed, a participant is unexpectedly absent — yet a
well-built decision process routinely absorbs this without losing
the room. Three conditions explain why disruption doesn't sink it:
everyone assembled genuinely wants the process to reach a real
outcome, not just perform one; the facilitator stays scrupulously
honest about what can and can't be concluded rather than bluffing
through a gap (participants detect bluffing quickly, and it costs
the facilitator's credibility for the rest of the session); and the
process itself is structured to build consensus continuously at each
step rather than saving all agreement for a single vote at the end,
so there is no single moment where the whole outcome can be
sprung as a surprise. Designing a process this way is cheap insurance
against the near-certainty that some part of it won't go as planned.
