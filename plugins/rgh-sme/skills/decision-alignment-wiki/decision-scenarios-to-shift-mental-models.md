---
type: concept
title: Decision Scenarios to Shift Mental Models
description: >
  Present stakeholders with several structurally different
  future scenarios, not a forecast, when the goal is to change
  how they see the system rather than to win one argument.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 5"
---

When decision-makers are working from a mental model of "how the
system behaves" that is about to be invalidated by events they
have not yet priced in, presenting a single forecast or
probability-weighted prediction tends to fail — it reads as one
more opinion to weigh against their existing model, not as a
reason to replace the model itself. Royal Dutch/Shell's planning
department faced exactly this in the early 1970s: they had
identified early signals of a coming oil-price discontinuity while
executives still assumed steady continued growth, and the hard
part was never the forecasting — it was getting the executives to
actually update their thinking.

The technique that worked was building **decision scenarios**:
not answers, but structured narratives of the forces at work, each
built on differing plausible assumptions, meant to give executives
their own feel for the system's dynamics and the concepts needed to
interpret incoming data — matching the same cognitive-load ceiling
found elsewhere in how people build any mental simulation, roughly
three variables and five or six transitions per scenario (see
[singular-evaluation-and-satisficing](singular-evaluation-and-satisficing.md)
for that ceiling's origin).

The specific, load-bearing detail: **use three scenarios, not
two.** One scenario mirrors the audience's existing mental model,
made explicit enough to see its own fragility (Shell's planners
privately nicknamed theirs the "three-miracle scenario," since it
depended on three unlikely assumptions all holding at once); the
other two are structurally different, not just optimistic/
pessimistic variants of the same story. Presenting only two
scenarios that differ along a single dimension reliably fails,
because the audience just mentally "splits the difference" between
them and ends up back near their starting assumption — a third,
structurally distinct scenario blocks that shortcut and forces a
genuine reconsideration of which underlying forces are actually in
play.

This is a different tool from
[walk-stakeholders-through-the-reasoning](walk-stakeholders-through-the-reasoning.md):
that technique presents a reasoning chain that already ends in a
specific recommendation, inviting stakeholders to check each step.
Decision scenarios are used earlier and for a different job —
before a specific recommendation exists, to change what
stakeholders believe is possible, so that a later recommendation
lands on updated ground instead of an unmoved one.
