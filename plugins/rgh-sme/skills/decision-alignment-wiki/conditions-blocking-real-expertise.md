---
type: concept
title: Conditions Blocking Real Expertise
description: >
  Years of tenure in a domain only builds trustworthy pattern
  recognition under conditions that make learning from
  experience possible — under the wrong conditions it just
  produces confident-sounding routine.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 16"
---

Deferring to someone's "experience" is only warranted where
experience reliably converts into real pattern-recognition skill —
and it does not do so everywhere. Real expertise fails to develop
when a domain has some combination of: being highly dynamic (the
underlying rules keep shifting under the practitioner); requiring
prediction of human behavior specifically (notoriously resistant to
reliable feedback); offering limited or delayed feedback on whether
a given call was actually right; and not providing enough
repeated, similar cases to build a real sense of typicality.
Legislative and regulatory work is a sharp illustration: a
politician can master the *procedural* craft of politics —
committee assignments, relationship-building, favor-trading —
without ever developing real predictive skill about which policies
will actually produce their intended effect, because the causal
link between a given law and its long-run societal outcome is
almost never cleanly traceable back to the decision that produced
it. The tenure is real; the expertise it is assumed to represent
often is not.

This is the load-bearing caveat behind
[recognition-primed-decision-making](recognition-primed-decision-making.md)
and
[deliberation-substitutes-for-missing-experience](deliberation-substitutes-for-missing-experience.md):
both describe why deferring to a genuine expert's direct
recognition can beat formal comparative analysis, but that argument
only holds in domains where the claimed expertise could actually
have formed. Before treating "this person has done this for twenty
years" as license to skip a comparative options analysis, check
whether the domain itself has the feedback structure — frequent,
similar, promptly-scored cases — that turns years into skill, or
whether it is one of the domains (forecasting rare events, judging
long-horizon organizational outcomes, predicting how a specific
person will react) where tenure alone does not reliably produce it.
