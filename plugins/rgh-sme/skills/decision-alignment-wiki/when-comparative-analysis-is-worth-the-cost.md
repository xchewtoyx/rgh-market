---
type: concept
title: When Comparative Analysis Is Worth the Cost
description: >
  Formal side-by-side options analysis earns its cost for
  reasons beyond inexperience — chiefly to justify a choice to
  overseers and to resolve conflict among stakeholders who
  weigh the same trade-offs differently.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 7"
---

[Deliberation-substitutes-for-missing-experience](deliberation-substitutes-for-missing-experience.md)
covers the main reason formal comparative analysis gets used:
compensating for a decision-maker's lack of pattern-matching
expertise. But field studies of experienced decision-makers found
several other conditions under which comparison is worth running
even when the deciders could otherwise recognize a workable option
directly:

- **The choice must be justified to a higher authority** that
  expects to see evidence alternatives were considered, regardless
  of whether the decider was actually uncertain.
- **Stakeholders disagree on priorities**, so a shared analytical
  framework is needed to reach a decision *together* rather than to
  help any one person decide.
- **The goal is genuinely to find the best option**, not merely a
  workable one — true optimization, as opposed to satisficing, is
  the one case where comparison is the whole point rather than a
  substitute for something else.
- **The problem has high computational complexity** (e.g.
  optimizing a portfolio across many interacting variables) that
  outstrips what pattern recognition alone can hold in mind.

Conversely, comparison tends to be skipped even by careful decision
processes under the opposite conditions: high time pressure, highly
experienced deciders, fast-changing conditions where an analysis
would be obsolete before it finished, and goals too ill-defined to
support fixed evaluation criteria across options.

**Worked case — the Denver Bullets decision.** A police
department's recommendation to switch handgun ammunition, and a
citizen group's objection to it, deadlocked politically because
each side weighed stopping-power, injury severity, and bystander
risk differently and neither trusted the other's judgment. The
resolution was a formal **compensatory** analysis (each option is
scored per dimension and dimensions are weighted, so strength on
one dimension can offset weakness on another): elicit the
politicians' own trade-off weights among the three dimensions
separately from a technical panel that blind-scored many candidate
bullets on those same dimensions, then combine the two. The
analysis surfaced a third option neither side had proposed, which
beat both the status quo and the original recommendation on the
disputed dimensions — and because the weights had been elicited
from the stakeholders themselves rather than imposed on them, all
parties accepted the result. The formal method's value here was not
finding a better answer than an expert could recognize — it was
producing a result whose legitimacy did not depend on any single
party's trusted judgment, which is precisely what stakeholder
conflict requires and pattern recognition cannot supply.

This complements
[multi-lens-comparison-without-false-precision](multi-lens-comparison-without-false-precision.md):
that note's caution against manufacturing false precision in a
weighted score applies to option sets that are genuinely subjective
and not reducible to agreed dimensions. The Denver Bullets case
differs precisely because stopping power, injury severity, and
bystander risk *were* independently measurable, and the weights
came transparently from the stakeholders being served by the
decision rather than from the analyst — the compensatory scoring
was resolving a real disagreement, not hiding one.
