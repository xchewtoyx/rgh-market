---
type: concept
title: Concrete Story as a Credibility Test
description: >
  Spell a proposed explanation or plan out as a specific,
  concrete causal chain rather than leaving it abstract — vague
  claims survive scrutiny that a fully worked-out story cannot.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 11"
---

A claim kept abstract ("the vendor's platform can scale to our
needs," "a conspiracy could easily have planted this evidence")
resists scrutiny precisely because it is never forced to specify
who did what, in what order, using what means, to produce the
observed result. The moment someone actually tries to construct
that chain in concrete detail, hidden requirements surface: steps
that need coordination no one could plausibly arrange, evidence
that should exist but doesn't, or effects that don't follow from
their supposed causes. A legal case study makes the mechanism
visible from both directions: a courtroom conspiracy theory that
sounded plausible while described only in vague, general terms
("they could have staged it") fell apart once someone forced out
its literal operational requirements — the required coincidences
and improvisations multiplied faster than the story's plausibility
could absorb them.

A good concrete story is judged by the same four criteria used to
evaluate a mental simulation (see
[singular-evaluation-and-satisficing](singular-evaluation-and-satisficing.md)
for the parallel mechanism at the individual-decision level):
**plausibility** (each step is believable, or an implausible one is
explicitly accounted for), **consistency** (the pieces don't
contradict each other), **economy** (no irrelevant padding), and
**uniqueness** (it isn't just as easily explained by an equally
plausible rival story). A proposal, root-cause finding, or
objection that cannot be walked through this way — that only holds
together as long as it stays general — has not actually been
tested yet, no matter how confident its presentation sounds.

Practical use in a decision document: when a recommendation or an
objection to it feels persuasive but slippery, ask its author (or
yourself) to narrate the specific sequence of events it implies,
naming the people, systems, and side effects involved, rather than
restating the general claim more forcefully. This is a cheap,
concrete diagnostic to run before
[fair-treatment-of-objections](fair-treatment-of-objections.md)
commits real time to litigating a claim that a five-minute concrete
walkthrough would have dissolved — and conversely, a recommendation
that survives being narrated this way in front of skeptical
stakeholders has earned real confidence, not just rhetorical
confidence.
