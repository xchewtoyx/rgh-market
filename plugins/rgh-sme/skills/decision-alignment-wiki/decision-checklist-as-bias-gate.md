---
type: concept
title: Decision Checklist as Bias Gate
description: >
  A short checklist built from catalogued past mistakes,
  applied at the moment of a high-stakes choice, counters
  overconfidence and works as a decisive gate rather than a
  slowdown.
sources:
  - title: "The Checklist Manifesto: How to Get Things Right"
    resource: "The Checklist Manifesto: How to Get Things Right (Atul Gawande), ch. 8"
---

Investors facing high-stakes, high-reward decisions
independently converged on the same countermeasure: a short
checklist run immediately before committing, built by
cataloguing their own and others' past mistakes rather than
invented from theory. One investor named the failure mode it
targets **"cocaine brain"** — the prospect of a large payoff
activates the same reward circuits as a drug high, and a
pre-committed checklist is systematic counter-pressure
against deciding in that state.

This checklist is a decision-time gate, not a data-gathering
exercise — it does not replace establishing whether the
underlying evidence is adequate (that discipline belongs to
`evidence-verification`); it exists to catch that a
known-dangerous pattern is present even when the evidence
looks solid and the decider feels certain.

The counterintuitive finding across cases: the checklist did
not slow decisions down. It created a clear decision gate,
which let one fund resolve evaluations that used to take
weeks by a fixed day, and let another triage far more
candidates in the same period. A separate study classifying
51 venture investors by decision style found the
methodical, checklist-driven group had roughly a fifth the
follow-on failure rate and more than double the median return
of every other style (intuitive snap judgment, exhaustive
data-then-gut, aggressive interrogation, relationship-driven,
or skip-evaluation-then-replace-later) — yet adoption stayed
rare, because using a checklist for a high-stakes call feels
like an admission that judgment alone cannot be trusted.

This is a different mechanism from
[communication-checklists](communication-checklists.md), which
forces multi-disciplinary alignment on unscripted problems
rather than guarding a single decider against a known bias.
It complements
[avoiding-quick-closure](avoiding-quick-closure.md) and
[judgment-pre-commitment](judgment-pre-commitment.md): all
three exist to make a decision defensible by constraining how
it gets made, not just what gets recommended.
