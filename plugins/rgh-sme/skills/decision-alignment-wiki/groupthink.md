**Groupthink** (Irving Janis) is what happens when members of a
small cohesive group maintain esprit de corps by unconsciously
developing shared illusions and norms that interfere with critical
thinking and reality testing. Groups that get along too well stop
questioning assumptions; unanimity itself becomes taken as evidence
the group is right — "we can't all be wrong, can we?"

The Bay of Pigs invasion (1961) is the canonical planning failure:
Kennedy's advisers proceeded with a covert exile invasion despite
a front-page leak and flaws visible at the time — for example, a
contingency assumed guerrillas could retreat to the Escambray
Mountains, but when planners moved the landing site to the Bay of
Pigs no one revisited the assumption; the mountains were eighty
miles away across swamp and jungle. The same advisory team later
resolved the Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) successfully — personnel
did not change; the group's decision process did.

After the Bay of Pigs, Kennedy's inquiry identified "cozy
unanimity" as the core problem and reformed deliberations for the
missile crisis:

- Skepticism became an explicit watchword; anyone could question
  anything, not only within their specialty.
- Designated **intellectual watchdogs** (Theodore Sorensen, Robert
  Kennedy) were tasked to pursue every bone of contention; Robert
  Kennedy served as an outspoken devil's advocate.
- Protocol and hierarchy were relaxed; new advisers brought fresh
  perspectives.
- Kennedy sometimes left the room so his presence would not
  suppress give-and-take.
- He kept his own initial leaning (preemptive air strikes) private
  early so it would not anchor discussion; by day's end the
  committee had seriously considered ten alternatives.

Groups can be wise or mad — what matters is not only who is in the
room but how the group is run. Deliberation destroys the
independence that makes aggregation work: once people talk together,
loud voices, bullies, superficially impressive talkers, or
credentialed figures can pull everyone toward a shared error, so
mistakes pile up instead of canceling. That is why
[individual-input-before-group-synthesis](individual-input-before-group-synthesis.md)
and keeping a leader's early lean private are structural counters,
not just cultural aspirations.

Groupthink pairs with cognitive loafing on teams — false agreement
breeds self-righteous complacency and less individual effort. The
opposite failure, rancorous dysfunction, is equally dangerous;
teams need to disagree without being disagreeable — see
[precision-questioning](precision-questioning.md) and
[dissent-without-confrontation](dissent-without-confrontation.md).
[Pre-mortem-technique](pre-mortem-technique.md) and
[productive-idea-conflict](productive-idea-conflict.md) address
late-stage suppression of doubt once a plan appears chosen.
