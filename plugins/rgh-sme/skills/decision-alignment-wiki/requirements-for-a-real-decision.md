title: Requirements for a Real Decision
description: >
  A proposal isn't actually a decision until it names two real
  alternatives, genuine uncertainty between them, a real cost to
  choosing wrong, and someone with authority to choose — check
  all four before analyzing anything.
sources:
  - title: "How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of Intangibles in Business"
    resource: "How to Measure Anything (Douglas W. Hubbard), ch. 4"

Before commissioning any analysis, options comparison, or
recommendation, check that the thing being decided actually
satisfies four requirements — skipping this check is how
organizations spend real effort analyzing something that was never
a decision in the first place:

1. **Two or more realistic alternatives.** "IT or no IT," "clean
   water or not" are not real decisions — they imply a binary that
   no one is actually choosing between. The real decision hiding
   underneath is usually narrower: which specific project, which
   specific policy, at what scope. A fast diagnostic for this
   failure: when someone asks "what's the value of X" for some
   standing function or capability, ask them directly whether
   they're actually considering doing without it — if not, "value
   of X in the abstract" isn't the real question, and pushing on
   this usually surfaces the actual comparison being made (this
   version of X versus a specific proposed change to it, or
   in-house versus a specific outsourcing alternative).
2. **Genuine uncertainty about which alternative is best.** If
   everyone already agrees on the answer, there is nothing left to
   decide — what may actually be needed is documentation or
   buy-in, not analysis.
3. **A real cost to choosing wrong** — including the case where
   every option is nominally positive but one is meaningfully
   better than another; forgoing the better option is a real cost
   even when the chosen one still "works."
4. **An identifiable decision maker** — someone (or some body)
   who can actually act on the answer. A question with no one
   positioned to act on it is a research question, not a decision.

**Common ways a proposal fails this check without anyone noticing:**

- **Circular framing** — "we need to measure progress to track
  progress" names no action that would change based on the
  answer.
- **Importance mistaken for decision-relevance** — "this matters to
  the company" is not the same claim as "an actual choice hinges on
  this."
- **An unnamed decision** — "this will help us decide better,"
  without saying which decision, commits to nothing checkable.
- **Impatience to skip straight to analysis** — building a model or
  gathering data before the decision itself is pinned down is like
  pouring concrete before an architect has chosen a site; the fix,
  even for a decision that feels obvious, is a short session with
  the actual decision-maker and the people who understand the
  domain, explicitly stating what the alternatives are before
  analyzing any of them. Some organizations that believe their
  decision is already well-defined discover, only once forced to
  write down the specific alternatives, that they never actually
  agreed on what those alternatives were.

A useful minimum test: can the choice be written as "estimated
benefit of Option A versus estimated cost, compared to the same for
Option B"? If that sentence cannot be filled in even roughly, the
decision is not yet well-defined enough for
[options analysis](compare-and-contrast-framing.md) or a
[lightweight-decision-case](lightweight-decision-case.md) to do any
real work — the missing piece is upstream of both.
