---
type: concept
title: Self-Serving Outcome Attribution
description: >
  People credit their own good outcomes to skill and their own
  bad outcomes to luck, then do the reverse for everyone else's
  outcomes — which quietly destroys the ability to learn from a
  track record.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts"
    resource: "Thinking in Bets (Annie Duke), ch. 3"
---

Separating decision quality from outcome quality (see
[decision-quality-independent-of-outcome](decision-quality-independent-of-outcome.md))
requires actually sorting each outcome into a "this was the
decision" bucket or a "this was uncontrollable circumstance" bucket
— and people sort in a strikingly consistent, self-serving pattern:
their own good outcomes go in the skill bucket, their own bad
outcomes go in the luck bucket, and both flip for everyone else's
outcomes — someone else's success gets called luck, someone else's
failure gets called a lack of skill. In accident reports, drivers
blame someone else the large majority of the time even in
single-vehicle crashes with no one else present. The pattern is
strong enough that in a genuinely zero-sum contest, crediting your
own win as skill logically forces crediting the loser's loss as
their lack of skill — the two attributions are the same claim
viewed from either side of the table.

The practical damage is not the ego protection itself; it is what
gets lost. Blaming a bad outcome on luck forfeits the chance to
examine and fix an actual decision flaw. Crediting a good outcome to
skill reinforces a decision process that may not have deserved it.
And the mirror-image bias toward *others'* outcomes cuts off a
second, larger source of learning: most of the time spent watching
someone else decide carries no stake for the observer, which should
make it a cheap, low-cost way to learn from their track record — but
the same bias that inflates a person's confidence in their own
process deflates their willingness to learn from someone else's,
by dismissing the other person's wins as luck before ever asking
whether they used a better process.

**Countermeasure**: apply
[decision-quality-independent-of-outcome](decision-quality-independent-of-outcome.md)'s
skill-versus-luck test symmetrically — ask, for both your own and
others' outcomes, whether repeating the same decision would
predictably reproduce the result. A useful check on a track record
someone reports as consistently skillful: verify they can name
mistakes inside their own wins as readily as they name bad luck
inside their own losses. A record with only success stories and
unlucky losses, and never a self-identified mistake, has not
actually been fielded honestly — it has just been filtered for
whatever protects the narrative.
