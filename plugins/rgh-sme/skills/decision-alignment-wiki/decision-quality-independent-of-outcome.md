---
type: concept
title: Decision Quality Independent of Outcome
description: >
  A decision is poor only if information available at the time
  would, correctly used, have pointed to a different choice —
  not merely because the outcome turned out badly.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 16"
  - title: "Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts"
    resource: "Thinking in Bets (Annie Duke), ch. 1"
---

The best decision reachable from the knowledge available at the
time can still produce a bad outcome — bad luck, not bad process.
Conflating the two produces two costly mistakes: crediting a lucky
bad decision as if it were good, and condemning an unlucky good one
as if it were bad. Poker players have a name for the error:
**resulting** — judging a decision's quality by its outcome instead
of by the process that produced it. A well-known NFL example:
a coach's pass-play call, intercepted for a Super Bowl-losing
outcome, was near-universally branded "the worst call in NFL
history," while a statistical review found that specific play type
had almost never been intercepted across the whole season — the
call was defensible; the outcome was simply the unlucky branch. The
coach's own postgame framing captured the error precisely: "the call
would have been a great one if we catch it" — the play's quality
had not actually changed; only the observers' knowledge of the
outcome had.

A more useful working definition: **a decision was poor if the
knowledge later gained would have led to a different decision in a
similar future situation** — that is, if there is something
specific and nameable that a comparably-placed decision-maker failed
to consider, not merely that things turned out badly. Merely knowing
the outcome does not by itself identify what, if anything, should
have been done differently. In practice, asking a group for their
"best and worst decisions" reliably produces their best and worst
*outcomes* instead — a leader who fires someone after a defensible,
well-benchmarked process, and later regrets it once results
disappoint, is exhibiting **hindsight bias**: retroactively treating
the bad outcome as though it had been obviously inevitable at
decision time, the same way no one credits a good decision to a
drunk driver who happens to get home safely.

**Redefining "wrong" the same way redefines "right."** A forecast or
recommendation stated as a probability — not a certainty — cannot be
called wrong solely because the less-likely branch occurred: a
sincerely-estimated 25% chance that came in is not a miss, and a
75% favorite that didn't land is not a failure of judgment, any more
than favored election or referendum forecasts are "wrong" when the
underdog wins. Symmetrically, credit for a good outcome should not
automatically flow back to whoever recommended it if the outcome
was itself the lower-probability branch that simply came in. This
matters because loss aversion makes outcome-based scoring feel
especially punishing: a loss registers roughly twice as strongly as
an equivalent gain, so a track record graded purely on outcomes will
feel like constant failure even under a strategy with a genuine
edge, since even a strongly favorable decision process still loses
a large share of the time.

This reframes what a post-decision review is actually for.
Discovering a bad outcome should be the *start* of an inquiry, not
its conclusion — the real analytic work is identifying which
specific factor (missing information, a design flaw, an
inexperienced call) actually produced it, since "we got a bad
result" says nothing on its own about whether the decision itself
was sound. This is what makes
[unfalsifiable-bias-framing](unfalsifiable-bias-framing.md) a real
trap to avoid: a critique attached only to the outcome, with no
specific missed factor named, has not actually located a decision
failure — it has just noticed that things went badly, which is a
different and much weaker claim.

**A useful image for why hindsight distorts this so reliably**:
picture the future as a tree with many branches meeting one growing
trunk at the present. As events resolve, the branch that actually
happened gets treated as though it had been the whole tree all
along, and every branch that didn't happen disappears from view —
even a genuinely unlikely branch looks, in hindsight, as if it had
been the only one possible. A U.S. federal appeals court overturned
a large jury verdict on exactly this basis, with the presiding judge
naming hindsight bias directly as the reason the verdict could not
stand: the jury had treated a bad outcome as proof the responsible
party must have known it was coming, when the available information
at the time did not actually make it foreseeable.

Sorting a given outcome into "this was the decision" versus "this
was luck" is itself a judgment people make in a strikingly biased
way — see
[self-serving-outcome-attribution](self-serving-outcome-attribution.md)
for the specific, predictable direction that bias runs and how to
counteract it.

Practically, this argues for building
[judgment-pre-commitment](judgment-pre-commitment.md) and a
[searchable-decision-archive](searchable-decision-archive.md) that
records the reasoning and the information actually available at
decision time, not just the eventual result — that record is the
only thing that lets a later reviewer tell "we made a good call and
got unlucky" apart from "we made a bad call and got away with it,"
two situations that look identical from the outcome alone.
