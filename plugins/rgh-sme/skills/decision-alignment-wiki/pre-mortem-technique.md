A **pre-mortem** (Gary Klein) is a post-mortem run in advance:
when an organization has almost but not formally committed to
an important decision, gather knowledgeable people and give
this premise — "Imagine we are a year into the future. We
implemented the plan as it now exists. The outcome was a
disaster. Take five to ten minutes to write a brief history
of that disaster" — then works backward from the imagined
failure to name what went wrong.

The reframing does real psychological work, not just rhetorical
work: once a plan is built, people become emotionally invested in
its success, and asking them to critique their own plan directly
produces only half-hearted scrutiny. Recasting the task as "explain
why this obviously failed" turns it into a creativity and
competence challenge — finding smart failure modes — rather than a
threat to defend against, which is why it reliably surfaces flaws
that direct self-critique misses. In practice the imagining step
itself is fast (well under ten minutes), though the ensuing
discussion of the causes it surfaces can run much longer; it is
cheap enough to run routinely at the kickoff of any new commitment,
not reserved for especially risky ones. A military mission-rehearsal
study makes the cost of skipping it concrete: helicopter crews
rehearsing a precisely timed mission never once asked, during
rehearsal, what to do if they arrived early or late — no contingency
planning occurred to anyone — and only one of twenty crews then hit
the actual timing window.

The effectiveness of this technique depends on a specific
framing detail: the failure must be posed as *certain*
("this failed — what happened?"), not merely *possible*
("this might fail — what could go wrong?"). Research on
"prospective hindsight" found the certain-outcome framing is
what improves the quality of the explanations generated;
hedged, merely-possible framing does not produce the same
gain. Committing mentally to the failure having already
happened lets people generate specific, concrete causes they
would otherwise censor while still trying to defend the plan
as sound.

Run it at the last moment before formal commitment, when a
team has already converged on a plan — that is exactly when
the technique earns its keep. As a group moves toward a
decision, especially once the leader has tipped her hand,
public doubts are gradually suppressed and eventually treated
as evidence of disloyalty; only supporters of the chosen
plan still have a voice, which feeds overconfidence. A
pre-mortem overcomes that late-stage
[groupthink](individual-input-before-group-synthesis.md) by
licensing each knowledgeable person to imagine failure in
writing before the room locks in, and it directs imagination
toward threats the plan's coherence story has screened out.
It is one way to counter
[avoiding-quick-closure](avoiding-quick-closure.md) and the
escalation-of-commitment pattern where the more a team has
invested in an idea, the harder it becomes to see its flaws —
running it early, before investment accumulates, is what
makes the technique cheap. It pairs naturally with
[decision-checklist-as-bias-gate](decision-checklist-as-bias-gate.md)
as another lightweight, repeatable gate applied before a
commitment rather than after.

The underlying mechanism — imagining execution to surface failure
points that a plan looks fine without — is the same one behind
[singular-evaluation-and-satisficing](singular-evaluation-and-satisficing.md),
where an individual expert mentally simulates a single option
before committing to it. A pre-mortem is that mechanism made
deliberate, adversarial, and group-run against a proposal that has
already survived initial scrutiny, rather than a split-second
private check.

A pre-mortem is the negative-space half of a pair — see
[backcasting-technique](backcasting-technique.md) for the positive-
space complement (imagining the goal already achieved and working
backward), and why running only one of the two inherits a
predictable optimism or pessimism bias the other half exists to
correct.

A pre-mortem is run once, before commitment. See
[predefined-abandonment-triggers](predefined-abandonment-triggers.md)
for the matching discipline once the plan is actually executing:
naming in advance the specific, observable conditions that would mean
this plan (not a hypothetical one) has failed and should be dropped.

A pre-mortem is not a panacea: it reduces damage from plans
built on uncritical optimism and coherence-over-evidence
storytelling, but it does not fully protect against nasty
surprises once execution begins.
