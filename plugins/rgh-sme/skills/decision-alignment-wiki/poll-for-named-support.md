---
type: concept
title: Poll for Named Support, Not Consensus
description: >
  Have the decision-owner explicitly ask each relevant party by
  name whether they support a plan, rather than inferring
  agreement from silence — and ask for disagreement, not agreement,
  to get the answer faster.
sources:
  - title: "Incident Management for Operations"
    resource: "Incident Management for Operations (Rob Schnepp, Ron Vidal, Chris Hawley), ch. 3"
---

Silence on a call or in a room is not agreement — it is the absence
of a signal, and treating it as agreement lets real doubt travel
forward undetected until the plan is already underway. The fix used
by incident commanders under real time pressure is to poll each
relevant party by name for explicit support ("Database, do you
support the plan?") rather than asking the room generally and moving
on when no one objects. A named question requires a named answer,
which surfaces hesitation while it's still cheap to address.

**Tone matters as much as the word.** A flat, low-conviction "I
support the plan" is itself information — a decision-owner who is
actually listening treats a weakly voiced yes as a cue to probe
further, not as a box checked. Text-based confirmation loses this
signal entirely, which is one reason a live, voiced check is worth
the extra time it costs over an async approval.

**Ask about disagreement, not agreement, to save real time.** "Does
everyone agree?" requires a full round of individual affirmations to
be answered honestly. "Is there any disagreement?" only requires a
response from someone who actually has one — silence is a valid,
fast answer to that specific question in a way it is not to the
first. Shaving this kind of redundant back-and-forth out of every
single exchange compounds directly into a faster overall decision.

**The bar is support, not unanimous enthusiasm.** A decision-owner
who conflates "this plan is wrong" with "this is not what I would
have chosen" will chase an unreachable 100% agreement instead of
moving forward — the two are different objections and only the first
is a reason to change course. It is legitimate to acknowledge a
dissenting preference, confirm it doesn't rise to "this won't work,"
and proceed.

This is a live, spoken variant of the same problem
[confidence-vote-for-commitment](confidence-vote-for-commitment.md)
solves for planning meetings: a flat yes/no ask suppresses the
signal a graduated or individually-targeted question would surface.
It also gives
[escalating-unresolved-disagreements](escalating-unresolved-disagreements.md)
its trigger — a named party who states real disagreement, not mere
preference, is exactly the signal that should route to escalation
rather than being steamrolled or silently overridden.
