---
type: concept
title: Expected-Value Scenario Planning
description: >
  Enumerate the possible outcomes of a decision with rough
  probabilities attached, and prioritize or budget by the
  resulting expected value rather than by a single most-likely
  or largest-possible number.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts"
    resource: "Thinking in Bets (Annie Duke), ch. 6"
---

Two steps turn a vague sense of "this might work out" into
something a decision can actually be built on: enumerate the
realistic range of outcomes a decision could produce, then attach a
rough probability to each — even a wide range like "20 to 80
percent" beats no estimate at all, since refusing to estimate a
probability is not actually avoiding a guess, it is just leaving
the guess implicit and unexamined.

**Worked case**: a grant-seeking organization budgeted from
requested amounts rather than expected value, treating a larger ask
as a better prospect regardless of its odds. Once each grant was
scored as probability times award size, a $50,000 grant at 70%
odds ($35,000 expected value) ranked above a $200,000 grant at 10%
odds ($20,000 expected value) — the nominally "smaller" opportunity
was actually the more valuable one to pursue. Making this explicit
changed staffing priorities, produced more realistic budgeting, and
built a habit of following up on both wins and losses to keep the
probability estimates calibrated over time.

**Worked case — revisiting a contested call**: a widely-criticized
game-ending play call looks different once the full branch
structure is mapped rather than just the two headline options.
Mapping every realistic outcome of each candidate play (score,
turnover, incompletion, penalty, sack) revealed a structural
asymmetry invisible from the outside — one option preserved an
extra scoring attempt that the other did not, at a turnover cost of
only a few percent — an advantage no amount of arguing about "which
play is safer" in the abstract would have surfaced, but that a
built-out probability tree made visible immediately.

This differs from
[decision-scenarios-to-shift-mental-models](decision-scenarios-to-shift-mental-models.md):
that technique uses a small number of qualitative, structurally
distinct narratives to change what an audience believes is
possible before a recommendation exists. This technique instead
quantifies a fuller branch structure to rank or budget among
already-identified options, and is the natural next step once
[compounding-probability-in-sequential-plans](compounding-probability-in-sequential-plans.md)
has flagged that a plan's realistic odds need to be stated
explicitly rather than assumed from its individual steps.
