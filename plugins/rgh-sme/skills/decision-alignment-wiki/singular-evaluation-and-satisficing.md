type: concept
title: Singular Evaluation and Satisficing
description: >
  Evaluate one option at a time against a "good enough" bar via
  mental simulation, moving to the next candidate only if it
  fails, instead of scoring several options against each other.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 3"
---

**Singular evaluation** judges one option purely on its own
merits — accept it if it seems workable, reject and move to the
next most typical option if it doesn't — as opposed to
**comparative evaluation**, which weighs two or more options
against each other before choosing. The everyday contrast: picking
from a restaurant menu is comparative (find the tastiest item);
choosing a gas station when low on fuel in unfamiliar territory is
singular (stop at the first reasonable one, since finding the
*best* station in town isn't worth the search).

Singular evaluation is grounded in Herbert Simon's concept of
**satisficing** — accepting the first option that clears a
sufficiency bar — as distinct from **optimizing**, which searches
for the provably best option and costs more time and information
to do. In studied field decisions, experienced people relied on
satisficing even under conditions where they had days or weeks to
decide, not only under extreme time pressure.

The mechanism that makes single-option evaluation reliable is
mental simulation: the decision-maker imagines carrying the option
out, and a problem surfaces as an imagined failure point rather
than as a worse score than a rival option. A rescue-scenario field
example: a fire officer mentally walked through three standard
rescue-harness techniques in sequence, rejecting each because
imagining its use revealed a specific injury risk to the victim,
before landing on and executing a fourth, improvised method — the
whole sequence took about a minute. The technique is not foolproof
(the chosen method in that example still had an unforeseen flaw,
caught only by luck), but is reported as "usually better than
anything else" available under real constraints.

which explains why experienced people default to singular

than to a single individual's split-second choice.
