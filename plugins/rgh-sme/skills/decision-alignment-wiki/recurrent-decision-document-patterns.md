---
type: concept
title: Recurrent Decision-Document Patterns
description: >
  Common business decision documents follow a small set of
  recurring argument patterns that a writer can match to the
  document's genre instead of inventing structure from scratch.
sources:
  - title: "The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking and Problem Solving"
    resource: "The Minto Pyramid Principle (Barbara Minto), ch. 4"
---

Once the
[problem-stage-question](problem-stage-question.md) is
identified, most decision documents fall into one of a few
recurring genres, each with its own expected argument shape:

- **Directives** — frame a required action and answer with
  the steps to carry it out.
- **Spending / investment requests** — must defend four
  things together: urgency (why now), adequacy of the
  proposed solution, the financial case, and any additional
  benefit beyond the immediate ask. Omitting one of the four
  leaves the request open to an obvious objection. A
  [lightweight-decision-case](lightweight-decision-case.md)
  keeps this genre to a page or two of essential fields rather
  than a full specification.
- **How-to documents** — derive steps by comparing the
  current process against the desired one; the gap itself
  generates the action list.
- **Known-alternative assessments** — evaluate a fixed set
  of options against explicit criteria or a stated objective
  ([desired-result-as-decision-criterion](desired-result-as-decision-criterion.md)),
  rather than against each other's pros and cons.

Consulting proposals and progress reviews follow their own
variants of the same
[problem-definition-framework](problem-definition-framework.md)
logic. Recognizing which pattern a document belongs to early
prevents missing a component readers will expect (e.g. a
spending request with no financial case) and keeps the
document aimed at a single
[problem-stage-question](problem-stage-question.md) instead
of drifting across several.
