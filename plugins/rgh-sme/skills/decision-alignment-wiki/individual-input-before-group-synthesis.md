---
type: concept
title: Individual Input Before Group Synthesis
description: >
  Have each participant produce their own view privately
  before group discussion, then merge, to counter groupthink
  and separate real disagreement from illusory disagreement.
sources:
  - title: "Continuous Discovery Habits"
    resource: "Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres), ch. 4"
  - title: "How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of Intangibles in Business"
    resource: "How to Measure Anything (Douglas W. Hubbard), ch. 12"
  - title: "Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction"
    resource: "Superforecasting (Philip E. Tetlock, Dan Gardner), ch. 9"
---

Groupthink degrades group decisions through uneven effort,
hesitation to voice a dissenting view once others have
spoken, and a ceiling on group output near the
least-engaged member. Aggregation across people only cancels
out error when judgments are formed **independently** first —
as in the classic fairground ox-weight-guessing example. Once
a group deliberates together, independence collapses: loudmouths,
bullies, superficially impressive talkers, or credentialed figures
can pull everyone toward a shared mistake, so errors compound
instead of averaging out. The counter is procedural, not
aspirational: require each participant to produce their own
version of the analysis (a map, a list of options, a written
position) independently, before any group discussion happens
— even though solo-then-merge feels slower and more
duplicative than jumping straight to a group conversation.

The pressure to conform to a group's first-voiced answer is
strong even on questions with an unambiguous right answer: in a
classic experiment, subjects picking a correct match from an
obvious visual comparison scored it right alone 99% of the time,
but dropped to 87% with two people answering incorrectly ahead of
them, and to just 53% when a reward for the group's unanimous
correctness added social pressure on top. Open discussion before
anyone has committed their own view invites exactly this pressure;
collecting independent answers first removes the opportunity for
it to operate before real disagreement is even visible.

Externalizing each person's view first (in writing or a
drawing, not just a spoken opinion) also forces a level of
specificity that talk alone lets people skate past — vague
verbal agreement can hide real differences, and a forced
concrete artifact surfaces them faster than continued
discussion would. This cuts the other direction too: a group
that seems deadlocked in debate has often already reached
agreement without recognizing it, because each side is
restating its position in different words; comparing the
concrete individual artifacts shows whether a standoff is a
genuine disagreement or a semantic one, before more meeting
time is spent on it either way.

Merge the individual views by pooling them, collapsing
genuinely duplicate points without erasing real distinctions,
and letting the group's shared version be richer than any one
person's starting view — this is the payoff that justifies
the extra procedural step. This complements
[decision-checklist-as-bias-gate](decision-checklist-as-bias-gate.md)
(guards a single decider against a known bias) and
[surface-ambivalence-early](surface-ambivalence-early.md)
(names mixed motives directly) as another way a decision
process constrains *how* a call gets made, not just what gets
recommended.

The same split applies to generating options, not just
analysis: research comparing group brainstorming to the same
people generating ideas alone consistently finds individuals
outperform groups on quantity, diversity, and novelty of
ideas, because groups suffer social loafing, early ideas
anchoring later ones toward conformity, one speaker blocking
another's train of thought, and performance sinking toward
the least-engaged member — even though groups report an
"illusion of group productivity" because sharing ideas makes
individual members feel less stuck. The synthesis that keeps
the benefit without the cost: alternate solo generation with
rounds of sharing (not group brainstorming itself) — each
person ideates alone, the group shares and riffs, then
everyone generates alone again, informed by what they heard.
Groups do outperform individuals at the following step,
*evaluating* an already-generated set — see
[dot-voting-to-narrow-options](dot-voting-to-narrow-options.md)
for that stage. See also [groupthink](groupthink.md) for how
cohesive groups mistake unanimity for correctness when independence
is not preserved.
