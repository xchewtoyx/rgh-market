---
type: concept
title: Reversibility as a Decision Criterion
description: >
  Match analysis depth and decision authority to whether a
  decision is a one-way or two-way door, not to its topic
  or apparent size.
sources:
  - title: "Continuous Discovery Habits"
    resource: "Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres), ch. 2"
---

Jeff Bezos's distinction: a **one-way-door decision** is
hard or costly to reverse (shutting down a product line,
signing a multi-year contract); a **two-way-door decision**
is cheap to reverse if it turns out wrong (most product,
feature, and process choices).

The two types warrant different treatment. One-way doors
justify slow, careful, heavily-analyzed, broadly-consulted
decisions — the cost of being wrong is high enough to pay for
that scrutiny. Two-way doors do not: the team should decide
quickly with available evidence and correct course once it
learns more, because the cost of reversing a bad call is low
but the cost of analysis paralysis compounds with every
similar decision left undecided. Treating a two-way door with
one-way-door rigor is the more common failure — it slows an
entire class of everyday decisions to the pace the rare
irreversible one actually requires.

Applying this criterion first — before debating the decision's
content — determines how much process the decision earns:
how many people must weigh in
([stakeholder-analysis-attributes](stakeholder-analysis-attributes.md)),
how much evidence is enough, and whether the choice can be
revisited later without re-litigating it from scratch
(bearing on what gets recorded per
[decision-engagement-phases](decision-engagement-phases.md)).

Explicitly framing a decision as reversible also guards
against confirmation bias after the fact: research on
decision framing found that people who view a choice as
reversible keep critically evaluating it afterward — noticing
both their choice's flaws and the alternatives' merits —
while people who frame the same choice as irreversible
selectively notice only their choice's strengths and the
alternatives' weaknesses. Calling a two-way door a two-way
door is therefore not just a speed tactic; it keeps the team
able to see disconfirming evidence once the decision is made,
which
[multi-lens-comparison-without-false-precision](multi-lens-comparison-without-false-precision.md)
depends on staying open to revisiting.
