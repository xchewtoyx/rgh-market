---
type: concept
title: Sponsorship Versus Mandate
description: >
  Distinguish an executive saying "I support this" from an
  executive granting "this is now required" — a decision that
  only secured the former will not actually stick.
sources:
  - title: "Observability Engineering (2nd Edition)"
    resource: "Observability Engineering (2nd Edition) (Charity Majors, Liz Fong-Jones, George Miranda), ch. 28"
---

**Sponsorship** is an executive expressing approval or goodwill
toward a proposal — "I support this," a nod in a meeting, a
line in an email. **Mandate** is that same executive granting
enforceable authority — "new work must use this approach, and
that requirement sticks" — that makes the decision binding on
people who were not in the room when it was made.

Treating sponsorship as if it were mandate is a common way a
decision that appeared to succeed quietly fails afterward: the
proposal was approved, nobody objected, and yet nothing changed
in practice, because no one with sponsorship-only backing has
the standing to hold a line when someone with real authority
first asks for an exception. Before treating a decision as
settled, ask specifically who has agreed to *support* it and,
separately, who has agreed to *require* it — the two lists are
often not the same people, and a proposal that only has the
first list has not actually been decided yet.

When seeking backing for a proposal, identify which executive
actually owns the pain the proposal addresses most directly —
not necessarily the most senior person available — and pursue
mandate from them specifically, rather than collecting broad
but shallow sponsorship from people adjacent to the decision.

This sharpens
[multi-party-decision-contracts](multi-party-decision-contracts.md)'s
distinction between who must agree, be informed, or can veto:
sponsorship without mandate is a party who agreed in the room
but was never actually empowered to make the agreement stick.
It also sets up the real test described in
[stakeholder-buy-in-sequencing](stakeholder-buy-in-sequencing.md) —
the first time someone with authority asks for an exception —
by making clear in advance who has the standing to grant or
refuse that exception, and connects to
[single-champion-fragility](single-champion-fragility.md): a
decision resting on sponsorship alone is exactly as fragile as
one resting on a single champion, because both depend on one
person's continued goodwill rather than on anything binding.
