---
type: concept
title: Recognition-Primed Decision Making
description: >
  Experienced decision-makers under time pressure recognize a
  situation as a familiar pattern and evaluate one plausible
  action at a time, rather than comparing multiple options.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 2-3"
---

Studies of experienced decision-makers under real time pressure
and high stakes (fireground commanders, pilots, ICU nurses)
found they almost never compare multiple options side by side.
In a 156-decision-point field study, 80 percent of decisions
showed no evidence of comparing alternatives at all — the
decision-maker recognized the situation as an instance of a
familiar pattern and acted on the first course of action that
came to mind. Formal option-comparison appeared mainly where
experience was genuinely absent (novices, or a situation with no
precedent for anyone present).

The **recognition-primed decision (RPD) model** describes what
happens instead: recognizing a situation as typical
simultaneously supplies plausible goals, the cues worth
attending to, expectancies about what should happen next (so
deviations are noticed as surprises), and a typical action —
these arrive together as a package, not as separate steps built
up from first principles. When a situation does not cleanly
match a known pattern, or an expectancy is violated, the
decision-maker shifts to diagnosing which interpretation actually
fits before an action suggests itself. Either way, a single
candidate action is then checked out, not chosen from a menu —
see
[singular-evaluation-and-satisficing](singular-evaluation-and-satisficing.md)
for how that check is done.

This directly bears on how much option-comparison a decision
document should demand. See
[deliberation-substitutes-for-missing-experience](deliberation-substitutes-for-missing-experience.md)
for when the classic canvas-all-options rational choice strategy
is genuinely earning its keep versus when it is a costly ritual
imposed on people who could recognize the right call directly.

This only holds where the claimed expertise could plausibly have
formed — see
[conditions-blocking-real-expertise](conditions-blocking-real-expertise.md)
for domains where accumulated tenure does not reliably produce real
pattern-recognition skill, and deferring to it is unwarranted.

This is not the same failure mode as
[avoiding-quick-closure](avoiding-quick-closure.md): quick
closure is settling on the first coherent idea in an
ill-structured, unfamiliar strategic situation to escape the
discomfort of ambiguity, before the situation is even understood.
Recognition-priming instead follows real situation assessment by
someone who has seen enough similar cases to assess it
correctly — the two look superficially alike (act on the first
idea) but the difference is whether genuine pattern-matching
expertise is doing the work.
