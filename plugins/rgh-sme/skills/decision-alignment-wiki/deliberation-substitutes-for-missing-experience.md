---
type: concept
title: Deliberation Substitutes for Missing Experience
description: >
  Formal comparative options analysis earns its cost mainly where
  the deciders lack the experience to recognize a good option
  directly, not as a general-purpose best practice.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 3"
---

Field studies of experienced decision-makers found their rare
uses of classic comparative option analysis — listing options,
weighing advantages and disadvantages of each against the others —
clustered almost entirely in situations where the deciders'
experience genuinely did not cover the case at hand. In one
extended incident (a multi-day industrial fire far larger than
any responder present had encountered), the *only* period of real
side-by-side option comparison came once local responders admitted
their pattern-matching had nothing to offer and switched into
deliberate planning; the incident was ultimately resolved by
bringing in outside experts who could recognize the right approach
directly.

The implication for decision documents: the classical rational
choice strategy (list options, weight evaluation dimensions, score
each option, total the weighted scores) has real advantages —
reproducible, quantitative, exposes what a novice doesn't know,
leaves nothing out, general-purpose. But it is a costly stand-in
for expertise a novice doesn't yet have, not a mark of rigor a
document should always perform. Demanding it from people who can
already recognize the right call directly adds cost without adding
decision quality, and can even signal to readers that the
recommender lacks confidence born of real experience. The strategy
does retain one distinct use even among experienced people: calibrating a
*team's* shared understanding of the relative strengths and
weaknesses of the live options, which matters for
[stakeholder-alignment](stakeholder-buy-in-sequencing.md)
independent of whether it changes the individual decision-maker's
own choice.

Practical read for framing a recommendation: match the depth of
formal comparison to how novel the situation is to the people
deciding, not to a fixed standard of thoroughness. See
[recognition-primed-decision-making](recognition-primed-decision-making.md)
for the underlying mechanism, and
[multi-lens-comparison-without-false-precision](multi-lens-comparison-without-false-precision.md)
for a middle path that keeps genuine, qualitative comparison
without manufacturing the false precision of weighted scores.

Missing experience is not the only reason formal comparison earns
its cost, though — see
[when-comparative-analysis-is-worth-the-cost](when-comparative-analysis-is-worth-the-cost.md)
for conditions (justifying a choice upward, resolving stakeholder
disagreement, genuine optimization goals) where it is worth running
even for people who could otherwise recognize the right call
directly.
