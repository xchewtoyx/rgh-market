In options analysis, the desired result (R2) is the yardstick,
not a vague aspiration. State it as specifically and
quantitatively as possible so each alternative can be accepted
or rejected by whether it closes the gap from the undesired
result (R1).

Without a concrete R2, stakeholders debate preferences and
histories instead of trade-offs against a shared success
condition. A precise R2 turns a
[problem-definition-framework](problem-definition-framework.md)
into fair options comparison and supports a clear
recommendation rather than leaving the reader to infer one.

Ambiguous R2 often means the real
[problem-stage-question](problem-stage-question.md) is still
"what strategy or objective to pursue," not "which known
option to pick."

When presenting alternatives, judge each candidate against
this defined R2 directly, not against the other candidates'
pros and cons — a pros-and-cons comparison invites the reader
to weigh preferences instead of checking fit to the agreed
success condition. If stakeholders actually want different
outcomes (e.g. stability versus fast growth), that is a sign
R2 itself is contested: organize the options analysis around
the competing R2s so the document surfaces that disagreement
instead of masking it as a choice between tactics.
