---
type: concept
title: Unfalsifiable Bias Framing
description: >
  A post-hoc explanation of a bad outcome as "bias" is worthless
  if the same reasoning would have indicted the opposite outcome
  just as easily — a defensible critique must say what the
  decision-maker should have done differently at the time.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 6"
---

After the USS Vincennes shot down a civilian airliner it had
mistaken for an attacking fighter, an official review attributed
the crew's error to unconscious bias — perceiving what they
expected rather than the actual data. Klein's counter: in the
month before the incident, US forces in the area had issued 150
aircraft challenges, of which 83 percent turned out to be Iranian
military and only 1.3 percent civilian — so acting on the
expectation that a challenged, non-responsive aircraft was hostile
was the empirically well-supported call. Had the crew instead held
fire and been struck by a real attacking fighter, the same critics
would have accused them of the mirror-image failure: ignoring known
base rates. Klein's conclusion: "if you act on expectancies and you
are wrong, you are guilty of expectancy bias. If you ignore
expectancies and are wrong, you are guilty of ignoring base
rates... No one can win" — a bias explanation that can account for
either outcome after the fact explains nothing about what actually
went wrong.

This matters for
[judgment-pre-commitment](judgment-pre-commitment.md),
[decision-quality-independent-of-outcome](decision-quality-independent-of-outcome.md),
and making a decision defensible generally:
a critique of a past decision only has content if it identifies
information that was actually available at decision time and would
have changed the call, or a specific step in the reasoning that a
comparably-placed decision-maker should have caught. "You were
biased" attached only to a bad outcome is not that — it is outcome
knowledge dressed up as process critique. Distinguish this
explicitly from cases where the failure genuinely was in the
process: in the same incident, a display that buried altitude
inside an unlabeled four-digit number with no trend indicator was a
real, identifiable, fixable defect independent of how the incident
turned out — that is a legitimate finding, because it would have
applied whether the crew guessed right or wrong that day.

See [outcome-blind-decision-review](outcome-blind-decision-review.md)
for a concrete practice that prevents this failure directly, by
withholding the outcome from reviewers until after they have judged
the reasoning.
