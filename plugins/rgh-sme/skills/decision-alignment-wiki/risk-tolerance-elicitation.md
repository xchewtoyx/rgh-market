---
type: concept
title: Risk Tolerance Elicitation
description: >
  Get a stakeholder group to state, in advance and in the
  abstract, how much chance of loss they'd accept for a given
  return — a short exercise that resolves disagreements about
  specific proposals faster than debating each one individually.
sources:
  - title: "How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of Intangibles in Business"
    resource: "How to Measure Anything (Douglas W. Hubbard), ch. 11"
---

Groups that disagree sharply about specific risky proposals often
turn out to share similar underlying risk tolerance — the
disagreement is really about how risky each proposal *is*, not
about how much risk is acceptable in general. A short elicitation
exercise separates the two questions: for a representative
investment size, ask at what chance of a negative return a given
return level becomes just barely acceptable, then repeat at a few
other return levels (including the zero-risk case — what's the
minimum acceptable return with no chance of loss at all).
Connecting these points sketches the group's own risk/return
boundary, built entirely from their own stated indifference points
rather than imposed by a fixed policy rule like a flat hurdle rate.

Run with a full stakeholder body rather than one owner, this takes
under two hours and reliably produces two payoffs beyond the curve
itself: a committee that could not agree on project priorities
often converges quickly once forced to state its general risk
appetite explicitly, since much of the apparent disagreement about
specific projects turns out to be disagreement about their
(unstated, differently assumed) risk levels rather than about
risk tolerance itself; and going through the exercise measurably
reduces skepticism toward quantitative risk analysis afterward,
likely because participants feel ownership over a boundary they
built themselves rather than one handed to them.

This is a lightweight companion to
[opportunity-cost-decision-gate](opportunity-cost-decision-gate.md):
that gate decides whether a specific proposal clears the bar
relative to competing uses of the same resources; a risk-tolerance
boundary supplies what the bar actually *is* before any specific
proposal is evaluated against it, so the two exercises don't have
to be re-litigated together every time a new proposal comes up.
