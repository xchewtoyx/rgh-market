---
type: concept
title: Risk Paradox and Label Scoring
description: >
  Verbal risk labels like "high/medium/low" are not actionable and
  create false confidence, and the decisions with the most at
  stake are typically the ones that get the least rigorous risk
  treatment.
sources:
  - title: "How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of Intangibles in Business"
    resource: "How to Measure Anything (Douglas W. Hubbard), ch. 6"
---

Labeling a risk "high," "medium," or "low" (or scoring it 1-5)
feels like risk analysis but does not actually support a decision:
a "medium" risk of losing $5M is not comparable to a "high" risk on
a project ten times smaller, and no one can say whether a "medium"
risk with a 50% return beats a "high" risk with a 15% return —
imagine writing "medium" in the dollar-amount field of an insurance
check to see how unusable the label really is. These schemes also
carry their own hidden distortion: users of label-based scoring
tend to cluster their answers around a few categories, which can
make wildly different risks look identical, and the schemes
typically have no calibration step at all, so the same
overconfidence problem
[decision-checklist-as-bias-gate](decision-checklist-as-bias-gate.md)
and calibrated-estimate techniques exist to catch goes unchecked. A
documented, separate danger: switching from a label-based scheme to
a genuine numeric risk model can measurably change what a decision-
maker believes is possible, precisely because the label version was
answering a vaguer, less demanding question all along — and people
using the vaguer method often *feel* more confident in the
resulting decision without that confidence being any better earned.

**The Risk Paradox**: within a given organization, quantitative
risk analysis is typically applied to routine, lower-stakes,
operational decisions (loan approval, production scheduling) and
is nearly absent from the largest, most consequential decisions
(mergers, major IT investment, big R&D bets) — exactly backward from
where rigor would pay for itself most. The usual justification — that the big decision is "too unique" or
"too elusive" to quantify — does not hold up: a big, one-of-a-kind
decision is exactly the case where being wrong is most expensive,
which is the opposite of a reason to skip a rigorous look.

This reinforces
[when-comparative-analysis-is-worth-the-cost](when-comparative-analysis-is-worth-the-cost.md):
match analysis rigor to what's actually at stake, not to how easy
the topic is to quantify with existing habits — and treat a request
to accept a verbal risk label in place of a number, on a decision
large enough to matter, as a request to decide with less
information than is actually affordable to obtain.
