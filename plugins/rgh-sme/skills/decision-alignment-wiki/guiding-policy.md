A **Guiding Policy** is the second component of [the-strategic-kernel](strategic-kernel.md). It establishes the overall direction and approach for addressing the obstacles identified in the [strategic-diagnosis](strategic-diagnosis.md).

Characteristics of a guiding policy:
- **Directional Clarity**: It defines the boundaries of how the organization will respond (e.g., pivoting to high-margin gourmet goods or becoming the most operationally efficient player).
- **Non-prescriptive**: It does not dictate day-to-day tactical details, leaving room for teams to design specific implementations.
- **Leverage-focused**: It directs resources toward the specific leverage points identified in the diagnosis.

For example, when Wells Fargo anticipated banking deregulation in the 1980s, its guiding policy was to become the most operationally efficient bank in California. This clear policy guided all subsequent decisions without micro-managing individual branch operations.

Once established, a guiding policy must be translated into [coherent-actions](coherent-actions.md) to prevent the strategy from remaining a high-level aspiration.

A **leverage point** — the specific small change a guiding policy directs resources toward — is not an intrinsic feature of the situation that any analyst would spot by inspection; it is relational to the solver's own experience and current state, the way a usable rock-climbing hold depends on the climber's fatigue, grip, and the rock's condition, not just the rock's shape. This is why two equally-informed people reviewing the same diagnosis can disagree about where the real leverage lies: recognizing it draws on a stock of prior cases the guiding policy's author has to draw an analogy from, not on the raw facts of the situation alone (Klein, *Sources of Power*, ch. 8).
