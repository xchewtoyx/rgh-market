---
type: concept
title: Opportunity-Cost Decision Gate
description: >
  Weigh a go/no-go call against what else the same resources
  could fund, not just against the option's own merits, and
  keep "no" a genuinely available outcome.
sources:
  - title: "Agile Software Requirements: Lean Requirements Practices for Teams, Programs, and the Enterprise"
    resource: "Agile Software Requirements (Dean Leffingwell), ch. 21, 23"
---

A large initiative can look entirely sound evaluated on its own
terms — clear value, credible plan, willing sponsors — and still
be the wrong call, because approving it necessarily means not
funding whatever else was competing for the same scarce
resources. A go/no-go decision evaluated in isolation from that
competing set is evaluating the wrong question.

Two disciplines keep the gate honest:

1. **Decision authority sits with whoever holds the broader
   view**, not with whoever sponsors the specific initiative.
   Final go/no-go on a large, cross-cutting initiative belongs
   to a body with visibility across the whole portfolio it draws
   from — the group that can actually see what else is
   competing for the same capacity — rather than the initiative's
   own champion or line owner, who structurally cannot weigh
   that trade-off impartially.
2. **"No" is an explicitly acceptable answer.** Even a
   business case that looks like a clear "go" on its own merits
   must still be weighed against the opportunity cost of
   pursuing it instead of other comparable-maturity candidates —
   a gate that only ever produces "go" once a case reaches this
   stage is not actually gating anything.

This sharpens
[reversibility-as-decision-criterion](reversibility-as-decision-criterion.md)
for initiatives large enough that commitment is expensive to
reverse: the gate exists specifically because analysis depth and
decision authority both need to scale up for exactly this kind
of choice. It also gives
[weighted-shortest-job-first](weighted-shortest-job-first.md) its
practical use here — ranking candidates by cost-of-delay over
effort is what makes the competing set visible and comparable
enough for the gate to actually weigh one against another,
instead of evaluating each candidate as if it existed alone.
