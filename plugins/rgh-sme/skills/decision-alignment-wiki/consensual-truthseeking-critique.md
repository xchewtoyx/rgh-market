---
type: concept
title: Consensual Truthseeking Critique
description: >
  Offering an unsolicited "maybe the problem is you" reframe of
  someone's own decision reads as an attack unless they have
  already opted into that kind of scrutiny.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking in Bets: Making Smarter Decisions When You Don't Have All the Facts"
    resource: "Thinking in Bets (Annie Duke), ch. 4"
---

Suggesting that someone's own choices, not bad luck, drove a bad
outcome can be correct and still land badly — a televised interview
where the host offered exactly this reframe to a guest recounting
her own drama-filled year became a public example of an "insulting"
question, even though the underlying observation was sound. The
failure was not the content of the insight; it was that the guest
had never agreed to a truthseeking exchange in the first place. The
same move, offered to someone who *has* opted in, is simply useful
feedback.

The practical rule: **truthseeking has to be chosen, not imposed.**
Before probing whether someone's own decision (not circumstance)
produced an outcome they're describing, check whether they are
actually asking for that kind of examination right now, or whether
they want something else — sympathy, venting, simply being heard.
Even inside an established truthseeking relationship, a
participant can legitimately opt out in the moment ("I just need to
vent about this one right now") without that being a breach of the
relationship, as long as the group returns to genuine scrutiny once
that moment passes.

This bounds
[fair-treatment-of-objections](fair-treatment-of-objections.md)
and
[walk-stakeholders-through-the-reasoning](walk-stakeholders-through-the-reasoning.md)
in practice: the techniques for testing a recommendation's
reasoning rigorously assume an audience that has agreed, explicitly
or through established norms, to that kind of scrutiny. Applying
the same rigor to someone who has not opted in — a stakeholder
venting about a setback, a colleague simply recounting what
happened — converts a legitimate technique into an unwelcome
ambush, and costs the relationship the technique depends on for its
next, actually-consented use.
