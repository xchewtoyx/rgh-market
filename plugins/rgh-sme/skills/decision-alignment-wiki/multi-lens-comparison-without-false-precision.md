---
type: concept
title: Multi-Lens Comparison Without False Precision
description: >
  Compare options across several qualitative lenses and let
  a team debate trade-offs directly, instead of scoring and
  stack-ranking them into an illusory objective answer.
sources:
  - title: "Continuous Discovery Habits"
    resource: "Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres), ch. 7"
  - title: "How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of Intangibles in Business"
    resource: "How to Measure Anything (Douglas W. Hubbard), ch. 12"
---

When comparing options that are genuinely subjective and
relative (not reducible to one metric), resist the urge to
assign each a numeric score per criterion and stack-rank the
totals. A weighted-scoring formula creates the appearance of
one objectively-right answer out of inputs that were
judgment calls all along — the false precision doesn't
resolve the underlying disagreement, it just hides where the
judgment happened.

Instead, assess each option against a small fixed set of
named lenses relevant to the decision (for example: size of
impact, market/competitive positioning, fit with company
strategy and available political capital, and customer
importance versus current satisfaction), making a relative,
data-informed but frankly subjective call on each lens
without collapsing them into a single number. Expect
disagreement across lenses — one option may win on impact,
another on strategic fit — and treat that as normal input to
a real team debate rather than a defect to be optimized away
by a formula.

This preserves useful doubt: a decision reached by visible
debate across named lenses stays open to revisiting if a lens
turns out to have been wrong, where a single composite score
tends to calcify into unquestioned certainty. This is not just a
theoretical worry: weighted-scoring and pairwise-comparison schemes
built on vague, unscaled attributes ("strategic alignment" scored
1-5) have no published evidence of actually improving decisions
over unaided judgment, and carry their own documented failure modes
— how many scale buckets are offered measurably shifts which bucket
people pick (**partition dependence**), and verbal or numeric labels
get interpreted so inconsistently across evaluators that apparent
agreement is often illusory rather than real. It pairs with
[avoiding-quick-closure](avoiding-quick-closure.md) — both
resist manufacturing certainty faster than the evidence
actually supports it — and complements
[structure-analysis-before-data](structure-analysis-before-data.md):
the lenses give the analysis its structure, but the comparison
itself stays a judgment, not an arithmetic output.

Time-box the comparison itself (an hour or two, at most a day
or two) rather than delaying for more data — this is usually
a [reversibility-as-decision-criterion](reversibility-as-decision-criterion.md)
two-way-door decision, so the cost of an imperfect call is
lower than the cost of the delay.

A narrower situation does warrant a real formula: sequencing
many competing initiatives that draw on one shared capacity,
where a single ranking is genuinely required rather than a
qualitative judgment among a few options. See
[weighted-shortest-job-first](weighted-shortest-job-first.md)
for when a transparent, debated-input formula earns its keep
instead of just hiding judgment behind arithmetic.

A second, different exception: if every option's competing
attributes (quality, speed, cost, error rate) can each be traced
through to how much they actually move one shared underlying goal
— typically revenue or cost — collapsing them into a single figure
is not manufacturing false precision, because the attributes were
never independently valuable to begin with; they were always worth
exactly however much they moved the one thing that matters. The
test for which situation you're in: can you state, with a straight
face, how many dollars one unit of "quality" is worth? If yes, a
single collapsed metric is legitimate. If the honest answer is "it
depends who you ask" or "that's a political judgment," the
attributes are genuinely incommensurable and belong on separate
lenses instead.
