---
type: concept
title: Compounding Probability in Sequential Plans
description: >
  A multi-step plan's overall success rate falls off far faster
  than each individual step's own success rate suggests, so a
  plan built from confident-feeling steps can still be a weak
  bet overall.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 16"
---

A plan with six sequential steps, each individually 90 percent
likely to succeed, is not a 90-percent-likely plan — it is roughly
a 53-percent-likely plan (0.9 raised to the sixth power), because
the whole plan only succeeds if every step does. Each step "feels"
safe in isolation, which is exactly what makes this trap easy to
walk into: a recommendation built by chaining several individually
solid-sounding steps can carry much more real risk than a reader
skimming step-by-step confidence levels would infer, and the more
steps a plan adds, the worse this compounding gets.

The practical implication for a decision document is to state a
multi-step plan's realistic *overall* success likelihood
explicitly, not just each step's, and to weigh that against
shorter plans with fewer dependent steps even when the shorter
plan looks individually less thorough. It also argues for
deliberately not over-specifying contingencies for every
low-probability branch of a long plan — effort spent detailing
what to do if step four of six fails is often better spent
shortening the plan to four steps, since compounding risk
degrades a plan's realistic odds faster than added contingency
detail can compensate for it.

This is a different risk from
[reversibility-as-decision-criterion](reversibility-as-decision-criterion.md):
that concept is about how expensive it is to undo a choice once
made; this one is about how unlikely a whole plan is to reach its
end state at all, independent of how easy any single step would be
to reverse.
