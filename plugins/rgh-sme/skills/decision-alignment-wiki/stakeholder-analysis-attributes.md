type: concept
title: Stakeholder Analysis Attributes
description: >
  Profile each stakeholder against a fixed set of attributes
  and rings of influence so identification does not silently
  miss someone who can affect or block the decision.
sources:
  - title: "Mastering the Requirements Process: Getting Requirements Right"
    resource: "Mastering the Requirements Process (Suzanne Robertson, James Robertson), appendix B"
---

Before relying on memory to name "the stakeholders," profile
every candidate against the same attributes so gaps are
visible rather than assumed away:

- **Role / title** — their position relative to the decision.
- **Vested interest** — what they gain or lose if the
  decision goes one way or another.
- **Degree of influence** — high / medium / low authority
  over the outcome, distinct from vested interest (a highly
  interested party can still have low authority, and vice
  versa).
- **Knowledge / expertise** — what domain knowledge they
  contribute that the decision needs.
- **Availability & commitment** — how much time they can
  actually give to review and discussion, since a nominal
  stakeholder who cannot show up still needs a plan to be
  consulted.
- **Attitude / stance** — champion, neutral, resistant, or
  adversary; a first pass at
  [managing-stakeholder-resistance](managing-stakeholder-resistance.md)
  before any meeting happens.

A **rings-of-influence** map catches categories of
stakeholder that a flat list tends to omit: the core team
doing the work, the client/sponsor funding it, operational
stakeholders who will live with the result day to day, and
external stakeholders (customers, regulators, partners) who
are affected but not in the room. Checking each ring in turn
is a cheaper way to find missing vertices than trying to
recall every affected party from scratch — it feeds directly
into
[multi-party-decision-contracts](multi-party-decision-contracts.md),
where influence and veto power decide who must agree, be
consulted, or merely be informed.

**Never let the approver ring collapse to a single person or
the owning team alone.** A team close to a decision develops
blind spots specifically about who depends on it — the people
best positioned to catch a missed dependency are outside the
team making the call. Deliberately include at least one
reviewer from outside the immediate owning group (a peer team,
a downstream consumer, someone with no stake in the outcome)
in the approver set, not just as an informed party.

A related but distinct miss is failing to notice someone with
no formal approval role at all who still controls a routine
lever the work depends on — see
[informal-blocking-power](informal-blocking-power.md).
