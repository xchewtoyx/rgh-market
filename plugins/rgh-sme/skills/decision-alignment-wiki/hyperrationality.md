type: concept
title: Hyperrationality
description: >
  Demanding a fully analytical, decompose-and-calculate basis
  for every decision degrades judgment rather than improving
  it, because most real decisions can't be decomposed into
  primitive units without arbitrarily distorting the situation.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 15"
  - title: "How to Measure Anything: Finding the Value of Intangibles in Business"
    resource: "How to Measure Anything (Douglas W. Hubbard), ch. 12"
---

Rational analysis — decomposing a problem into calculable units,
stripping out ambiguous context, applying formal logical or
statistical procedures, and making the reasoning explicit enough
for others to check — is a genuine and valuable capability, not
something to work around. **Hyperrationality** is what happens when
it gets used past where it fits: an escalating insistence on
handling *every* decision this way, refusing to act without a
fully rigorous logical or empirical basis, ending in outright
paralysis. The early stage looks indistinguishable from healthy
rigor, which is what makes it easy to slide into without noticing.

Four structural reasons formal analysis cannot be the universal
method, not just practical inconveniences to be engineered around:

- **There is no natural "basic element" to decompose a situation
  into.** Any decomposition scheme reflects the analyst's own goals
  and chosen method — a fire looks like a different set of
  "elements" to a firefighter, an insurance adjuster, and an arson
  investigator, and none of the three decompositions is the
  objectively correct one. Forcing a fixed scheme either narrows the
  problem artificially or distorts the real situation to fit it.
- **Rules are ambiguous in application, not just in content.**
  An if-then rule's hard part is never the rule itself; it is
  judging whether the real, messy, in-context situation actually
  satisfies the "if" — which is exactly why decision-makers convey
  underlying intent rather than trying to pre-specify every
  contingency (see
  [communicating-intent-for-coordinated-action](communicating-intent-for-coordinated-action.md)).
- **Populating the calculation is itself a hard judgment call.**
  Even once the right method is chosen, it needs actual numbers —
  probabilities, weights, utilities — and producing those inputs is
  its own difficult, subjective task that formal method alone does
  not solve.
- **The relevant factors combine faster than they can be
  calculated.** Real settings have too many interacting
  considerations for a full accounting of their implications to
  stay tractable; unaided calculation runs into combinatorial
  explosion well before a natural decision needs to be made.

The practical implication is not "abandon analysis" but "use
experience to frame the problem before analysis is applied, and know
where analysis should hand back to judgment." This sets the
opposite boundary from
[avoiding-quick-closure](avoiding-quick-closure.md): that concept
warns against settling for the first idea before it's been tested;
hyperrationality warns against refusing to settle on anything until
an unreachable standard of rigorous, fully-decomposed justification
is met. Both failures end a decision process badly, from opposite
directions, and a document that demands exhaustive analytical
justification for a call that could be recognized directly is
exhibiting the same failure this concept names — see

Piling on more data, more contributors, or more elaborate process
is not a safe default correction either: confidence tracks the
sheer *volume* of analysis performed far more reliably than it
tracks accuracy, so a longer study, a bigger committee, or a more
sophisticated-looking model can all raise everyone's stated
confidence while leaving the actual decision no better, or even
worse, than before — this is documented across domains as varied as
forecasting, stock-picking, and lie detection, wherever confidence
and accuracy were checked separately rather than assumed to move
together. Treat rising confidence during a review as a symptom
worth checking, not evidence the review itself is working.
