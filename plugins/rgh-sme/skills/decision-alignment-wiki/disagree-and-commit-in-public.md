---
type: concept
title: Disagree and Commit in Public
description: >
  Partners who jointly hold decision authority resolve
  disagreement privately, then visibly support the resulting
  decision in front of the teams who must execute it.
sources:
  - title: "Agile Software Requirements: Lean Requirements Practices for Teams, Programs, and the Enterprise"
    resource: "Agile Software Requirements (Dean Leffingwell), ch. 11, 14"
---

When a decision is jointly owned by two roles rather than one —
for example a market-facing owner and a delivery-facing owner
who must both live with the same call — the trust between them
is what keeps the decision from unravelling the first time they
privately disagree about it. That trust rests on a small set of
concrete behaviors, not goodwill in the abstract: transparency
about intent, honesty about constraints, actually meeting the
commitments made to each other, and admitting when one of them
was wrong rather than defending a bad call to save face.

The behavior that makes the trust visible to everyone else is
narrower and more specific: **when one partner's decision
conflicts with the other's own judgment, support it publicly
anyway.** Raise the disagreement privately, before or after the
public moment, but never contradict or visibly withhold support
from the partner's call in front of the teams — a joint decision
that one owner quietly undermines in public is not actually
joint, and teams that see the split stop trusting either
decision-maker's authority. The same discipline shows up as a
standing rule for the pair to operate under: **never surprise
each other in front of others.** Anything that will be said in a
shared meeting gets said to the partner first, so neither is ever
put in the position of learning their own decision's fate at the
same moment the room does.

Two lighter-weight habits reinforce the same discipline day to
day: routinely ask the partner "how can I help?" rather than
waiting to be asked, and treat "we support each other's
decisions" as a standing rule to operate under, not a one-off
gesture reserved for the moments of genuine disagreement.

This is a narrower claim than
[stakeholder-contracting](stakeholder-contracting.md)'s general
up-front agreement on wants and boundaries: contracting sets the
terms before work starts, while this is the ongoing discipline
that keeps two people with overlapping decision authority from
visibly contradicting each other once decisions start landing.
It also complements
[escalating-unresolved-disagreements](escalating-unresolved-disagreements.md) —
escalation is the structured path when disagreement cannot be
resolved between the two partners at all; disagreeing and
committing in public is what the partners do with each other once
one of them has made the call, escalated or not.
