title: Building a Case for Change
description: >
  A sustained campaign of evidence, education, and allies that earns
  a proposal a hearing with decision-makers, rather than a single
  pitch made once and dropped if it does not land.
sources:
  - title: "Building Secure and Reliable Systems"
    resource: "Building Secure and Reliable Systems (Heather Adkins, Betsy Beyer, Paul Blankinship, Piotr Lewandowski, Ana Oprea, Adam Stubblefield), ch. 21"
---

Before a proposal reaches a formal decision forum, it usually needs
groundwork identifying who actually holds the authority to approve
it (see [stakeholder-analysis-attributes](stakeholder-analysis-attributes.md))
and understanding the pressures — from their own management, board,
or customers — that shape what they will say yes to. Building the
case itself is a sustained effort with several distinct threads,
not a single pitch:

- **Gather data.** Back the proposed change with measured evidence
  (time saved, defects prevented, dashboards tracking progress)
  rather than the intuition that prompted it — decision-makers need
  something they can point to, not just a confident assertion.
- **Educate others.** Problems that are obvious to the people living
  with them daily are often invisible to everyone else; talks,
  postmortems, and information sessions raise the general level of
  understanding before a decision is even asked for.
- **Align incentives.** Connect the proposal to goals its audience
  already cares about — a reliability fix framed only as risk
  reduction is a weaker argument than the same fix framed as also
  protecting revenue.
- **Find allies.** Other people who already see the value add
  weight to the argument, especially if they sit closer to the
  decision-makers, and they double as an informal check on the
  proposal's own assumptions before it is presented.
- **Observe industry trends.** Evidence that other organizations
  have already made the same change, and what happened when they
  did, substitutes for track record the proposer does not yet have.
- **Change the zeitgeist.** For proposals that need broad consensus
  rather than one approval, shifting how people generally think
  about the problem over time can matter more than any single
  meeting.

**Pick your battles.** Constant advocacy for every worthwhile change
creates fatigue and resistance to the next ask, so prioritize the
proposals most likely to succeed now and consciously shelve the
rest. A shelved proposal is not wasted: the data, allies, and
education gathered for it remain available for the moment the
organization is ready to act.

When the case-building above does not produce agreement, see
[escalating-unresolved-disagreements](escalating-unresolved-disagreements.md).
When it does produce agreement, check what kind before treating
the case as won — see
[sponsorship-versus-mandate](sponsorship-versus-mandate.md).

Building the case is only the first step of a longer sequence
for change that requires sustained organizational follow-through
rather than a single approval — see
[kotters-eight-step-change-model](kotters-eight-step-change-model.md)
for how that case-building connects to enlisting participants,
banking early wins, and making the change stick.
