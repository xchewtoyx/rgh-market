---
type: concept
title: Stakeholder Buy-In Sequencing
description: >
  Convince stakeholder groups for a new policy in a
  deliberate order — the groups whose agreement is the real
  linchpin first, groups who mainly need reassurance last —
  rather than pitching everyone simultaneously.
sources:
  - title: "Implementing Service Level Objectives"
    resource: "Implementing Service Level Objectives (Alex Hidalgo), ch. 6"
---

When introducing a new organization-wide policy that changes
how multiple groups work together (a shared decision-making
rule, an error-budget-style automatic gate, a new review
process), the order in which stakeholder groups are convinced
matters as much as the argument made to each.

Two groups whose day-to-day friction the policy is meant to
resolve are the actual linchpin: get their agreement on the
underlying *principles* first, before involving anyone else —
their buy-in is what makes the policy real, and every other
group's confidence depends on knowing these two are already
aligned. Groups whose main question is "are the people who
actually do the work on board?" come next, once that answer is
yes. Groups whose main concern is organizational risk or
compliance come after that — they mostly need reassurance
that the policy does not increase their exposure, not
persuasion that it is a good idea. Groups who are consumers of
the decision rather than approvers of it should be informed,
not asked for approval, once the policy is set — treating them
as a sign-off gate when they were never going to be one just
adds delay without changing the outcome.

Each group also needs the argument translated into what it
already cares about, not one generic pitch repeated verbatim —
the same policy is a velocity argument to one audience, a
leverage/accountability argument to another, and a risk
argument to a third.

The moment that actually tests whether an organization is
serious about a new policy is not the day everyone signs off
on it — it is the first time enforcing it costs something real
and someone with authority asks for an exception. Granting that
first "just this once" exception undermines the whole effort
more than any amount of upfront disagreement did; holding the
line the first time makes every subsequent enforcement
progressively easier, and is worth treating as a distinct,
anticipated event in the rollout rather than an unplanned test
of resolve.

This complements
[multi-party-decision-contracts](multi-party-decision-contracts.md)
(mapping who must agree, be informed, or can veto) by adding
the order those parties should actually be approached in, and
extends
[joint-ownership-decision-budget](joint-ownership-decision-budget.md):
a pre-agreed threshold only stays credible if its first real
test is not the occasion it gets quietly waived.
