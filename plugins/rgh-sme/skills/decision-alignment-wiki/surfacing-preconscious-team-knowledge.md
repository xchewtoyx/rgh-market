---
type: concept
title: Surfacing Preconscious Team Knowledge
description: >
  Information one team member holds correctly but never voices
  to the group is invisible to a decision, and stays that way
  until someone deliberately checks for it rather than assuming
  silence means agreement.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions (Gary Klein), ch. 14"
---

A group making a decision together has a shared, discussed
understanding — what got said out loud — and a separate, larger pool
of things individual members privately know but never mentioned,
because no one asked, it seemed obvious, or raising it felt like
overstepping. The dangerous case is not disagreement that gets
voiced and argued over; it is correct information that a member
holds silently while the group acts on a different, unstated
assumption. In one documented case, a group asked "what is our
current status?" got three different answers from three different
members holding three different pieces of accurate information —
and the person actually about to act on the decision held the least
accurate picture of the three, without anyone, including him,
noticing the gap before it mattered.

This differs from
[escalating-unresolved-disagreements](escalating-unresolved-disagreements.md):
that concept handles a disagreement that has already surfaced and
needs a path to resolution. This one is about information that
never surfaces at all, often precisely because deference norms make
correcting a leader's stated understanding feel like a challenge to
their authority rather than routine information-sharing — the
member who could have corrected a costly misunderstanding stays
silent not because they doubt themselves, but because the social
cost of speaking up outweighs, in the moment, the as-yet-unrealized
cost of staying quiet.

The practical countermeasure is to treat silence as unverified, not
as agreement: when a decision hinges on a specific shared fact
("how many of X do we actually have," "what does this reading
mean"), ask each relevant person to state their own understanding
independently rather than asking the group as a whole and accepting
the first or loudest answer — a question addressed to "everyone"
in a group with deference norms will reliably surface only the most
senior or most confident answer, not the most accurate one. This is
the same failure
[fair-treatment-of-objections](fair-treatment-of-objections.md)
addresses for open objections, applied one step earlier — to
information no one has framed as an objection yet because it hasn't
been asked for.
