---
type: concept
title: Fair Treatment of Objections and Alternatives
description: >
  Address the strongest predictable objections and
  alternatives to a recommendation directly, and respond to
  each proportionately, instead of strawmanning or ignoring
  them.
sources:
  - title: "The Craft of Research, Fifth Edition"
    resource: "The Craft of Research (Booth, Colomb, Williams, Bizup, FitzGerald), ch. 9"
---

A recommendation earns credibility by anticipating the
objections, alternatives, and limitations a thoughtful,
skeptical reader would actually raise — not by staying silent
about them and hoping nobody asks, and not by raising only
weak versions of them for the pleasure of dismissing those
instead ("strawmanning"). Engage the strongest form of a
competing option or objection a reasonable stakeholder could
raise, not the easiest one to knock down. When no one on the team
happens to hold the strongest counterview, see
[dissent-without-confrontation](dissent-without-confrontation.md)
for ways to manufacture it deliberately (a standing devil's-advocate
role, or having someone argue the opposing position directly)
rather than settling for whatever objection occurs naturally.

Once an objection is named, respond to it proportionately
rather than reflexively defending the original recommendation
against everything raised:

- **Concede** the point outright if it is correct — this
  costs less credibility than being caught defending an
  indefensible detail later.
- **Narrow or qualify** the claim so it holds where the
  objection applies and no further.
- **Rebut** with stronger evidence or reasoning where the
  objection is mistaken.
- **Explain** why the objection, though real, does not
  overturn the overall recommendation.

Which response fits is itself part of the analysis, not a
rhetorical afterthought — a recommendation that survives this
treatment is more defensible precisely because its weak points
were tested before a stakeholder found them, not after. When an
objection sounds persuasive but hard to pin down, try
[concrete-story-as-credibility-test](concrete-story-as-credibility-test.md)
before spending real effort rebutting it — many objections that
survive in the abstract dissolve as soon as they're narrated as a
specific sequence of events. This is what
[compare-and-contrast-framing](compare-and-contrast-framing.md)
requires in practice: naming alternatives is not enough if
each is dismissed unfairly, and
[walk-stakeholders-through-the-reasoning](walk-stakeholders-through-the-reasoning.md)
gives the natural point in a presentation to surface this
fair treatment rather than only the survivor option.
