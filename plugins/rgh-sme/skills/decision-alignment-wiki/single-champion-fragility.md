---
type: concept
title: Single-Champion Fragility
description: >
  A decision that depends on one person's continued advocacy
  rather than institutionalized process or governance decays
  once that person leaves, even if it was originally a real
  success.
sources:
  - title: "Designing Connected Content: Plan and Model Digital Products for Today and Tomorrow"
    resource: "Designing Connected Content (Mike Atherton, Carrie Hane), ch. 10"
---

A decision can be genuinely adopted — approved, resourced,
implemented, and working — and still fail to survive, because
its continued existence depended on one internal champion's
ongoing attention rather than on anything institutionalized.
When that person changes roles or leaves, nobody else was
positioned to notice erosion, defend the approach against
reversion, or keep investing in its upkeep, and the decision
quietly decays: a replacement team rebuilds the old way, or a
structure simply stops being maintained and goes stale in
place. Two documented real-world cases follow this exact
pattern — a structured-content project that was fully adopted
by its organization but reverted to the prior ad hoc approach
once its champion moved on, and a similarly successful project
that survived technically but froze in place, unmaintained,
once its champion left.

A contributing failure mode worth naming separately: an
initiative visible only to the small team that built it, with
no communication of its value to the wider organization, is
politically fragile even while it is succeeding — nobody
outside the core team understood what was being attempted or
why it mattered, so nobody outside that team was positioned to
defend or continue it later.

The fix is to treat institutionalization — documented process,
a governance point that outlives any one person's tenure,
visible communication of the decision's value beyond the
immediate team — as part of the decision's follow-through, not
an optional extra once the decision itself is "done." This
sits alongside
[line-ownership-of-implementation](line-ownership-of-implementation.md)
(implementation belongs to the line organization, not a
facilitator) as a related but distinct risk: even a decision
correctly owned by the organization, not by an outside helper,
can still collapse if it depends on one internal advocate
rather than durable structure. It also motivates keeping a
[searchable-decision-archive](searchable-decision-archive.md)
— a record that outlives the person who made the original
case is one concrete way to reduce this fragility.
