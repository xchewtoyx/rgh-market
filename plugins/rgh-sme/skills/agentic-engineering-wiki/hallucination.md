title: Hallucination
description: >
  A model cannot internally distinguish a recalled fact from a plausible
  invention, so confident-sounding fabrication is a structural property of
  generation, not an occasional bug.

An LLM is a [document-completion engine that mimics its training
data](little-red-riding-hood-principle.md): given a prompt, it emits the
statistically most likely continuation, with no internal flag distinguishing
"this is a recalled fact" from "this is a plausible-sounding invention." A
fabricated detail — a URL, a citation, a person's name — is emulated with the
same confidence as a true one, because emulating exactly this kind of
plausible-looking content is what training optimizes for. Directives like
"don't make stuff up" have limited effect precisely because the model has no
internal signal to condition on: from its own perspective, a hallucination and
a correct answer are generated by the identical process.

Two complementary causal accounts for why it happens, from the [sampling and
generation process](temperature-and-sampling.md) itself rather than a
training-data gap alone:

- **Self-delusion** — the model cannot distinguish prompt-supplied data from
  its own already-generated tokens; once it has emitted a claim, later tokens
  condition on that claim as established fact, letting a small early error
  compound into a larger one ("snowballing"). A model that misreads an image
  early on will then confabulate further details consistent with its own
  wrong premise rather than reconsidering it.
- **Mismatched internal knowledge** — instruction-tuning trains a model to
  mimic human-labeler-written responses that may draw on knowledge the
  labelers had but the model itself never learned, teaching it to produce
  confident answers even where its own knowledge is thin. Reinforcement
  learning from human feedback does not reliably fix this: labelers rate
  pairwise preference without seeing *why* one answer is better, giving the
  model no explicit signal to distinguish "I know this" from "I don't."

These accounts are complementary, not competing: self-delusion locates the
cause in the autoregressive generation process itself, mismatched knowledge
locates it in what training taught the model to claim.

When building
[factual-consistency evaluation](factual-consistency-evaluation.md) suites,
concentrate cases on queries the model is most likely to invent answers
for: **niche knowledge** (entities and facts under-represented in training
and retrieval corpora) and **questions about things that do not exist**
(asking what someone said about a topic they never addressed). Those two
patterns produce more hallucinations than well-covered, existentially valid
queries, so an eval drawn only from popular facts will understate risk.

**Mitigation** does not come from asking the model to try harder — it comes
from making claims independently checkable. Prefer completions that carry
verifiable structure: an explanation of reasoning, a calculation, a source
link, or specific keywords that can be searched, over a bare unqualified
assertion. Contrast "there was an English king who married his cousin" (hard
to check) with "...namely George IV, who married Caroline of Brunswick"
(checkable). Shorter responses also fabricate less simply because there are
fewer tokens in which to fabricate. The best available posture: "trust but
verify, minus the trust" — treat every claim as needing independent
confirmation, not as innocent until proven false. [Context
grounding](context-grounding.md) attacks the same problem from the supply
side, by giving the model the facts it needs so it has less occasion to
invent them, and [argument hallucination](argument-hallucination.md) is the
specific case of this failure mode inside tool-call arguments.

Hallucination also compounds [inconsistency](output-consistency-and-robustness.md):
because generation is probabilistic, the same prompt can hallucinate on one
call and not on another, which is part of why hallucination and
inconsistency are usually discussed together as the two faces of a model's
probabilistic output.

Don't assume instruction-tuning or RLHF reliably fixes this: reported
findings are mixed, and at least one widely cited study found RLHF training
made hallucination measurably *worse* relative to supervised fine-tuning
alone, even though human raters still preferred the RLHF model's answers
overall on other dimensions. Fixing the reward signal itself — training a
reward model to explicitly penalize fabrication, not just rank pairwise
preferences without explaining why one answer beats another — is a proposed
but not yet standard fix. The practical stance stays the same regardless of
how a given model was trained: treat every unverified claim as a candidate
fabrication, not as innocent until proven false.
