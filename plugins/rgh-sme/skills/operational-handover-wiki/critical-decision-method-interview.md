Asking an expert to explain their expertise directly rarely works — much of it is perceptual and habitual, not something they've ever had to put into words. A more reliable way to surface it is to anchor the conversation in one concrete, nonroutine incident they actually lived through, and walk back through it in detail.

## The Technique

1. **Ask for a specific challenging incident**, not a general description of the job — a recent case that demanded real experience, ideally one the person would naturally tell as a story. Routine cases tend to produce vague generalities; a genuinely difficult, memorable case draws out the interviewee's best material and, because people enjoy narrating their own expertise, sustains engagement through a long interview.
2. **Let them narrate it freely first**, uninterrupted, to get the overall shape of what happened.
3. **Walk through it a second time to pin down the timeline**, identifying **decision points**: moments where more than one course of action was genuinely available, even if the person only consciously considered one of them.
4. **At each decision point, probe**: was any other course of action considered? If so, how was the choice made between them? If not, what made the chosen action so obviously the right one — what specific cues told you that? This is the step that surfaces the tacit pattern-matching a generic question would miss.
5. **Watch for what a less experienced person would have missed** at the same point — this contrast is often what makes the expert's cue-reading explicit for the first time, even to themselves.

## A More Thorough Version: Four Passes Through One Incident

Finding a genuinely good incident to interview about is itself nontrivial — an emotionally dramatic story (a close call, a death) is often analytically empty if no real difficult judgment was actually made in it. Ask specifically for a nonroutine incident "where a novice might have faltered," then work it in up to four passes:

1. **Brief first pass**: hear a short version purely to judge whether it's worth a deeper interview and to spot which parts merit the most probing.
2. **Full retelling onto a timeline**: pin down the sequence of events in detail, which itself tends to surface inconsistencies worth asking about.
3. **Probe the thought process at each shift**: what was noticed at each point the situation assessment changed; what alternative goals or actions existed and whether they were considered; "what if" probes such as imagining a specific piece of information had never arrived. A sharper variant of this probe: pick an element the story treats as essential, and ask "now imagine that didn't happen — why not? What would explain that?" Both forms aim to surface assumptions the expert is holding without realizing they're holding them.
4. **Novice-contrast pass, time permitting**: at each choice point, ask directly what a less experienced person might have gotten wrong there, and why. This is often the single most reliable way to make an expert's invisible cue-reading explicit.

Generic, decontextualized questions about expertise ("how do you judge an audience?", "how do you choose your best move?") reliably produce vague, unhelpful answers — this is a property of the question, not evidence that the expertise itself is inarticulable. Anchoring every question in one specific incident is what makes the difference.

## Permission Stories

Some incidents, once elicited, teach more than the technical decision they center on — they also show what a given culture actually permits when the stakes are high enough: when it's acceptable to physically push through a colleague's hands to be heard, to escalate over a nominally-higher-ranked person's objection, or to keep questioning a reading that everyone else already believes. Recognize when an elicited story is doing this double duty, and preserve that context when the story is reused for training — the interpersonal-permission lesson is often as valuable to a newcomer as the technical one, and it disappears if the story is trimmed down to its technical content alone.

## Practical Notes

- Use two interviewers where the stakes justify it: one keeps the story moving and maintains rapport, the other takes notes and tracks a prepared checklist of things to probe for if they don't surface on their own.
- Record the session (audio at minimum) — the point at which something matters is often not obvious until later analysis, so err toward capturing more than seems necessary at the time.
- New interviewers need practice before leading a session alone; pattern recognition for *where* expertise tends to hide in an incident is itself a skill that takes time to build, ironically mirroring the very expertise-acquisition process the technique is trying to elicit.
