When trying to get a large or unfamiliar population of operators to reliably follow a practice, the intervention format matters more than how thorough or expert-authored it is. Several approaches for driving this kind of behavior change exist, and they do not perform equally:

- **Comprehensive expert guidelines**: Publishing a thorough, authoritative reference document. This is the intuitive choice — write down everything an expert knows — but it is consistently ineffective at changing what people actually do day to day. A comprehensive document sitting on a shelf (or in a wiki) does not, by itself, change bedside or on-call behavior; length and completeness do not translate into compliance.
- **Training programs**: Continuous practitioner education. Effective in principle, but prohibitively expensive to deliver and impossible to scale to every operator or site that needs it.
- **Incentive schemes**: Financial or performance incentives tied to following a practice. Produces only modest gains, and is vulnerable to gaming and reporting bias — operators optimize for the measured proxy rather than the underlying behavior.
- **Leverage interventions**: A simple, measurable, easily transmissible behavior vehicle targeted at specific trigger points. This is consistently the most effective format — not because it contains more expertise than the comprehensive guideline, but because its simplicity and specificity make it actually usable in the moment the behavior needs to happen.

## Why Leverage Interventions Win

A leverage intervention pairs a simple artifact with explicit instructions for exactly when to use it — not general advice, but specific, concrete trigger points (e.g. "at these particular moments, do this particular thing"). This is precisely what a well-designed checklist is: it doesn't teach the underlying expertise, it standardizes the timing and execution of specific, already-known-to-be-important actions. The intervention doesn't need to be sophisticated to work — it needs to be simple enough to actually get used at the trigger point, every time.

## Applying This to Operational Documentation

When choosing how to document an operational practice for an unfamiliar operator, resist the instinct to write the most complete reference possible. A comprehensive design document or runbook has value as background material, but it is not what makes an operator reliably do the right thing under pressure — see [Runbook and Checklist Design](runbook-checklist-design.md) for the specific ergonomics (item count, time budget) that keep a leverage-style checklist usable. Reserve comprehensive material for context an operator consults occasionally, and keep the artifact meant to drive in-the-moment behavior as short and trigger-specific as possible.
