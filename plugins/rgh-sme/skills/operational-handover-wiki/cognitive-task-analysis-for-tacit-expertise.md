An organization that would never let a retiring employee walk out the door with a company laptop routinely lets the same employee walk out with decades of expertise worth far more — because that expertise was never written down anywhere, and often couldn't have been simply by asking "what do you know?" Most of what makes an experienced operator effective isn't extra facts or rules; it's learned perception — recognizing a situation as an instance of a familiar pattern, noticing when an expected event fails to occur, judging when a reading from an instrument should be distrusted. This kind of knowledge resists being captured by traditional task analysis, which documents procedures but has little vocabulary for perception, judgment, or decision skill.

**Cognitive task analysis (CTA)** is a five-step discipline for finding, extracting, and reusing this specific kind of tacit expertise:

1. **Locate** the sources of expertise. Expertise is usually distributed unevenly across several people with different specialties, not concentrated in one canonical "expert" — the search step itself typically requires acquiring enough background domain knowledge to know who to ask.
2. **Evaluate** whether the expertise is worth extracting. Not everything a skilled person knows is high-value or affordable to elicit — weigh the likely payoff (how often the knowledge will be reused, how costly its loss would be) against the real cost of interviews, transcription, analysis, and building the resulting training or reference material.
3. **Extract** it via structured knowledge elicitation — most reliably a [Critical-Decision-Method Interview](critical-decision-method-interview.md) built around a specific challenging incident rather than general questions about what someone knows, but also simulated think-aloud tasks or concept-elicitation interviews.
4. **Codify** it into a form other people can actually use: diagrams, critical-cue checklists, annotated real incident stories, transcripts, or short models of the decision process — not necessarily formal prose documentation.
5. **Apply** it — to training curricula, interface or system design, hiring/selection criteria, or a durable internal reference, reusing the same codified material across more than one of these purposes where possible.

## Why This Differs from Ordinary Documentation

Traditional handover documentation and procedure manuals capture the *rule*, but the hard part of following a rule is perceptually recognizing when its trigger condition has actually occurred — "brown on top," "the roof feels spongy," "the console is lying to you this time" are all judgment calls that a written threshold can gesture at but not fully specify. CTA targets exactly this gap: it treats the goal as finding out how an expert *sees* a situation, not just what they'd tell you to do once they've seen it.

## Why Perceptual Training Is Usually the Skipped Half

Studies of informal on-the-job training consistently find that experienced staff teaching newcomers spend almost all their effort on procedures and equipment, and only "spottily" on the perceptual side of the job — not because they think it doesn't matter, but because they were never trained to articulate it themselves and default to what's easy to demonstrate. Left unaddressed, this gap shows up later as new hires who can follow the runbook but can't tell when the runbook doesn't apply, and it is a plausible contributor to turnover in roles where people feel persistently underprepared despite having completed onboarding.

## Relationship to Other Handover Techniques

CTA is a formal, front-loaded elicitation effort aimed at producing reusable material (training courses, cue checklists, reference diagrams) — distinct from [Live Knowledge Transfer Techniques](live-knowledge-transfer-techniques.md), which transfer tacit knowledge through real-time collaboration during an actual handover rather than through a dedicated interview-and-codify project. The two are complementary: CTA is worth the investment when the same expertise will be reused across many future hires or a wide rollout (e.g., a training course for an entire technical staff), while live techniques suit a one-off transfer between a specific outgoing and incoming owner. CTA-derived cue checklists also make good input to [On-Call Onboarding and Training](on-call-onboarding-and-training.md) curricula, particularly for the triage and debugging judgment calls that a plain procedural checklist can't teach on its own.
