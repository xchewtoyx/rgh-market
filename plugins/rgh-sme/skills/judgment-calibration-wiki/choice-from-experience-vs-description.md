---
type: concept
title: Choice from Experience vs Description
description: >
  Rare events are overweighted when prospects are described verbally but often
  underweighted when learned only through repeated sampling without the rare draw.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 30"
---

[Prospect theory decision weights](prospect-theory-decision-weights.md) and the
[possibility effect](possibility-and-certainty-effects.md) analyze **choice from
description** — verbal or numeric statements of a prospect ("5% to win $12").

A separate research line studies **choice from experience**: participants
repeatedly press one of two buttons, each yielding a random reward per an
underlying (undisclosed) probability distribution, and learn the prospects only
by sampling outcomes over many trials before a critical paid choice. The two
buttons have approximately equal expected value but differ in variability (e.g.,
one gives $10 on 5% of trials, the other $1 on 50%). Because sampling is genuinely
random, a participant's experienced sample need not match the true statistical
setup.

**Key finding: choice from description produces the possibility effect** (rare
outcomes overweighted relative to probability, as prospect theory predicts), **but
choice from experience never shows overweighting of rare events — underweighting
is common instead.**

This maps onto everyday variable outcomes from a stable source: a usually-good
restaurant occasionally serves a brilliant or awful meal; a usually-pleasant
friend is sometimes moody; California has rare earthquakes. Rare events are
typically *not* overweighted in decisions like choosing a restaurant or
retrofitting against earthquake damage.

The interpretation isn't fully settled, but one major driver of underweighting in
both experiments and the real world is agreed on: **many participants never
personally experience the rare event.** Most Californians have never lived through
a major earthquake; before 2008, no banker had personally experienced a devastating
financial crisis. Ralph Hertwig and Ido Erev: chances of rare events (such as the
burst of housing bubbles) receive less impact than they deserve according to
objective probabilities — one explanation for tepid public response to long-term
environmental threats.

Contrast [denominator neglect](denominator-neglect.md) and [frequency format
framing](frequency-format-framing.md), which amplify rare-event weight when
description makes numerators vivid.
