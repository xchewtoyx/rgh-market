---
type: concept
title: Endowment Effect
description: >
  Owning a good for use raises its subjective value relative to not owning it,
  because giving up feels like a loss weighted more than acquiring feels like a gain.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 27"
---

Richard Thaler's **endowment effect**: Professor Rosett would not sell a wine bottle
from his collection at $100 yet would never pay more than $35 for the same quality
— at prices between $35 and $100 he would neither buy nor sell. Standard theory
predicts a single value; owning the good increased its value. Found especially in
goods not regularly traded. Concert ticket: buying price $500, selling price > $3,000
once owned.

[Prospect theory](prospect-theory-decision-weights.md) explains it via [loss aversion
and reference points](loss-aversion-and-reference-points.md): willingness to
buy/sell depends on reference point (own it or not). Owning, you weigh pain of
giving up; not owning, pleasure of getting. Value function steeper for losses than
gains.

**Not universal — "for use" vs. "for exchange."** No effect trading $5 for five
singles or routine shoe shopping — merchant holds shoes as proxy for money. Goods
held "for exchange" don't trigger it; goods held "for use" (wine, tickets, leisure,
living standard) do.

## Mug experiments (Kahneman, Knetsch, Thaler)

Tokens (exchange goods) traded per standard theory. $6 coffee mug ("for use"), randomly
given to half as Sellers, rest as Buyers: average selling price ≈ double buying price;
trades under half predicted — market efficiency broke down.

Third group **Choosers** pick mug or equivalent cash (objectively same as Sellers'
choice): **Sellers $7.12, Choosers $3.12, Buyers $2.87.** Sellers/Choosers gap reflects
"emotion of the moment" — reluctance to give up possession ([System 1 and System 2](system-1-and-system-2.md)
automatic evaluations). Sellers/Choosers-to-Buyers ratio (~2:1) matches loss-aversion
coefficient in risky choice — same gain/loss function for riskless and risky decisions.
Similar ~2:1 asymmetry in household responses to staple price changes.

Knetsch pen/chocolate: ~10% swapped when given costless alternative after sitting
with initial gift through questionnaire.

Boston condo study: during downturn, owners with higher purchase-price reference
set higher asking prices, sold slower, received more — though purchase price is
[sunk history](sunk-cost-fallacy.md) for a rational agent.

**Boundary conditions:** effect eliminated when owners see goods as carriers of
value for future exchange. John List's baseball-card studies: novice traders reluctant
to part with owned cards; reluctance disappeared with experience (18% vs 48% swap
rates). Effect appears only with physical possession before trading announced.
Veteran traders ask "How much do I want that mug?" rather than treating possession
as decisive.
