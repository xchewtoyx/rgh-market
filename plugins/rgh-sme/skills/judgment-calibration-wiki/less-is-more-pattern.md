---
type: concept
title: Less Is More Pattern
description: >
  Adding detail or items can increase perceived probability or value in single
  evaluation because System 1 averages instead of adding.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 15"
---

Christopher Hsee's dinnerware: Set A (40 pieces, 7 broken cups/saucers) vs Set B
(24 intact pieces). Set A strictly dominates. In **joint evaluation**, A priced
higher ($32 vs $30). In **single evaluation**, B priced much higher ($33 vs $23)
— the average value of A's dishes is dragged down by broken items.

**Less is more**: removing 16 items (7 intact) from A raised perceived value.
John List replicated in baseball cards: ten high-value cards valued more than
ten plus three modest cards in single evaluation; joint evaluation reversed.

Same structure as [conjunction fallacy](conjunction-fallacy.md): probability is
sum-like — P(teller) = P(feminist teller) + P(non-feminist teller). Single
evaluation lets System 1 average instead of add.

Wimbledon: P(Borg loses first set) ≥ P(Borg loses first set but wins match) by
logic, yet 72% ranked the recovery story as less probable — coherence beats
logic in direct comparison.

Joint evaluation eliminates error in dinnerware but not reliably in Linda —
intuition often overcomes logic even when items are compared side by side.

See [single versus joint evaluation](single-vs-joint-evaluation.md) for preference
reversals, evaluability (Hsee dictionaries), and mock-jury anchoring patterns
sharing this structure.
