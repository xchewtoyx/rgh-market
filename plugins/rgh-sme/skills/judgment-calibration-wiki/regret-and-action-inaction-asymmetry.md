---
type: concept
title: Regret and Action-Inaction Asymmetry
description: >
  Identical outcomes trigger stronger regret and blame when produced by action
  or deviation from default than when produced by inaction or staying with default.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 32"
---

**Regret** is both an emotion and "a punishment we administer to ourselves." The
fear of regret factors into many decisions. Intense regret occurs when it is
easiest to imagine having done something other than what you did — a **counterfactual
emotion** triggered by available alternatives to reality. After plane crashes,
stories circulate about passengers who "should not" have been on the plane (last-minute
seat, transferred airline) — unusual events are easier to undo in imagination than
normal ones.

## Regret vs blame: different norms

**Hitchhiker robbery**: Mr. Brown almost never picks up hitchhikers but did and
was robbed; Mr. Smith frequently does and was robbed. Who feels greater regret?
88% said Brown, 12% Smith. Who is blamed more? 23% Brown, 77% Smith. Regret compares
to what the **individual** usually does (abnormal for Brown); blame compares to a
**conventional** norm (Smith habitually takes unreasonable risks).

## Action vs inaction is really default vs deviation

Paul considered switching stocks but didn't; George switched — both now hold A and
would be equally better off with B. Who feels greater regret? 8% Paul, 92% George
despite objectively identical positions. People expect stronger emotional reactions
to outcomes produced by **action** than identical outcomes from **inaction** — verified
for gambling and losses; applies to blame too.

The operative distinction is not commission vs. omission per se but **default options
vs. deviations from default**. Default for a stock you own: don't sell. Default when
greeting a colleague: greet him. Selling and failing to greet are both departures.

**Blackjack experiment**: asked "Do you wish to hit?" or "Do you wish to stand?" —
regardless of question, answering "yes" produced much more regret after bad outcomes,
because the question suggested a default of "I don't have a strong wish to do it."

**Coach example**: after a bad loss, action (personnel/strategy change) is the expected
default — failure to act produces blame and regret.

## Consequences for judgment and choice

Regret-risk favors conventional, risk-averse choices. Consumers reminded of possible
regret prefer brand names over generics. Fund managers clean unconventional stocks
from portfolios near year-end. Medical decisions: choosing an unconventional treatment
that may improve odds carries substantial regret/blame/litigation risk because "in
hindsight, it will be easier to imagine the normal choice." Success from unconventional
choice helps reputation less than failure hurts it.
