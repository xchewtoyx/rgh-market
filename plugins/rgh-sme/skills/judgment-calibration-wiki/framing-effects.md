Framing effects: "the unjustified influences of formulation on beliefs and
preferences." Logically equivalent statements evoke different [System 1](system-1-and-system-2.md)
associations — Humans are not as reliably rational as Econs whose preferences
attach only to states of the world.

**Gamble vs. lottery ticket**: 10% win $95 / 90% lose $5 vs. pay $5 for 10% win
$100 — far more accept the lottery frame. "Losses evoke stronger negative feelings
than costs" (Thaler: costs are not losses). Credit-card industry fought to label
differentials "cash discount" not "credit surcharge."

**KEEP vs. LOSE** (identical £20 sure outcome): amygdala active when conforming
to frame; anterior cingulate when resisting. Most "rational" subjects learned to
translate LOSE into KEEP equivalents.

**Survival vs. mortality** (Tversky): "90% one-month survival" vs. "10% mortality"
— 84% choose surgery under survival frame, 50% under mortality frame; medical
training is no defense. Single-exposure real choices offer no trial learning;
reframing is effortful and System 2 is lazy.

**Asian disease problem**: "200 saved" vs. "400 die" — identical outcomes, opposite
choices (risk-averse for gains, risk-seeking for losses per prospect theory).
Public-health professionals equally susceptible; confronted with inconsistency,
"embarrassed silence" — System 2 has no independent moral intuitions.

**Schelling tax paradox**: child exemption as reduction vs. childless surcharge —
System 1 rule "favor the poor" yields contradictory verdicts from reference-point
shift. Framing is not masking a deeper preference — "there is no underlying
preference masked by the frame."

For calibration: separate evidence from wording. [Sherman Kent probability language](sherman-kent-probability-language.md)
and explicit numerics reduce ambiguous framing enabling
[wrong-side-of-maybe](wrong-side-of-maybe-fallacy.md) reinterpretation.

## Good frames vs. bad frames

Not all frames are equal — some are demonstrably better guides to rational
decisions. **Sunk-cost framing**: woman loses two $80 theater tickets vs. loses
$160 cash before buying — most believe ticket-loser won't rebuy (doubling show cost
via [mental accounting](mental-accounting.md) tied to "theater" account) while
cash-loser will buy (charged to "general revenue," small wealth reduction). Cash-loss
frame is superior: loss is sunk either way; "history is irrelevant and the only
issue that matters is the set of options now and their likely consequences." Heuristic
for ticket-loser: "Would you have bought tickets if you had lost the equivalent
amount of cash?" General principle: **broader frames and inclusive accounts
generally lead to more rational decisions.** See [MPG illusion](mpg-illusion.md)
for another superior reframing.

**Single vs joint evaluation**: logically equivalent scenarios can yield opposite
judgments when viewed alone vs. compared side by side — see
[single versus joint evaluation](single-vs-joint-evaluation.md). Rationality is
generally better served by broader comparative frames, but context manipulators
exploit joint presentation.

## Default framing and organ donation

Organ donation rates in neighboring European countries differ startlingly (Austria
~100% vs. Germany ~12%; Sweden ~86% vs. Denmark ~4%) — best predicted by **default
option**. High-donation countries use opt-out (check box to *not* donate); low-donation
countries use opt-in. Unlike many framing effects traced purely to System 1 associations,
Kahneman explains this via [lazy System 2](lazy-system-2-and-rationality.md): people
check the box if they've decided; if unprepared, checking requires effort, so most
accept the default. A form requiring solving 13×37 to check the box would further
sway rates.

When formulation this strongly controls outcomes, policy choice is not neutral —
despite being driven by "an utterly inconsequential feature of the situation." This
dependence is embarrassing to how we experience our own minds but undeniable evidence
against rational-agent theory: a worthy theory asserts certain events are impossible;
when "impossible" events occur, the theory is falsified — yet theories survive long
after conclusive contrary evidence. Believers in rational agents take for granted that
formulation cannot determine preferences on significant problems and don't investigate;
skeptics trained to notice inconsequential determinants are not surprised.
