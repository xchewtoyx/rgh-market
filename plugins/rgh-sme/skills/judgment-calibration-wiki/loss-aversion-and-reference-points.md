Prospect theory replaces Bernoulli's utility of **wealth states** with value of
**gains and losses** relative to a **reference point** (usually status quo,
sometimes expectation or entitlement). Anthony thinks in gains; Betty in losses —
same final wealth, opposite risk attitudes.

**Theory-induced blindness**: Bernoulli's model survived 250 years despite
inability to represent loss aversion — anomalies explained away once a theory is
accepted.

Problems 3–4: identical final wealth lotteries, different preferences because
reference points differ ($1,000 gift vs. $2,000 gift) — decisive counterexample.

Three System 1 principles:

1. Evaluation relative to reference point (adaptation level — three-bowls water demo).
2. **Diminishing sensitivity** to both gains and losses.
3. **Loss aversion**: losses loom larger than gains (evolutionary threat priority).

Value function: S-shaped, steeper on loss side. Mixed gamble (50% lose $100 / win
$150): most reject despite positive EV — fear of $100 loss exceeds hope of $150
gain. Typical loss-aversion ratio **1.5–2.5** (many say $200 balances $100 loss).
Traders and "think like a trader" instructions show reduced loss aversion.

Reconciling risk aversion (gains) and risk seeking (bad options): in mixed gambles,
loss aversion dominates; in all-loss choices, diminishing sensitivity drives
gambling to avoid sure loss.

Matthew **Rabin's theorem**: explaining small-stake loss aversion via utility of
wealth is mathematically absurd — rejecting small favorable gambles commits you
to rejecting large favorable ones.

**Albert and Ben twins**: identical tastes, identical starting point (low income,
low leisure). Coin flip: Albert gets +$10,000 raise (A), Ben gets +12 vacation days
(B) — indifferent ex ante. Once settled, each prefers staying put: Albert frames
switching as "$10,000 salary cut for 12 vacation days" (loss); Ben frames switching
as losing vacation days for salary gain. **Tastes vary with reference point**;
disadvantages of change loom larger than advantages → **status-quo bias** and bias
toward small over large changes. Standard indifference-curve maps omit current
position — implicitly assume only present circumstances matter, past irrelevant,
new job evaluation independent of current terms ("Bernoulli's error" again).

Unemployed workers' acceptable new wages average ~**90%** of previous wage, dropping
<10% over a year — loss aversion in bargaining where both sides know the reference
contract.

## Status quo defense and fairness norms

Loss aversion "shows up almost everywhere" in renegotiations: existing terms set the
reference point; concessions are your losses and my gains, valued asymmetrically —
shrinking-pie negotiations harder than expanding-pie. Negotiators signal attachment
to bargaining chips to extract reciprocal painful concessions. Territory defenders
in the animal world almost always win challenges within seconds — same principle for
institutional reforms: potential losers (more active, determined) resist disproportionately,
yielding expensive, diluted outcomes; grandfather clauses protect incumbents. Loss
aversion is a **powerful conservative force** — "gravitational force" holding life
near the reference point.

**Fairness questionnaires** (Thaler, Knetsch, Kahneman): raising snow-shovel price
$15→$20 after blizzard — 82% Unfair (pre-blizzard price as reference). Cutting
incumbent employee $9→$7 when market is $7 — 83% Unfair; hiring replacement at $7 —
73% Acceptable. Entitlement is **personal** to incumbents. Firms may pass losses to
protect profit entitlements but are judged harshly for exploiting power to increase
profit at others' expense. Sharing windfall gains is favored but not required. Unfair
pricing reduces future sales (~15% purchase cut, $90/customer average loss when catalog
showed recent price drop).

For calibration: separate descriptive loss aversion in judgments from normative
fairness expectations in stakeholder reactions.
