---
type: concept
title: Representativeness Heuristic
description: >
  Judging probability or likelihood by how much an instance resembles a
  stereotype, often substituting similarity for statistical reasoning.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 14"
---

The representativeness heuristic answers
"how probable is X?" by asking "how similar is X to my stereotype of the
category?" — a [substitution](substitution-heuristic.md) of an easy
similarity judgment for a harder probability question.

In the Tom W puzzle, psychology graduate students who knew base rates and
were told the personality sketch was of uncertain validity nonetheless ranked
fields by *likelihood* identically to rankings by *similarity*. Even
statisticians like Robyn Dawes initially guessed "computer scientist?" before
catching themselves.

Representativeness often beats chance: friendly-acting people usually are
friendly; tall thin athletes more often play basketball than football. But
exclusive reliance commits two statistical sins:

1. **Excessive willingness to predict low-base-rate events.** A stranger
   reading the *New York Times* on the subway is more likely to have no college
   degree than a PhD, because nongraduates vastly outnumber PhDs among riders —
   even though PhD "fits" the stereotype better. Same logic for a shy poetry
   lover being more likely a business student than a Chinese-literature
   student.

2. **Insensitivity to evidence quality**, a consequence of
   [WYSIATI](wysiati.md). Unless you immediately reject doubtful evidence,
   System 1 processes available information as if it were true. When evidence
   quality is doubtful, probability judgments should stay close to the
   [base rate](base-rate-neglect.md), nudging well-populated fields slightly
   down and rare fields slightly up — not swinging fully toward the stereotype.

Michael Lewis's *Moneyball* illustrates the cost: baseball scouts forecast
partly from build and look until Billy Beane picked undervalued players by
past-performance statistics.
