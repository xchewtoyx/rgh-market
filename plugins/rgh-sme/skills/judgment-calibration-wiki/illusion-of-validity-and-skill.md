The illusion of skill and illusion of validity: experts (and [hedgehogs](hedgehogs-vs-foxes.md)
with all-explaining theories) feel confident and coherent even when their world is
not predictable.

Tetlock's twenty-year study (284 pundits, 80,000+ predictions): experts performed
**worse than assigning equal probability** to three outcomes — "poorer predictions
than dart-throwing monkeys." Even within specialty, barely better than nonspecialists;
most knowledgeable often *less* reliable. The most overconfident experts were most
likely to be invited onto news shows. Excuses when wrong: timing, unforeseeable
event, or "wrong for the right reasons."

## CFO survey and socially enforced overconfidence

Duke University CFO survey: chief financial officers forecast S&P 500 returns
annually; across 11,600 forecasts, the correlation between predicted and actual
returns was slightly **below zero** — when CFOs predicted a market decline, a rise
was slightly more likely. Worse, they showed no awareness their forecasts were
worthless: alongside best-guess estimates they gave 90%-confidence bounds forming
an "80% confidence interval," which should produce ~20% "surprises" (outcomes
outside the interval); actual surprise rate was **67%**, over 3× the expected rate.
To achieve a genuine 20% surprise rate given their actual knowledge, CFOs should
have stated an 80% interval of roughly -10% to +30% for annual S&P returns — "more
than 4 times wider than the intervals they actually stated." A CFO stating the
honest, wide interval "can expect to be laughed out of the room" — admitting the
true extent of ignorance is professionally unacceptable. President Truman's wish
for a "one-armed economist" (tired of "on the other hand...") captures the social
pressure. The CFOs most confident about the S&P were also overconfident about their
own firm's prospects, and their firms took on more risk.

Kahneman and Lovallo coined **"bold forecasts and timid decisions"**: risk-takers
in the economic domain don't have an unusual taste for high-stakes gambles — "they
are merely less aware of risks than more timid people are." Nassim Taleb: inadequate
appreciation of environmental uncertainty pushes economic agents into risks they
should avoid — yet markets reward confident (even misleadingly so) providers of
information over honest truth-tellers.

In medicine, ICU autopsies vs antemortem diagnoses: "clinicians who were 'completely
certain' of the diagnosis antemortem were wrong 40% of the time." Rationale:
"it is considered a weakness and a sign of vulnerability for clinicians to appear
unsure. Confidence is valued over uncertainty." Experts who honestly disclose
ignorance risk being replaced by more confident competitors who better win client
trust.

Kahneman's summary: "An unbiased appreciation of uncertainty is a cornerstone of
rationality—but it is not what people and organizations want," since extreme
uncertainty is paralyzing when stakes are high and "acting on pretended knowledge
is often the preferred solution." Overconfidence is a [WYSIATI](wysiati.md)
manifestation: estimates rely only on information that comes to mind, forming a
coherent story; information one never knew cannot be allowed for.

"The idea that the future is unpredictable is undermined every day by the ease
with which the past is explained." Luck in large historical events (e.g., 50-50 sex
of Hitler/Stalin/Mao embryos) makes long-term prediction a joke — yet the illusion
persists and pundits exploit it nightly.

Two lessons: prediction errors are inevitable because the world is unpredictable
(not the experts' fault alone); **high subjective confidence is not a trustworthy
indicator of accuracy** — low confidence may be more informative.

Behavior on a test poorly predicts behavior in the real world because each is
determined by many situation-specific factors — but a test with validity .20 or
.30 should still be used; just don't expect more. The same skepticism applies
to stock pickers beating the market and pundits making long-term forecasts.

Subjective confidence — rooted in cognitive ease and narrative coherence, not
accuracy — is generally overrated. The associative machine (following
[WYSIATI](wysiati.md)) suppresses doubt and evokes confirming material. Many
pseudo-experts don't know they don't know.

In [low-validity environments](wicked-and-low-validity-environments.md),
[Meehl's evidence](meehl-clinical-vs-statistical-prediction.md) shows
algorithms beat clinicians who aren't inept — the world itself limits
predictability. [Two conditions for trustworthy expert intuition](two-conditions-for-trustworthy-expert-intuition.md)
clarify when confidence might track skill.

The line separating the possibly predictable near future from the unpredictable
distant future is yet to be drawn.
