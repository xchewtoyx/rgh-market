Kahneman and Tversky coined **planning fallacy** for forecasts that (1) sit
unrealistically close to best-case scenarios and (2) would improve by consulting
similar-case statistics.

Examples: Scottish Parliament building (£40M estimate → ~£431M actual); worldwide
rail projects (ridership overestimated in >90% of cases, average 106% error,
45% cost overruns, no improvement over 30 years); kitchen remodels ($18,658
expected vs $38,769 actual).

Optimism isn't the only driver: contractors profit on additions; some planners
know overruns rarely kill projects and seek approval anyway. Decision-makers who
approve without demanding an [outside view](inside-view-vs-outside-view.md)
share responsibility.

Kahneman was personally cured of the planning fallacy when Seymour Fox cited
base rates — but the team exhibited **irrational perseverance**, continuing
after a rational basis to quit. Once the outside-view forecast revealed the
curriculum project's true expected duration (7–10 years, high failure risk), the
team neither confronted it nor quit — tacitly agreeing to continue without an
explicit forecast, partly because quitting after heavy [sunk investment](sunk-cost-fallacy.md)
would have been embarrassing. Kahneman calls this **lethargy** — unwillingness to
think about what had happened — particularly troubling for a team dedicated to
teaching rationality. He has since acquired a habit of seeking the outside view,
"but it will never be the natural thing to do."

Sample dialogue phrases for flagging these biases: "He's taking an inside view,"
"She is the victim of a planning fallacy... there are too many different ways for
the plan to fail," "What would be your baseline prediction?", "We are making an
additional investment because we do not want to admit failure... an instance of the
sunk-cost fallacy."

With Dan Lovallo, Kahneman later argued [optimistic bias](optimistic-bias.md) drives
risk-taking: executives decide on delusional optimism rather than rational
weighting of gains, losses, and probabilities — overestimating benefits,
underestimating costs, overlooking mistakes. This explains litigation, wars, and
small-business formation.

Mitigation: [reference class forecasting](reference-class-forecasting.md), [premortem technique](premortem-technique.md).
For software and product work, see [product build outcome base rates](product-build-outcome-base-rates.md)
and [process-skill decoupling](process-skill-decoupling.md) — discovery outputs are
guesses until ship-and-observe, not plans calibrated to likely success.
