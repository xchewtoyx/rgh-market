---
type: concept
title: Responsibility Aversion Beyond Loss Aversion
description: >
  People demand far more to accept a risk they actively choose than to pay to
  prevent the same risk — responsibility for bad outcomes amplifies loss aversion.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 32"
---

[Loss aversion](loss-aversion-and-reference-points.md) weights losses roughly **twice**
as much as gains in money contexts; the coefficient rises for domains more important
than money, like health — and reluctance to "sell" important endowments rises sharply
when doing so could make you **responsible** for an awful outcome.

**Thaler's vaccine/volunteer pair** (modified): (1) 1/1,000 chance of fatal disease;
a pre-symptom vaccine exists — max you'd pay? Most name a limited amount. (2) Volunteer
to accept 1/1,000 chance of the same disease — minimum fee demanded? The demanded
fee is far higher than willingness to pay — typical ratio around **50:1**. Two reasons:
selling health isn't considered a legitimate transaction, and — perhaps most important —
you bear responsibility for a bad outcome you **actively chose** versus one you merely
failed to prevent by inaction (staying with the default of doing nothing).

**Insecticide/child-safety**: baseline risk 15/10,000 bottles; cheaper product raises
to 16/10,000 — more than two-thirds of parents would not switch at any discount;
minority willing demanded discounts far larger than they'd pay for a far bigger safety
improvement. Attitude "incoherent and potentially damaging": parents have finite safety
budgets ([mental accounting](mental-accounting.md)); money saved accepting a minute
risk increase could fund larger gains elsewhere (safer car seat, socket covers). Resistance
may be driven more by selfish fear of [regret/shame](regret-and-action-inaction-asymmetry.md)
than optimizing the child's actual safety.

**Precautionary principle**: aversion to trading increased risk for other advantages
scales to law and regulation — especially in Europe, prohibiting any action that might
cause harm, placing burden of proving safety on risk-takers; absence of evidence of
harm is not sufficient justification. Cass Sunstein: costly and, interpreted strictly,
paralyzing.
