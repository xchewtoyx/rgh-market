  How elite forecast teams combine psychological safety, active open-mindedness,
  and generous information sharing to beat solo work without groupthink.

In the Good Judgment Project, teams averaged **23%** more accurate than solo
forecasters in year 1; when top performers were grouped into **superteams**
(~12 members, remote-only, with high-performance-team coaching), those who had
already been superforecasters became roughly **50%** more accurate — a result
replicated in year 3.

Remote collaboration trades off warmth against friction management: never meeting
face-to-face makes it easier to disregard people (and internet debate can turn
poisonous), but distance can also preserve critical distance in disputes. A real
risk is **CEO disease** — labeling people "superforecasters" and surrounding them
with peers of similar stature can inflate egos so judgments feel self-evidently
right.

Effective teams avoided both groupthink and flame-war extremes. Tetlock parallels
their culture to Amy Edmondson's **psychologically safe** surgical teams — a nurse
corrects a surgeon without hesitation. Linguistic marker: members said **"our"**
more than **"my."** Contrary to workload-sharing intuition, the best teams often
**increased** individual effort as commitment grew; a working core of five or six
members typically drove most output.

Concrete dynamics from GJP superteams:

- **Elaine Rich** (pharmacist): initially intimidated by credentialed teammates,
  stayed quiet on sensitive questions; over time felt obligation not to freeload,
  found team work "tons more" effort but "a rush."
- **Marty Rosenthal** (team-building consultant): early "dancing around" —
  disagreeing indirectly to avoid offense — faded once members explicitly invited
  pushback and thanked critics. With no assigned leader, he practiced **leading
  from behind** (detailed forecast write-ups as models, organizing workload calls)
  rather than taking over.
- **Paul Theron** (investment manager): on Honduras's 2013 election, tracked
  down specialist analysts, learned a poll lead was "dodgy," shifted his forecast,
  shared everything — Hernández won. "Each team member brings something different."

GJP measured **active open-mindedness (AOM)** at the team level. Team AOM
correlated with accuracy, but team AOM was **not** the sum of individual AOM
scores — collective thinking is an **emergent property** of communication
patterns. Open-minded people who do not engage each other underperform the sum of
their parts; opinionated people who genuinely engage can outperform it.

Adam Grant's **giver / matcher / taker** typology: givers (contribute more than
they receive) improve group outcomes; matchers reciprocate; takers extract.
Examples: Marty Rosenthal deliberately generous to shift norms; Doug Lorch shared
programming tools (best individual year-2 score); Tim Minto shared time-decay
analysis code (topped year-3 chart) — both men's teams won their year's team
competition.

**Caveats**: not a corporate recipe ("hire stars, add teams, remove groupthink,
add givers"). Some unsupportive teams still scored well; one top forecaster
refused to comment on teammates' forecasts to avoid groupthink. Labeling people
"super" can divide organizations; cross-functional moves disrupt workflows;
replication inside real hierarchies is harder. Superteam research was still
**infancy-stage** when Tetlock wrote.
