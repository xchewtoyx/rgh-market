"She has no evidence the firm is badly managed. All she knows is its stock went
down. This is an outcome bias, part hindsight and part halo effect."

**Outcome bias**: evaluating a decision by its result rather than by what was
knowable when it was made. "Let's not fall for the outcome bias. This was a
stupid decision even though it worked out well."

Hindsight bias makes past events seem inevitable once known — corrupting learning
from forecasts and decisions.

**Hindsight bias** ("I-knew-it-all-along"): once outcomes are known, reconstructing
prior beliefs is imperfect — people exaggerate probabilities they assigned to
what occurred and misremember dismissing what did not (Fischhoff/Beyth on Nixon
diplomacy). Same for others' past predictions.

Hindsight bias produces **outcome bias**: judging decision quality by results, not
by process given what was knowable. Low-risk surgery with fatal complication →
jury believes operation was risky all along. Bridge-monitor study: told flood
occurred, 56% said city should have hired monitor vs. 24% without hindsight —
worse consequences, greater hindsight bias. 9/11: "elementary" Tenet should have
gone to the president — on July 10, no one could know this tidbit would dominate
history.

Agents (physicians, CEOs, diplomats) get blamed for good decisions with bad
outcomes and too little credit for good process with lucky outcomes. Anticipated
hindsight pushes defensive SOP choices and risk aversion; it also crowns reckless
lucky gamblers with halos of prescience.

**Counterfactual asymmetry** (Tetlock's Expert Political Judgment work): experts
welcome counterfactuals implying a failed forecast was "almost right" but dismiss
those implying a correct forecast could easily have been wrong. Successful people
rarely admit they did not fully earn a good outcome — so
[forecast postmortems](forecast-postmortem.md) must scrutinize successes, not
only failures.

Pair with [narrative fallacy](narrative-fallacy.md) and
[illusion of validity](illusion-of-validity-and-skill.md).

## Anticipated regret and hindsight avoidance

Susceptibility to [regret](regret-and-action-inaction-asymmetry.md) is "a fact of
life to which one must adjust," akin to susceptibility to fainting spells. A sufficiently
rich, cautious investor may choose a portfolio minimizing expected regret even at
the cost of expected wealth.

Precautions: be explicit about having anticipated regret before deciding — remembering
you considered the possibility carefully reduces regret if things go badly. Regret and
hindsight bias travel together; anything that precludes hindsight helps. Kahneman's
policy for long-term decisions: be either **very thorough or completely casual** —
hindsight is worst when you think "just enough" to later tell yourself "I almost made
a better choice."

Daniel Gilbert's **psychological immune system**: people generally anticipate more
regret than they experience, underestimating defenses they will deploy. Don't put too
much weight on anticipated regret — even if it occurs, it will hurt less than expected.
