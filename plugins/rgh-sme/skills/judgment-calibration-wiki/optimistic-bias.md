"The [planning fallacy](planning-fallacy.md) is only one of the manifestations of
a pervasive optimistic bias." Most people view the world as more benign than it
is, their own attributes as more favorable than they truly are, and their goals as
more achievable than likely — and they exaggerate their ability to forecast the
future, which fosters optimistic overconfidence. Kahneman: "In terms of its
consequences for decisions, the optimistic bias may well be the most significant
of the cognitive biases." Because it is both a blessing and a risk, temperamental
optimists "should be both happy and wary."

Optimism is largely inherited and part of a general disposition toward well-being.
Benefits for mildly optimistic people: cheerfulness, popularity, resilience,
stronger immune systems, longer life, willingness to work longer hours and take
calculated risks. These blessings accrue only to people "only mildly biased and
who are able to 'accentuate the positive' without losing track of reality."

Optimistic individuals disproportionately shape collective life — inventors,
entrepreneurs, political and military leaders. Hypothesis: "the people who have the
greatest influence on the lives of others are likely to be optimistic and
overconfident, and to take more risks than they realize." Optimistic bias plays a
role whenever individuals or institutions voluntarily take on major risk; risk
takers typically underestimate the odds against them and underinvest in finding
out the true odds. Their sustained confidence helps raise resources and improve
odds of prevailing — "when action is needed, optimism, even of the mildly
delusional variety, may be a good thing."

## Entrepreneurial delusions

US small-business five-year survival is about 35%, but entrepreneurs radically
discount this for themselves. Survey: average estimate of success for "any business
like yours" was 60% (roughly double the true value); for their *own* venture, 81%
put odds at 7-in-10 or higher and 33% said failure risk was zero. The [outside
view](inside-view-vs-outside-view.md) often never occurs to a new restaurant owner
even though 60% of new restaurants fail within three years.

Thomas Åstebro's studies (Canada's Inventor's Assistance Program): of lowest-graded
projects (D/E, over 70% of submissions), only 5 of 411 reached commercialization
and none succeeded. About half quit after discouraging grades; 47% persisted,
doubling losses before giving up — persistence correlated with optimism scores.
Overall return on private invention was lower than private equity; self-employment
generally yields lower average financial returns than employment at equal
qualifications.

At the corporate level, the hubris hypothesis: optimistic CEOs (identified by
personal stock holdings) took excessive risk — favoring debt, overpaying for
targets, value-destroying mergers. Acquirer stock suffered more when the CEO was
overly optimistic; they bet more when they personally had more at stake, not less.
Prestigious CEO awards compound damage: award winners' firms subsequently
underperform while compensation rises.

Optimistic risk-taking likely contributes to capitalist dynamism even though most
risk-takers end up disappointed — raising the policy question of whether
government should support would-be entrepreneurs likely to fail.

## Product and MVP optimism

In product discovery, teams often treat story maps and minimum viable solutions as
likely winners rather than hypotheses — the biggest mistake is believing the MVP
will succeed before market observation. Patton's observed split (~20% real success,
~20% real failure, ~60% costly mediocrity) and Standish unused-feature statistics
give an [outside view](inside-view-vs-outside-view.md); see
[product build outcome base rates](product-build-outcome-base-rates.md). Celebrating
at ship while usage is unknown repeats inside-view optimism; see
[process-skill decoupling](process-skill-decoupling.md).

Four [WYSIATI](wysiati.md)-linked mechanisms behind entrepreneurial optimism:

- Focusing on goal and plan while neglecting base rates → [planning fallacy](planning-fallacy.md).
- Focusing on what we want and can do while neglecting others' plans → [competition neglect](competition-neglect.md).
- Attributing outcomes to skill rather than luck → illusion of control.
- Focusing on what we know while neglecting unknowns → [overconfidence](illusion-of-validity-and-skill.md).

## Implementation benefit and limits of correction

Despite costs to decision quality, optimism's main benefit is resilience after
setbacks. Martin Seligman's "optimistic explanation style" — credit for successes,
little blame for failures — builds resilience and can be taught (e.g., cold-call
insurance sales). Kahneman: "I have yet to meet a successful scientist who lacks
the ability to exaggerate the importance of what he or she is doing" — without a
"delusional sense of significance," researchers would be worn down by many small
failures and rare successes.

Kahneman is skeptical that overconfident optimism can be trained away ("I am not
optimistic"). Exception: Royal Dutch Shell geologists became less overconfident
about drilling-site assessments after training against past cases with known
outcomes. Overconfidence was also mitigated when judges considered competing
hypotheses. See [premortem technique](premortem-technique.md) as a partial remedy.
