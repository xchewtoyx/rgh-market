---
type: concept
title: Forecast Accuracy Accountability
description: >
  Judging forecasters by process compliance without scoring outcomes repeats
  medicine's pre-evidence error — accuracy must be measured to improve.
sources:
  - title: Superforecasting
    resource: "Superforecasting (Tetlock, Gardner), ch. 4"
---

The intelligence community historically assessed forecasts through **accountability
for process** — whether analysts followed prescribed methods (alternative hypotheses,
contrary evidence) — not whether forecasts were actually right. Tetlock's analogy:
ensuring physicians wash hands and examine patients but never checking whether
treatment works.

Even the CIA's psychology-of-analysis training manual (Richards Heuer) has never
been tested for whether it improves accuracy — "some analysts think the training
is so intuitively compelling that it doesn't need to be tested." Archie Cochrane's
critique applies: don't believe until you test.

The Iraq WMD National Intelligence Estimate (2002) asserted active programs with
language admitting no surprise — wrong after invasion. Robert Jervis concluded the
judgment was sincere and **reasonable given available evidence**, yet still wrong —
and that analysis could have been better, yielding **less certainty** (60–70% rather
than near-certainty), which might not have cleared a "beyond reasonable doubt" bar.
Postmortems found no red teams, devil's advocates, or competing-hypothesis papers.

IARPA's forecasting tournament and National Research Council (2010, Fischhoff chair)
recommendation: "The IC should not rely on analytical methods that violate well-documented
behavioral principles or that have no evidence of efficacy beyond their intuitive appeal"
and should "rigorously test current and proposed methods."

The right evaluative question is not "Was the judgment correct?" but **"Was the
judgment reasonable given information available at the time?"** — conflating decision
quality with outcomes degrades judgment over time. See [outcome bias and hindsight](outcome-bias-and-hindsight.md),
[Brier score](brier-score.md), [cargo cult science and doubt](cargo-cult-science-and-doubt.md).

Organizations resist comparative judgment tests — "Bob in the CEO suite does not want
to hear... that Dave in the mail room is better at forecasting."
