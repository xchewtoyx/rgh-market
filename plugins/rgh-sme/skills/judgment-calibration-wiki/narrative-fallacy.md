"Narrative fallacy" (Nassim Taleb): flawed stories about the past shape views of
the world and expectations for the future. Compelling narratives are simple,
concrete, assign larger roles to talent and intentions than luck, and focus on
events that happened rather than counterfactuals that did not. "A compelling
narrative fosters an illusion of inevitability."

Google's origin story tempts exaggerating skill and underestimating luck; the
[halo effect](halo-effect.md) lends heroes invincibility. The ultimate test of
an explanation is whether it would have made the event predictable in advance —
no success story meets that for one-off ventures with few reps at "create a giant
company." "The more luck was involved, the less there is to be learned."

Runs on [WYSIATI](wysiati.md): build the best story from limited information;
coherent stories are *easier* when you know less. "Our comforting conviction that
the world makes sense rests on our almost unlimited ability to ignore our
ignorance."

Misuse of "knew" for the 2008 crisis implies the past is more knowable than it
is — and therefore the future should be similarly knowable. Ban "know" unless
truth was demonstrable at the time; "intuition" and "premonition" suffer the same
retrospective-only usage.

The leaderless group challenge seemed to reveal each candidate's "true nature as
direct and compelling as the color of the sky" — yet predictions of officer
training performance were "better than blind guesses, but not by much." The
dismal truth had no effect on next-day confidence — the [illusion of validity](illusion-of-validity-and-skill.md).

Related: [outcome bias](outcome-bias-and-hindsight.md), halo effect.
