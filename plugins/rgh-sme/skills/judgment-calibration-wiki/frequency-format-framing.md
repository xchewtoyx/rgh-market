"What percentage of participants..." produced **65%** conjunction errors; "How
many of the 100 participants..." only **25%**. "100 individuals" evokes spatial
frequency representation — imagine people sorting into a room by category, making
subset relations visually obvious.

This is the **frequency format** effect, confirmed in many experiments. It
addresses [denominator neglect](denominator-neglect.md) and the [conjunction
fallacy](conjunction-fallacy.md) by forcing [System 1 and System 2](system-1-and-system-2.md)
to see that detailed scenarios are subsets of broader ones. [System 1 and System
2](system-1-and-system-2.md) handle individuals far better than abstract categories.

## Low-probability events weigh heavier in frequency format

"A vaccine carries a 0.001% risk of permanent disability" feels small; "One of
100,000 vaccinated children will be permanently disabled" calls up one specific
disabled child while 999,999 safe children fade into the background. **Low-probability
events are weighted more heavily when described as relative frequencies ("how many")
than as abstract probabilities ("how likely").**

Cited studies:

- "A disease that kills 1,286 people out of every 10,000" was judged *more*
  dangerous than "a disease that kills 24.14% of the population" — despite the
  first risk being only half the second.
- "1,286 out of 10,000" was judged more dangerous than "24.4 out of 100" — same
  ratio, different framing.

The effect would likely shrink in a within-subjects design forcing direct
comparison (engaging System 2), but life is usually a between-subjects
experiment — you typically see only one framing at a time.

Even experienced forensic psychologists and psychiatrists aren't immune: judging
whether to discharge a violent patient, professionals shown "10% probability of
committing an act of violence" denied discharge 21% of the time vs. **41%** when
shown "Of every 100 patients ... 10 are estimated to commit an act of violence" —
same statistic, frequency format nearly doubling the denial rate.

## Format as deliberate manipulation

Advocates and litigators engineer format for emotional effect. Mental-health
advocacy might frame "~1,000 homicides a year by seriously mentally ill
individuals not taking medication" alternatively as "1,000 out of 273,000,000
Americans," "0.00036% annual likelihood," or "1,000 Americans ... or less than
one-thirtieth the number who die of suicide" — each framing tuned to a different
reaction. A defense attorney favors "a false [DNA] match occurs in 1 of 1,000
capital cases" over "the chance of a false match is 0.1%" to invite jurors to
picture the specific defendant wrongly convicted; the prosecutor favors the
abstract decimal framing. See [framing effects](framing-effects.md).

Contrast [Sherman Kent probability language](sherman-kent-probability-language.md):
numeric probabilities with explicit denominators serve a similar testability
function for forecasts.

Applied to [scope sensitivity](scope-sensitivity.md): framing must include the
reference class size and time window, not just the story content.
