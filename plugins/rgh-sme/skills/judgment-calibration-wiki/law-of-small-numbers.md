Kahneman and Tversky coined "belief in the law of small numbers": intuitions
about random sampling appear to satisfy the law of large numbers in small
samples too.

**Kidney cancer counties puzzle**: lowest- and highest-incidence US counties
share the same profile — rural, sparsely populated, Republican-leaning. Rural
lifestyle cannot explain both extremes. Small populations draw smaller samples,
so extreme rates appear more often — a statistical artifact, not causation.

**Marble urn illustration**: drawing 4 marbles yields homogeneous outcomes
(all red or all white) 8× more often than drawing 7. "Large samples are more
precise than small samples" and "small samples yield extreme results more often
than large samples do" are the same statement.

System 1 is adept at causal stories but inept with merely statistical facts.
Psychologists routinely chose sample sizes accepting up to 50% risk of failing
to confirm true hypotheses — despite teaching statistics.

**Bias of confidence over doubt**: "In a telephone poll of 300 seniors, 60%
support the president" gets summarized as "elderly support president" — sample
size treated as inert background. Attention goes to the story, not source
reliability; [WYSIATI](wysiati.md) prevents discriminating intermediate
reliability differences. System 1 suppresses ambiguity; sustaining doubt is
harder work than sliding into certainty.

Treat statistical intuitions with proper suspicion; replace impression with
computation where possible.

## Pattern seeking in randomness

"The associative machinery seeks causes." Statistical regularities require asking
what could have happened instead — "chance selected it from among its alternatives."
We are pattern seekers who reject "true randomness" when we detect apparent rules,
though random processes routinely generate rule-looking sequences.

Baby sequences (BBBGGG, GGGGGG, BGBBGB): any specific six-birth sequence is equally
likely, yet only BGBBGB *looks* random — judged far more likely even after instruction.

William Feller on London Blitz rocket hits: gaps suggested non-random bombing and
suspected German spies in unharmed areas; statistical analysis showed distribution
typical of random process *and* typical in evoking false non-randomness. "To the
untrained eye, randomness appears as regularity or a tendency to cluster."

Yom Kippur War: uneven squadron losses prompted inquiry into what unlucky squadron
did wrong; Kahneman advised accepting difference as blind luck — random search for
non-obvious cause was hopeless.

**Hot hand illusion** (Gilovich, Vallone, Tversky): thousands of NBA shot sequences
satisfy randomness tests despite widespread belief in temporary "hot" streaks that
changes real play-calling. "If you follow your intuition, you will more often than
not err by misclassifying a random event as systematic." Connect [skill vs luck
diagnosis](skill-vs-luck-diagnosis.md): how many good years before crediting an
investment adviser or CEO?

**Gates small-schools myth** (Wainer & Zwerling): top schools overrepresented among
small schools, driving ~$1.7B Gates investment — but worst schools are *also*
smaller than average; small schools are more variable, not better on average.

Two broader lessons: attention goes to message content more than reliability
([WYSIATI](wysiati.md)); many facts invite causal explanation but are due to chance,
including sampling accidents.
