title: Prospect Theory Disappointment Limit
description: >
  Prospect theory treats "win nothing" as zero value at the reference point in
  all contexts, but high-probability large wins make zero intensely disappointing.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 26"
---

[Prospect theory](loss-aversion-and-reference-points.md) assigns the reference point
(usually status quo) value zero — reasonable-seeming, but producing absurd
consequences when "winning nothing" means different things experientially.

Compare three prospects, all able to produce "win nothing":

- **A**: one chance in a million to win $1 million
- **B**: 90% chance to win $12, 10% chance to win nothing
- **C**: 90% chance to win $1 million, 10% chance to win nothing

Prospect theory assigns "winning nothing" the same value (zero) in all three. But
winning nothing is a non-event in A and B, yet **intensely disappointing** in C —
a high probability of a large sum sets up a tentative new reference point, so
winning nothing relative to that expectation is experienced as a large loss (like
an informally promised raise that fails to materialize).

Prospect theory **cannot cope with this**: it does not allow an outcome's value to
change when it is highly unlikely or when the alternative is very valuable. The
anticipation of disappointment is real — a flaw as obvious as counterexamples to
Bernoulli's utility-of-wealth model.

Kahneman applies his own **theory-induced blindness** critique reflexively: flaws
in prospect theory contributed to its acceptance as the main alternative to expected
utility. Prospect theory also shares with utility theory the assumption that options
are evaluated separately — which [regret in choice](regret-and-action-inaction-asymmetry.md)
shows is wrong.
