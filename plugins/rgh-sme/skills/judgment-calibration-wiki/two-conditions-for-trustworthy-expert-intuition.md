---
type: concept
title: Two Conditions for Trustworthy Expert Intuition
description: >
  Expert intuition is likely valid only when the environment is sufficiently
  regular and the judge had prolonged practice learning its regularities.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 22"
---

Kahneman and Klein's joint conclusion: **the confidence people have in their
intuitions is not a reliable guide to validity** — do not trust anyone,
including yourself, to tell you how much to trust their judgment.

Validity depends instead on two conditions for skill acquisition:

1. An environment that is **sufficiently regular to be predictable**
2. **Prolonged practice** with opportunity to learn those regularities

When both hold, intuitions are likely skilled. Chess is an extreme case;
bridge, poker, physicians, nurses, athletes, and firefighters also qualify —
accurate intuitions rest on highly valid cues [System 1](system-1-and-system-2.md)
has learned even if System 2 cannot name them.

Stock pickers and long-term political forecasters operate in **zero-validity**
environments: failures reflect genuine unpredictability, not lack of skill.
Intuitive hits there are due to luck or lies; intuition cannot be trusted
without stable regularities.

Even in regular environments, evaluate **provenance** (regularity + learning
history) rather than confidence or plausibility — analogous to authenticating
art by provenance rather than by looking at the piece. A talented chess
youngster still makes confident mistakes en route to expertise.

In low-validity environments, System 1 still produces fast, confident answers
via [substitution](substitution-heuristic.md), and substituted answers feel
subjectively indistinguishable from genuine expert judgment.
