---
type: concept
title: Allais Paradox
description: >
  Choices violating expected utility theory by overweighting certainty relative
  to small probability improvements.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 29"
---

Maurice Allais (1952) showed rational-choice adherents violate expected utility:

- Problem A: 61% chance $520,000 OR 63% chance $500,000
- Problem B: 98% chance $520,000 OR 100% chance $500,000

Most pick left in A and right in B — a logical sin. Problem B's urns are A's
urns with 37 white marbles swapped for red; the left urn's improvement is
objectively larger, so consistent choosers must prefer left in both — yet most
flip for the guaranteed-win urn.

The 100% vs 98% gap (crossing into certainty) psychologically dominates the
63% vs 61% gap — the [certainty effect](possibility-and-certainty-effects.md).

Most economists filed Allais away as anomaly; Kahneman and Tversky kept utility
theory as normative logic but abandoned people-as-rational-choosers, building
[prospect theory](prospect-theory-decision-weights.md) to describe actual behavior.

Amos Tversky called rationalizers of such patterns "lawyers for the misguided."
