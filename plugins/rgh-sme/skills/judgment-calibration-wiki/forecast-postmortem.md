Commandment **(8)**: look for errors behind mistakes but beware rearview-mirror
[hindsight bias](outcome-bias-and-hindsight.md). Don't justify or excuse
failures — own them. Conduct unflinching postmortems: "where exactly did I go
wrong?"

The common error is learning too little from failure (overlooking flaws in basic
assumptions); it's also possible to learn too much from a minor technical mistake
with outsized ramifications.

Postmortem successes too: a success may reflect offsetting errors rather than
sound reasoning — repeating flawed logic sets you up for a nasty surprise.

Long-term accuracy requires good [calibration and resolution](calibration-vs-resolution.md)
— moving beyond blame-game ping-pong to creatively reduce both misses and false
alarms. Commandment **(10)**: mastering the error-balancing bicycle requires
doing, with unambiguous feedback — reading manuals can't substitute for practice.

Other commandments balance opposing errors: [inside/outside view](inside-view-vs-outside-view.md),
[granular updating](granular-bayesian-updating.md), under/overconfidence,
triage of [forecastability](forecastability-triage.md). Commandment **(11)**: don't
treat commandments as commandments — Moltke: two cases are never exactly the same.

Top forecasters actively seek improvement — unlike fixed-mindset subjects who
avoid revisiting failures. They run lengthy team postmortems after misses,
reflect individually in idle moments, and leave detailed forecast comments from
the moment a question opens so future self-critique can reconstruct reasoning.
When a team got a question right but nearly missed, good forecasters still ask
whether luck masked a flaw — the same [outcome bias](outcome-bias-and-hindsight.md)
that makes successes feel fully earned.

The forecasting try-fail-analyze-adjust cycle runs over months or years with noisy
feedback, so [perpetual beta](perpetual-beta.md) — continuous updating and
self-improvement — matters more than raw intelligence for rising into the top ranks.
