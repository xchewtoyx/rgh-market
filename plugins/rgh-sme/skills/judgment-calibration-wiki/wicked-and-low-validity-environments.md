---
type: concept
title: Wicked and Low-Validity Environments
description: >
  Environments where feedback misleads learning or valid cues are too weak for
  reliable intuitive forecasting, even for genuine experts.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 22"
---

Robin Hogarth's **wicked environments** let professionals learn the *wrong*
lessons. An early-20th-century physician developed a "clinical" sense for
typhoid by palpating patients' tongues without washing his hands — predictions
were accurate only because he was infecting each subsequent patient.

**Low-validity** environments have genuine unpredictability but some weak
structure. Paul Meehl's clinicians operated here: even the best statistical
algorithms beat them but were never highly accurate either. Human learning
normally finds strong cues if they exist; algorithms win in noise because they
detect *weakly* valid cues and apply them more *consistently*.

**Zero-validity** environments (stock picking, long-range political forecasting)
offer no stable regularities to learn. Blaming forecasters for failing is wrong;
blaming them for believing they can succeed at an impossible task is fair.

These distinctions connect to [two conditions for trustworthy expert intuition](two-conditions-for-trustworthy-expert-intuition.md)
and [Meehl's clinical versus statistical prediction](meehl-clinical-vs-statistical-prediction.md).
