**Focusing illusion** (one sentence): "Nothing in life is as important as you think
it is when you are thinking about it." A product of [substitution](substitution-heuristic.md)
and [WYSIATI](wysiati.md) — whatever aspect attention is directed to dominates a
global evaluation, even though people don't literally forget there's more to life
than the focal topic.

## Climate and life satisfaction (Schkade study)

Large samples at California, Ohio, and Michigan universities: respondents either
reported detailed satisfaction with life aspects or predicted how someone "with
your interests and values" elsewhere would answer. Californians and Midwesterners
differed greatly on **attitude toward their own climate** (Californians liked it,
Midwesterners disliked theirs), but **no difference in overall life satisfaction**
between regions. Students in both regions still believed Californians are happier —
from **exaggerated belief in climate's importance**. Origin: Kahneman's family debate
about moving from California to Princeton; he argued climate is not a major
well-being determinant (Scandinavian happiness cited against "warmer planet =
happier" forecasts).

## Car pleasure and duration neglect

"How much pleasure do you get from your car?" gets an instant answer; "When do you
get pleasure from your car?" shows you rarely think about the car while driving —
mood is set by whatever else occupies attention. The first question was actually
answered as **"How much pleasure when you think about it?"** — substitution causing
**duration neglect** and a focusing illusion misleading both current enjoyment ratings
and future purchase decisions.

Long-term Californians rarely weigh climate in global life evaluation ("like having
ten toes: nice, but not something one thinks much about"). Recent movers from Ohio
are primed by contrast and **over-weight climate for years** — the illusion can
produce genuine comfort: whether or not the move improved happiness, thinking about
climate makes the mover believe he is happier.

## Paraplegia, adaptation, and forecast error

"What proportion of the day do paraplegics spend in a bad mood?" primes a picture
of dwelling on the condition — accurate soon after accident, not later. Detailed
observation: paraplegics in fairly good mood more than half the time as early as
one month after accident — working, reading, jokes, friends, politics like anyone.

**Attentional adaptation** — thinking about a circumstance less over time — applies
to most long-term circumstances (paraplegia, marriage): **part-time states** inhabited
only when attended to. **Exceptions that do not adapt**: chronic pain, constant loud
noise (biologically tuned to capture attention), severe depression (self-reinforcing
miserable thoughts).

Beruria Cohn's thesis (survey data): estimates of paraplegics' bad-mood proportion,
split by accident 1 month vs 1 year ago and whether respondent knew a paraplegic.
Recent accident: estimates converged (~70–75% bad mood). One year out: those who
**knew** a paraplegic estimated **41%** bad mood; those who did not, **68%** — only
personal exposure correctly forecast adaptation. Same pattern for lottery winners at
one month vs one year.

## Evaluation vs experience

Life satisfaction of paraplegics and others with chronic burdensome conditions runs
**low relative to experienced well-being**, because being asked to evaluate life
inevitably reminds them of others' lives or their former life. Colostomy patients:
experience sampling shows no happiness difference vs healthy population, yet many
would trade years of life for a shorter life without colostomy — evaluation pulls
focal burden into global judgment.

Forecasting implication: predictions about others' (or your own) future well-being
from a salient factor overweight what is easy to simulate now. Pair with
[scope sensitivity](scope-sensitivity.md) (quantity and time horizon),
[prototype vs sum representation](prototype-vs-sum-representation.md) (vivid focal
cases), [affect heuristic](affect-heuristic.md).
