---
type: concept
title: Granular Bayesian Updating
description: >
  Frequent small probability revisions proportional to diagnostic weight of new
  evidence — the Bayesian spirit without necessarily crunching the formula.
sources:
  - title: Superforecasting
    resource: "Superforecasting (Tetlock, Gardner), ch. 7, Supernewsjunkies?"
---

Top forecaster Tim Minto: initial forecasts in 5–15 minutes, then daily returns
with contrary-evidence trawling — often a dozen to 50+ updates per question.
On Syrian refugee counts, 34 updates averaged **3.5 percentage points** each; final
Brier score 0.07. Too few updates risks underreaction; too many large updates
risks overreaction; many *small* updates thread between both.

Worked example: Senate control forecast at 60% moves to ~64% when one Colorado
poll shifts — bounded because one state rarely flips a majority, narrowed by
how many other races look winnable, time to election, and poll predictiveness at
that distance.

Bayes in odds form: Posterior Odds = Likelihood Ratio × Prior Odds. Jay Ulfelder
on Chuck Hagel's nomination: base rate 96% (1 rejection in 24 since WWII);
stress-testing a colleague's 50-50 estimate with likelihood inputs still only
dropped the forecast to 83%. Superforecasters are "Bayesians who do not use
Bayes' theorem" — internalizing update-in-proportion-to-diagnostic-weight matters
more than algebra.

**Not a rigid rule**: Doug Lorch, finding a 95% Arctic sea-ice forecast built on
a stale source with a widening gap, abandoned small increments — reset to 55%,
dropped to 15%, then resumed small updates. Orwell: break any rule sooner than
saying something outright barbarous.

Pair with [Sherman Kent probability language](sherman-kent-probability-language.md)
— distinctions like 0.4→0.35 require numeric granularity vague "maybe" cannot express.

**Updating frequency correlates with skill.** Top forecasters update far more
often than regular ones — e.g., 16+ forecasts per question with alerts tuned to
how fast situations move. Initial forecasts alone already beat regular forecasters
by ~50%; updating skill is additive. Obvious news (near-certain "yes" after a
public announcement) confers no edge — edge comes from catching quieter diagnostic
signals first (e.g., inferring positive polonium detection from a forensic delay).

**Underreaction** resists revising settled beliefs even against clear evidence —
[belief perseverance](belief-perseverance-and-identity-protective-cognition.md).
Bill Flack on Yasukuni Shrine: discounted a credible signal because visiting made
"so little sense" — he was unconsciously answering "Would I visit?" not "Will Abe
visit?" ([substitution](substitution-heuristic.md)). Earl Warren internment logic:
no sabotage interpreted as confirming future sabotage.

**Overreaction** jumps too far on weak or stale evidence. Doug Lorch leapt to 95%
on Arctic sea ice from a month-old consensus report in a fast-moving domain; ice
extent ended higher than the prior year. The [dilution effect](dilution-effect.md)
— confidence swayed by clearly irrelevant facts — is one overreaction driver.

Under- and overreaction are forecasting's Scylla and Charybdis; good updating
threads the middle passage. Non-professionals with less ego invested in any single
forecast may underreact less than career analysts — though "superforecaster" identity
can raise commitment too.
