title: Meehl Clinical vs Statistical Prediction
description: >
  In low-validity environments, simple statistical formulas consistently match
  or beat clinical judgment — with no documented exception across decades of
  research.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 21"
---

Paul Meehl reviewed studies comparing clinical predictions (subjective
impressions of trained professionals) against statistical predictions (a
formula combining a few scores by rule). Example: counselors who interviewed
freshmen for 45 minutes and had grades, aptitude tests, and personal
statements were outperformed by an algorithm using only high school grades and
one aptitude test (11 of 14 counselors beaten). Similar patterns appeared for
parole violations, pilot training, and criminal recidivism.

Roughly 200 follow-up studies: ~60% show significantly better accuracy for
algorithms; the rest are draws — a tie is tantamount to a win for statistical
rules, since they are cheaper. **No exception has been convincingly documented.**

Domains now include cancer longevity, hospital stay, cardiac diagnosis, SIDS
susceptibility, business success, credit risk, foster-parent suitability,
juvenile recidivism, violent behavior, scientific-presentation evaluation,
football winners, and Bordeaux wine prices — all [low-validity environments](wicked-and-low-validity-environments.md).

Meehl, thirty years later: "There is no controversy in social science which
shows such a large body of qualitatively diverse studies coming out so
uniformly in the same direction as this one."

The question is not whether experts are well trained but whether their world is
predictable. See also [simple models beating expert judgment](simple-models-beating-expert-judgment.md)
and the [illusion of validity and skill](illusion-of-validity-and-skill.md).

**Why clinicians resist**: [illusion of validity](illusion-of-validity-and-skill.md)
about long-term predictions — understandable because short-term session hunches
get instant feedback while multi-year outcomes never do; the skill/limit boundary
is "not obvious to them." Meehl quoted clinicians calling statistics "mechanical,
atomistic… blind" vs. clinical method "dynamic, holistic… understanding" —
Kahneman parallels sympathy for humans in man-vs-machine contests (John Henry;
Kasparov vs. Deep Blue) and preference for "natural" over synthetic even when
identical (organic apples; "All Natural" beer). French wine community reacted to
Ashenfelter's formula "somewhere between violent and hysterical."

**Consequential decisions**: Meehl noted horror at denying treatment because a
"blind mechanical" equation misclassifies — proponents counter it's unethical to
use intuition when a lower-error algorithm exists. People weigh **cause of mistake**:
algorithm error feels more poignant than human error with same outcome — emotional
asymmetry becomes moral preference. Hostility may soften as algorithms embed in
recommendations, credit limits, cholesterol guidelines, sports analytics.

**Kahneman's army interview (1955)**: six traits scored via fixed factual questions
in fixed order — anti-[halo effect](halo-effect.md) design. Interviewers resented
"turning us into robots"; compromise allowed post-structure "close your eyes"
global 1–5 score. Sum of six structured ratings beat old global interview
("completely useless" → "moderately useful"); intuitive global score **equally**
predictive — formula weighted both equally. Lesson: "intuition adds value even in
the justly derided selection interview, but only after disciplined collection of
objective information and disciplined scoring of separate traits." System unchanged
~45 years later.
