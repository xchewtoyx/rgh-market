Attributed to Paul Slovic: people let their likes and dislikes determine beliefs
about the world — "the dominance of conclusions over arguments is most pronounced
where emotions are involved."

The **affect heuristic** is substitution: "How risky is X?" becomes "How do I feel
about X?" Frightening thoughts occur with particular ease; fluent danger imagery
exacerbates fear. Slovic linked this to Antonio Damasio's view that emotional
evaluations and approach/avoidance tendencies centrally guide decision making —
patients who cannot display appropriate pre-decision emotions show impaired
choices.

**Technology risk/benefit experiment**: respondents rated benefits and risks of
technologies (fluoridation, chemical plants, preservatives, cars), then read
passages emphasizing either high benefits or low risks. Messages shifted emotional
appeal — and, with no new evidence on the opposite attribute, shifted beliefs in
that opposite direction too: liked technologies seen as less risky; low-risk
technologies rated higher on benefits. "The emotional tail wags the rational dog."
Good technologies appear to have few costs; bad ones appear to have no benefits —
decisions feel easy despite real tradeoffs.

Examples: political preference shapes which health-policy arguments feel compelling;
hawks see other nations as weak and submissive, doves as strong and resistant;
attitude toward irradiated food, nuclear power, or motorcycles drives perceived
benefits and risks — disliking something predicts high risk and negligible benefit.

Learning that a disliked activity's risk is lower than thought can shift beliefs
a little — but the same information also improves perceived **benefits**, even
when nothing was said about benefits. System 2 in attitude contexts acts more as
**endorser** of System 1's emotions than critic, searching for information
consistent with existing beliefs.

This is a form of [substitution](substitution-heuristic.md): "How risky is X?"
becomes "How do I feel about X?" Fits the broader pattern where an active,
coherence-seeking System 1 suggests solutions to an undemanding System 2.

When evaluating judgments, ask: "Do we still remember the question we are trying
to answer? Or have we substituted an easier one?"
