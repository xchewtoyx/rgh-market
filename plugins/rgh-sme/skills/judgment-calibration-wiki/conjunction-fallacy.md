---
type: concept
title: Conjunction Fallacy
description: >
  Judging a conjunction of events more probable than one of its constituents
  when a richer story feels more representative.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 15"
---

Linda is thirty-one, outspoken, concerned with social justice, a philosophy
major. "Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement" feels more
probable than "Linda is a bank teller" — yet P(conjunction) ≤ P(either part).

**89% of undergraduates** and **85% of decision-science doctoral students**
violated the logic. The fallacy persists like the Müller-Lyer illusion even when
recognized — representativeness/coherence substitutes for probability.

**Comparison format matters**: 64% got a short two-item comparison right; only
15% on an eight-item list where items weren't directly compared — forcing
comparison engages System 2.

Forecasting trap: "Earthquake in California causing a flood drowning 1,000+" is
more plausible but strictly less probable than "Massive North American flood
drowning 1,000+" — adding detail makes scenarios more persuasive but less likely.

Correctives: anchor on [base rates](base-rate-neglect.md); question evidence
diagnosticity; use [frequency format framing](frequency-format-framing.md)
("how many of 100") to make subset relations visually obvious.

Related: [less-is-more pattern](less-is-more-pattern.md).
