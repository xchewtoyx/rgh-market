[System 1](system-1-and-system-2.md)'s central job is maintaining and continuously
updating a **model of normality** for your personal world — built from associative
links among circumstances, events, actions, and outcomes that co-occur with some
regularity. As links strengthen, the pattern represents the structure of events in
your life and shapes interpretation of the present and expectations of the future.

**Surprise** is the most sensitive indicator of how you understand the world. Two
varieties:

- **Active (conscious) expectations** — you know you are waiting for a specific
  event (listening for the door when a child is due home). Surprise if the expected
  event fails to occur.
- **Passive expectations** — a much larger set of events you do not wait for but
  are not surprised by when they happen, because they are *normal* for the situation
  even though not probable enough to be actively expected.

## One coincidence rewrites the norm

A single incident can update the associative network so a second, objectively less
likely recurrence feels *more* normal:

- Meeting acquaintance Jon at a small island resort, then again two weeks later in a
  London theater — the second meeting was statistically far less likely than any
  other acquaintance appearing, yet felt more normal because Jon had become "the
  psychologist who shows up when we travel abroad." [System 2](system-1-and-system-2.md)
  recognized the absurdity; System 1 had already normalized it.
- A car on fire beside the same stretch of road on two successive Sunday drives —
  that location became "the place where cars catch fire," turning a passive
  expectation into an active one for months afterward.

## Norm theory (Kahneman & Miller)

**Norm theory** explains how events come to be perceived as normal or abnormal. At a
restaurant, the first guest to taste soup winces. That single incident retroactively
alters the normality of subsequent events — it becomes unsurprising if the same guest
startles at a waiter's touch, or if another guest recoils from the same soup. Later
events feel normal not because they confirm advance expectations, but because they
**recruit** the original episode from memory and are interpreted jointly with it. Two
abnormal events, once linked, weave into one coherent pattern (e.g., "an exceptionally
tense guest" or, if a second diner rejects the soup, "the soup is bad"). See
[associative coherence and activation](associative-coherence-and-activation.md).

Brain-response studies show norm violations detected with remarkable speed — a
distinctive pattern within two-tenths of a second of an anomalous word (e.g., "Earth
revolves around the **trouble** every year"). The same fast response occurs for
semantic/social incongruities requiring extensive world knowledge — a male voice
saying "I believe I am pregnant," or an upper-class accent saying "I have a large
tattoo on my back" — instant cross-reference of voice-identified social category
against category norms.

The [Moses illusion](moses-illusion.md) is a canonical demonstration. Connect

registers surprise when violated), [automatic causal storytelling](automatic-causal-storytelling.md),
