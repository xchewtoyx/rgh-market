**GJP origins**: Tetlock and Barbara Mellers launched the Good Judgment Project
(summer 2011), one of five university teams in IARPA's forecasting tournament
(Sept 2011–June 2015). **20,000+** volunteers answered ~**500** world-affairs
questions (~**1 million** judgments), horizons typically **one month to one year**
— shorter than Tetlock's earlier decades-long expert study. GJP beat the control
group by **60%** (year 1) and **78%** (year 2), competitors by **30–70%**, and
even professional intelligence analysts with classified access. IARPA dropped other
teams after two years.

Two headline conclusions: **(1)** foresight is real — measurable skill at 3–18 month
horizons, not mystical decades-ahead vision; **(2)** skill comes from **what
people do**, not who they are — learnable habits. A ~**60-minute** tutorial
(Ten Commandments summary) improved tournament accuracy ~**10%** across all
volunteers — low cost, outsized effect. Aaron Brown (AQR): sustained modest edges
separate consistent winners from chronic losers; poker analogy — elite players
distinguish 60/40 from 40/60 bets.

**Meehl's rule in geopolitics**: when a well-validated statistical algorithm
exists, use it — but such algorithms rarely cover novel complex geopolitical
questions, so subjective judgment retains a large practical domain. Watson's
David Ferrucci: lookup ("Which two Russian leaders traded jobs in the last ten
years?") differs from forecasting ("Will they trade jobs in the next ten years?")
— modeling intentions and causal politics remains "staggeringly hard." Machines
may mimic meaning before originating it; Herbert Simon's 1965 twenty-year
full-automation forecast did not pan out.

**Near-term trajectory**: not full replacement but **human-machine hybrids** —
"freestyle chess" teams beating humans or computers alone — pairing experts with
systems to counteract cognitive biases. The "dueling pundit" polemic model
(Krugman, Ferguson, Friedman, Stephens) may look obsolete as hybrids mature.
