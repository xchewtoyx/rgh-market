  which beat ordinary teams, which beat wisdom-of-crowd averages of solos.

Good Judgment Project randomized comparisons across years produced a consistent
**aggregation hierarchy** for geopolitical forecasting:

1. **Wisdom-of-crowd average** of solo volunteer forecasts (baseline).
2. **Ordinary teams** — about **10%** more accurate than the crowd average.
3. **Prediction markets** (Inkling, Lumenogic, etc.) — about **20%** more accurate
   than ordinary teams.
4. **Superteams** (elite forecasters on coached remote teams) — **15–30%** more
   accurate than prediction markets.

Year-1 team gains (~23% over solo) led GJP to make teams permanent. Year-2
superteams (~50% gain for already-elite individuals) showed teaming itself can
**offset** expected [regression to the mean](regression-to-the-mean.md) from
[skill vs luck diagnosis](skill-vs-luck-diagnosis.md).

Finance colleagues object that GJP markets lacked liquidity, real money, and a
critical mass of traders — a fair, testable caveat. Tetlock notes markets still
did "a pretty good job" on complex global events, and the strong-form efficient
market hypothesis is hard to reconcile with known psychological biases.

Scott Page's "diversity trumps ability" claim: aggregation gains come from
**different** perspectives; uniform views produce more of the same. GJP prioritized
ability when forming superteams; Page argues stronger diversity-first selection
might have helped further. Tetlock frames ability **and** diversity as tunable
mix, not a dichotomy.
