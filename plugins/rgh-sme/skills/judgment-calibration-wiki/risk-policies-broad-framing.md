A **risk policy** is a broad frame embedding one risky choice in a set of similar
choices — e.g., "always take the highest deductible" or "never buy extended
warranties." Occasional losses are tolerable because the policy wins in aggregate.

**Narrow vs. broad framing**: concurrent decisions (i) sure $240 gain vs. 25%
chance $1,000; (ii) sure $750 loss vs. 75% chance lose $1,000. Separately, ~73%
choose A and D (risk-averse on gains, seeking on losses). Combined, AD (25% win
$240, 75% lose $760) is **dominated** by BC (25% win $250, 75% lose $750) — yet
only ~3% chose B+C when split. Humans are narrow framers by nature; [WYSIATI](wysiati.md)
and effort aversion block enforcing consistency even when asked to consider jointly.

**Samuelson's friend**: rejects one 50/50 lose-$100/win-$200 bet but accepts 100
such bets — "I would feel the $100 loss more than the $200 gain." Samuelson proved
utility maximizers who reject one should reject many — common sense rejects that
conclusion. Rabin & Thaler: 100-bet package has EV $5,000, ~1/2,300 chance of
any loss, ~1/62,000 of losing >$1,000. Sam's loss aversion (2×) makes single toss
worth $0 subjective value but bundles become attractive as loss probability shrinks.

**"You win a few, you lose a few"**: mantra for small positive-EV gambles viewed as
a bundle — only for **independent** bets, when loss won't threaten total wealth,
and **not for long shots**. Broad framing blunts loss aversion: "think like a trader"
instructions reduced skin-conductance response to losses and increased risk taking.
Investors checking performance daily suffer narrow framing — quarterly review may
suffice; less churn, less pain, often better outcomes.

Analogous to the [outside view](inside-view-vs-outside-view.md): shift from this
case's specifics to statistics of similar cases.

**Two opposing biases** broad framing can balance:

- [Planning fallacy](planning-fallacy.md) optimism
- [Loss aversion](loss-aversion-and-reference-points.md) caution on marginal bets

They need not cancel; organizations should aim to eliminate both excesses.

Thaler's 25 division managers: none accept a 50-50 large loss/double gamble alone;
CEO wants all to accept — statistical aggregation across bets mitigates firm risk
(same logic as many coin tosses vs. one).

Examples: "Evaluate portfolio only quarterly — too loss averse for daily prices";
"Think like a trader — you win a few, lose a few"; executives loss-averse in their
domains → organization under-takes risk.
