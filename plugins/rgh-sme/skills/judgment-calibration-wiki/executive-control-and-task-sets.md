Effort is required to hold **multiple ideas simultaneously** when they need separate
actions or must be combined by a rule — rehearsing a shopping list, choosing between
restaurants, combining a surprising survey result with a small sample. Only
[System 2](system-1-and-system-2.md) follows rules, compares objects on several
attributes, and makes deliberate choices; System 1 detects simple relations
("they are all alike") and integrates information about one thing, but cannot juggle
multiple distinct topics or use purely statistical information well. Combining a
"meek and tidy soul" [representativeness](representativeness-heuristic.md) intuition
with [base-rate](base-rate-neglect.md) knowledge is a System 2 task "few people"
know how to perform.

## Task sets

A crucial System 2 capability is adopting **task sets**: programming memory to obey
an instruction overriding habitual response (e.g., "count all occurrences of letter
f on this page"). Psychologists call adoption/termination of task sets **executive
control**; neuroscientists identify brain regions for executive function, including
areas active whenever conflict must be resolved, and prefrontal cortex (more developed
in humans than other primates), associated with intelligence-related operations.

**Switching task sets** is itself effortful, especially under time pressure —
switching from counting f's to counting commas requires overriding the newly formed
f-focus tendency. This **switching cost** helps explain why Add-3 and mental
multiplication are so hard: Add-3 holds several digits, each tagged with different
processing state (queued, mid-transformation, transformed awaiting report).

People strong on working-memory tests with repeated switching between demanding
tasks tend to score well on general-intelligence tests — but attentional control is
not merely intelligence: efficiency of attention control predicts air-traffic-controller
and Israeli Air Force pilot performance beyond intelligence alone.

## Time pressure

Time pressure further drives effort. In Add-3, the metronome and **memory decay**
force pace — "like a juggler with several balls," material must be constantly
refreshed. "The most effortful forms of slow thinking are those that require you
to think fast." Demanding day-to-day work rarely matches Add-3 or six-digit recall.
