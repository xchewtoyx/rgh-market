---
type: concept
title: Vivid Outcomes Reduce Probability Sensitivity
description: >
  Rich mental representations of outcomes — emotional or not — shrink the role
  probability plays in valuation relative to expected-value calculation.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), ch. 30"
---

Standard utility theory weights outcomes exactly by probability; [prospect theory
decision weights](prospect-theory-decision-weights.md) already compress intermediate
probabilities. Both assume decision weights depend only on probability, not outcome
magnitude or type — a prediction that fails for vivid or affect-laden prospects.

University of Chicago study ("Money, Kisses, and Electric Shocks"): gamble
valuations were far less sensitive to probability when outcomes were emotional
(kissing a movie star; a painful but safe electric shock) than for cash. Heart-rate
studies found fear of an impending shock was essentially uncorrelated with shock
probability — mere possibility triggered full fear response. Explanation: "affect-laden
imagery" overwhelms response to probability.

Princeton follow-up: low probability sensitivity for emotional outcomes may be the
*normal* case; cash gambles are the exception because they have definite, easily
anchored expected value. Cash equivalents for "84% chance to win $59" vs. "84%
chance to receive a dozen red roses" — money anchors near ~$50 EV; roses have no
such anchor. Sensitivity to probability was much more pronounced for money than roses.
Even attaching explicit price ("Value $59") did not restore sensitivity — recipients
didn't use price as a monetary anchor when thinking of "roses."

To rule out emotion alone: willingness to pay to avoid painting an apartment vs.
cleaning dormitory bathroom stalls — despite stalls being more aversive, decision
weights did not differ.

Kahneman's hypothesis: a **rich, vivid representation of an outcome** — emotional
or not — reduces probability's role. Adding irrelevant vivid detail to a monetary
outcome should disrupt calculation — e.g., "$59 next Monday" vs. "a large blue
cardboard envelope containing $59 next Monday morning" should show less probability
sensitivity for the envelope framing. Mechanism: cognitive ease contributes to the
[certainty effect](possibility-and-certainty-effects.md) too — a vividly held image
makes the vividly represented possibility of its *not* occurring also overweighted.
Combined enhanced possibility and certainty effects leave little room for decision
weights to shift between 21% and 84%.
