**Resulting** is the poker term for judging the quality of a decision by the quality of its outcome, rather than by the quality of the reasoning and the information actually available at the time it was made. It is a close cousin of **hindsight bias** — the tendency, once an outcome is known, to see it as having been predictable or even inevitable ("I should have known"). Both errors are seductive because outcomes are concrete and legible while decision quality is not, but they diverge sharply whenever a decision is made under genuine uncertainty: a reasonable, well-evidenced call can still be followed by a bad result purely through bad luck or a low-probability event, and a poor, thinly-reasoned call can still get lucky and work out.

This matters directly for evaluating decisions made during an incident. A responder who chose to roll back a deployment based on the [strongest available signal](change-correlation-in-debugging.md) made a good decision even if the rollback turned out not to be the actual cause and the outage continued — the decision was sound given what was knowable at the time, and grading it by the outcome alone teaches the wrong lesson (e.g., "don't trust deploy-correlation next time," when the real lesson might be "the signal was reasonable but incomplete, and here's what would have made it more complete"). Conversely, a responder who guessed right by chance, without a defensible process, shouldn't have that guess reinforced as good practice just because it happened to work.

The corrective is to evaluate a past decision against the information and reasoning available **at the moment it was made**, not against information that only became available afterward — which requires actually recording what was known and why at decision time, not reconstructing it retroactively once the full picture is clear. This is the same discipline [systematic troubleshooting](systematic-troubleshooting-process.md) asks for looking forward (reason from current evidence, not from what later evidence will show); resulting is the mirror-image failure mode when looking backward at a decision already made. It is a distinct failure from [confirmation bias during an active investigation](confirmation-bias-in-debugging.md) — that one distorts which evidence you seek while still mid-investigation; resulting distorts how you grade a decision after the fact, once the outcome is already known. [Stating hypotheses with a calibrated confidence level](calibrated-confidence-in-hypothesis-statements.md) during the incident is what makes this kind of after-the-fact evaluation possible at all — a decision recorded only as a flat claim gives a postmortem nothing to check against except the outcome, inviting resulting by default.
