title: Recognition-Primed Diagnosis
description: Experienced operators mostly diagnose incidents by recognizing a situation as a familiar pattern and mentally simulating whether the first plausible response will work, not by comparing multiple hypotheses side by side — a strategy that is fast and usually correct but breaks down on genuinely novel failures.
sources:
  - title: "Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions"
    resource: "Sources of Power (Gary Klein), ch. 2-3"
---

Studies of experienced decision-makers under time pressure (firefighters, pilots, operators) found that roughly 80% of their decisions were made without ever comparing two options against each other. Instead, experience lets a person recognize a situation as an instance of a familiar pattern, which simultaneously supplies a plausible goal, the cues worth attending to, an expectation of what should happen next, and a typical response — the response is simply the first one that comes to mind, not the winner of a comparison. This is the **recognition-primed decision (RPD)** model, and its evaluation step is **singular, not comparative**: the operator mentally simulates the single candidate response, checks whether the simulation reveals a problem, and only moves to a second candidate if the first one fails the mental run-through.

Applied to production debugging, this describes most on-call pattern-matching: an experienced engineer sees a shape of symptoms, recognizes it as "one of those," and reaches for the fix that has worked before — without formally comparing it to alternatives. This is fast, usually correct, and is exactly what [debugging from first principles vs. tacit knowledge](debugging-from-first-principles-vs-tacit-knowledge.md) calls tacit-knowledge debugging. RPD explains *why* that mode works when it works: expertise is pattern recognition plus fast mental simulation, not exhaustive comparison.

The model also explains why tacit-knowledge debugging fails on a genuinely novel incident: when a situation doesn't clearly match a known pattern, experienced people don't force a bad match — they shift into slower, explicitly comparative evaluation, deliberately weighing several courses of action, precisely because recognition has nothing to offer. This is the same fallback [known-unknowns vs. unknown-unknowns](known-unknowns-vs-unknown-unknowns.md) describes for tooling: pattern-matched runbooks and dashboards cover known-unknowns, but an unrecognized failure shape forces the slower, hypothesis-driven investigation that the [core analysis loop](core-analysis-loop.md) formalizes as a repeatable procedure. A practical implication for incident response: an operator's confident, fast diagnosis is trustworthy exactly to the degree the current failure resembles ones they've seen before, and a wise team treats an operator's own hesitation ("this doesn't feel like a match") as a genuine signal to escalate to slower, evidence-gathering investigation rather than to keep pushing for a quick call.
