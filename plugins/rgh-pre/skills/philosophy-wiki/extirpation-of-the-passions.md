---
type: concept
title: Eliminating the Passions Rather Than Moderating Them
description: >
  The Stoic argument, against the rival Peripatetic view, that destructive
  emotions should be uprooted entirely rather than kept at a "moderate"
  level, because a moderate emotion still rests on a false judgment and
  tends to escalate.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), Letters 85, 116"
---

The Peripatetic school, following Aristotle, held that virtue lies in
feeling emotions like anger, fear, and grief in the *right amount* — not
absent, but moderate and well-calibrated. The Stoics rejected this and
argued instead that such emotions should be eliminated altogether, not
merely dialed down. Seneca's fullest defense of the harder position is
Letter 85, responding directly to Peripatetic objections.

His central argument is that a "moderate" bad emotion is not actually
safer than a strong one, because emotions are governed by their external
trigger rather than by the person's intentions, and once a person concedes
that some anger or grief is appropriate, there is no principled place to
stop the escalation: "if you attribute any emotions at all to the sage,
reason will be outmatched by them... A crowd of emotions, even moderate
ones, is more powerful than any single emotion would be, even at full
force." He makes the case that several small vices in one person — a
little greed, a little ambition, a little anger — are in practice harder
to manage than one large one, and treats the whole idea of a "moderate"
passion the way he would treat moderate insanity or moderate illness: not
a real, stable category at all. His practical conclusion is that
prevention beats management: "it is easier to forestall their beginnings
than to govern the impulse," since once a passion is triggered by
something genuinely outside our control, it will scale with the size of
that external trigger rather than staying at any chosen level.

Letter 116 restates the position more concisely and addresses the natural
objection that total elimination sounds inhuman: "I myself don't see how it
can be healthy or useful to have even a moderate amount of an illness." He
is careful to distinguish this from advocating flat suppression of all
feeling — legitimate *wanting*, as opposed to anxious *desire*, survives the
cure, so ordinary pleasures remain available, "pursued with firmer resolve"
precisely because the person is their master rather than their slave. He
also grants an important asymmetry in capacity: the fully wise person might
safely permit himself controlled tears or pleasure and stop at will, but
"we others have difficulty in withdrawing, and so it's best for us not to
go on at all" — a caution grounded in the wider Stoic account of
[stages of moral progress](stages-of-moral-progress.md), where only the sage
has the stability to flirt with a passion without being carried away by it.

This thesis is the reason emotions like anger and grief are treated, across
the letters, as errors of judgment to be corrected rather than as forces to
be tamed into acceptable proportion — the underlying premise being that
[virtue is the sole good](virtue-as-the-sole-good.md), so any passion keyed
to the rise or fall of a mere [indifferent](preferred-indifferents.md) is,
at root, always a mistake, whatever its intensity. It is distinct from the
Stoic sage's stable positive affect, discussed separately as
[joy as opposed to pleasure](joy-vs-pleasure.md).
