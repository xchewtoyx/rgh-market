---
type: concept
title: Courage of Prevention
description: >
  The claim that the higher courage often lies in preventing unnecessary
  conflict — through restraint, diplomacy, and refusal to escalate — rather
  than in winning the fights that need never have happened.
sources:
  - title: "Courage Is Calling: Fortune Favors the Brave"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 16"
---

Lincoln's underrated achievement was his effort to prevent the Civil War, not
just win it. Despite a fair election win and repeated assurances he wouldn't
exceed constitutional authority, the South seceded before his inauguration.
His first inaugural address still closed with an appeal to "the better angels of
our nature": "We are not enemies, but friends... Though passion may have strained,
it must not break our bonds of affection." At the Fort Sumter standoff he sent
only food and supplies, not troops or guns, refusing to escalate. Principle: "A
confrontation that doesn't need to happen, shouldn't happen." Suffering takes
courage to endure, but wisdom and compassion obligate us to avoid it when
unnecessary and to protect others from it too — heroes fight as hard to
*prevent* conflict as to win the rare one they're drawn into.

Gandhi said he'd rather choose violence than cowardice, but chose something
more heroic still — fighting with soul and spirit, unarmed, against armed
enemies. Malala Yousafzai, shot by the Taliban for seeking an education: "Even
if there was a gun in my hand and he was standing in front of me, I would not
shoot him." This heroism is less cinematic than a cavalry charge — people read
about wars, not the diplomacy that prevented them; about whistleblowers, not
the leaders who reformed companies from within without needing one. "Nobody gets
credit for things that didn't happen." FDR's real accomplishment, per Holiday,
was less staring down the Depression than the reforms that quietly prevented
future depressions.

Physical courage (brave soldiers) and moral courage (wise statesmen) are
complementary: one fights the battles, the other cultivates the relationships
and policies that reduce their necessity — both are warriors for their cause.
"Half-cocked is not courage"; "'macho' is often masochism"; there's no glory
in winning a fight, physical or verbal, toward immoral or unnecessary ends.
Heroic questions: Does anybody need to die or lose their reputation over this?
Could better decisions solve it in the future? What if you let the other person
save face? Hannah Johnston Bailey: "A man has not the moral courage to plead
for peace, for fear he shall be accused of effeminacy or cowardice" — LBJ in
Vietnam knew it was a losing proposition but feared looking soft. Sun Tzu: best
to win without fighting, maneuvering so the enemy has already lost.

Lincoln's restraint maneuvered the South into firing first and appearing the
aggressor in a war they claimed to be victims of — a moral contradiction they
never overcame. He used the time gained by restraint to collect the ingredients
for victory. Willing to negotiate and compromise, but not to run away — "we
avoid the petty fights so we can be ready for the ones that matter." When war
did come, Lincoln fought as hard as Churchill and de Gaulle later would.
Shakespeare's Polonius: "Beware / Of entrance to a quarrel, but being in, /
Bear 't that th' opposed may beware of thee." This extends [courage requires a
worthy cause](courage-requires-a-worthy-cause.md) by asking whether the cause
justifies the fight at all, and pairs with [nonviolent restraint under
assault](nonviolent-restraint-under-assault.md) when prevention fails and
absorption replaces retaliation.
