Across the Stoic writers, keeping mortality constantly in view functions as
a corrective instrument rather than a source of dread. Epictetus's advice
is brief and direct: "Keep death, exile, and other frightening things
before your eyes daily, especially death — this prevents mean thoughts and
excessive desire" (Handbook 21). The logic is that most trivial anxieties
and petty ambitions only feel urgent because a person is implicitly
planning as though they had unlimited time; remembering that time is
strictly finite reorders what actually deserves attention.

Seneca returns to this discipline more than almost any other. Letter 1
opens the whole collection with the claim that time is the one thing that
is truly ours, and that most of life is lost not to disaster but to
inattention — "when we are inattentive, we miss it all." He recommends
daily rehearsal of readiness to relinquish life (Letter 4, Letter 26,
Letter 61) and treating each day as if it might be the last: "this day is
my last — or if not the very last, still almost the last" (Letter 15). He
approvingly reports the practice of Pacuvius, who held mock funeral rites
for himself nightly, reframed positively as a way of treating each morning
as a gain and each night's close with the thought "I have done living; I
have run the race that fortune set for me" (Letter 12). Late in the
collection, on hearing of a friend's sudden death after an ordinary,
productive final day, he draws the corresponding rule for daily life:
"Let us settle our accounts with life each and every day... By putting the
final touch on one's life every day, you don't lack time" (Letter 101). He
ties the same discipline to self-discipline generally: "nothing will do as
much to help you toward self-control in all things as the reflection that
life is short... in every act, keep your eyes on death" (Letter 114).

Marcus Aurelius treats the same practice as a way of forcing clarity about
what is actually worth doing: "You could leave life right now. Let that
determine what you do and say and think" (*Meditations* 2.11); "not to live
as if you had endless years ahead of you. Death overshadows you. While
you're alive and able — be good" (4.17); "perfection of character: to live
your last day, every day, without frenzy, or sloth, or pretense" (7.69).

Montaigne, after being thrown from a horse and nearly dying, found the brush
with mortality energizing rather than morbid — death "concentrates the mind
wonderfully," as Samuel Johnson later put it — and spent the rest of his life
deliberately studying death across cultures to preserve that clarity without
requiring another accident. Shakespeare, aging, wrote that "every third thought
shall be my grave." The cliché question — what would you do if told you had
cancer? — hides an insidious lie: the diagnosis is already terminal for
everyone; the death sentence was decreed at birth, and probability erodes
tomorrow constantly. Death is not up to us beyond ordinary care; how we live
given finitude is. In the shadow of mortality, prioritization, graciousness,
and principle become easier — "Why would you do the wrong thing? Why feel
fear?" — and if even mortality can be turned to advantage, lesser obstacles
have no excuse for despair.

The practice is distinct from, though it supports, the separate
philosophical case that
[death itself is not something to be feared](death-is-not-to-be-feared.md).
Memento mori is not primarily an argument about the nature of death; it is
a discipline of attention, closely related to
[the view from above](view-from-above.md) as a technique for stripping
triviality from present concerns, and to
[premeditating adversity](premeditation-of-adversity.md) as a way of facing
the future without being blindsided by it.
