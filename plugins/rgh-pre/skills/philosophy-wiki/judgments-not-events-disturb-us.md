The *Handbook*'s fifth chapter states the claim that the rest of Stoic
practice is built on: "It isn't the things themselves that disturb people,
but the judgements that they form about them." Death is not terrible in
itself — if it were, it would have seemed terrible to Socrates too, and it
did not. What varies between a person who is shattered by an event and one
who is not is never the event; it is the belief formed about it.

Epictetus draws out the mechanism using the Iliad: "The Iliad consists of
nothing more than impressions and the use of impressions" (Discourses
1.28.12). Paris's abduction of Helen, and Helen's decision to go with him,
were both produced by impressions; a different impression in Menelaus
(treating the loss as a trivial one) would have erased the entire war along
with the poems it inspired. Every catastrophe in every classical tragedy —
Medea, Oedipus, Atreus — "has no other origin than this": an impression
followed without being tested. He offers a three-stage account of how far a
person has progressed philosophically: the uneducated blame others for
their trouble, the partly-educated blame themselves, and the fully educated
blame no one (Handbook 5) — because they have stopped locating the source
of their distress outside their own judgment at all.

Marcus Aurelius restates this as a piece of self-command available at any
moment: "External things are not the problem. It's your assessment of
them. Which you can erase right now" (*Meditations* 8.47). He treats this
as therapeutic in the most literal sense — a way of stripping "the added
judgement" off a bare fact. Told that someone's son has died, the correct
response registers only the fact ("his son has died"), not the further,
separate, optional judgment that this is a catastrophe (Epictetus,
Discourses 3.8). Seneca makes the same point about physical suffering:
"pain is a trivial matter when not augmented by belief... every thing
depends on belief; each person is as wretched as he believes himself to be"
(Letter 78), and generalizes it to misfortune overall: "nothing can be
terrible for a man unless there is something in the natural course of
things that he thinks is terrible" (Letter 96).

Stepping back, situations by themselves are neither good nor bad — that
evaluation is something humans supply through perception. Shakespeare's
Hamlet states the same insight in dramatic form: "There is nothing either good
or bad, but thinking makes it so." There is the event itself, and separately
the story told about what it means: an employee's costly mistake can be read as
a catastrophe or as training only experience could provide — the event identical,
the outcome shaped by which judgment is assented to. People are complicit in
both creating and destroying their own obstacles through perception; no one can
force you to agree that a situation is hopeless — relinquishing that power is
itself a decision. Epictetus's podium-and-prison epigraph makes the same point
from another angle: high and low circumstances differ, but freedom of choice can
be maintained in either if you so wish.

This is not a claim that events never matter or that pain isn't real — the
Stoics are explicit that even the wise person feels genuine bodily
sensations and involuntary first reactions (see
[the discipline of testing impressions before assent](testing-impressions-before-assent.md)
for how a trained mind still catches and interrupts a bad judgment before it
becomes a settled belief). It is a claim about where the *disturbance*
comes from, and therefore where the remedy has to be applied: not at the
level of controlling events, most of which are outside
[what is up to us](dichotomy-of-control.md) anyway, but at the level of
examining and correcting the story we tell about them. Marcus's habitual
version of this examination is
[stepping back to view the event from a larger vantage point](view-from-above.md),
and Epictetus's is deliberately choosing
[which "handle" to pick an event up by](two-handles-of-events.md).

Byron Katie restates the same structure for a contemporary audience: "I have
never experienced a stressful feeling that wasn't caused by attaching to an
untrue thought" — sequence: untrue thought that argues with reality, stressful
feeling, action on that feeling that creates more stress. A stressful feeling is
"a compassionate alarm clock that says, 'You're caught in the dream'" — the
feeling often arrives before the thought is noticed, signaling which story to
examine. See [world as self-projection](world-as-self-projection.md) for how
uninvestigated stories compose the "dream," and [arguing with reality causes
suffering](arguing-with-reality-causes-suffering.md) for why resisting what
already happened is the characteristic form such untrue thoughts take.
