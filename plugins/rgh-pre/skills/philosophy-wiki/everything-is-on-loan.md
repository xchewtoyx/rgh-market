title: Everything We Hold Is Only on Loan
description: >
  The framing of possessions, relationships, and even one's own life as
  temporarily granted rather than owned outright, so that loss is a return
  rather than a theft.
sources:

  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"

A load-bearing Stoic reframe for loss of any kind — money, possessions,
health, even a child or a friend — is to treat what was lost as something
that was never truly owned, only lent. Epictetus states the rule for
language as well as attitude: "never say 'I've lost it,' say 'I've given it
back'" — applied explicitly to the death of a child or spouse, who should
be thought of the way a traveler thinks of a room at an inn: welcome to use
"so long as he entrusts it to you" (Handbook 11). He extends the same
image to a sea voyage: gather shells on the beach, but keep attention on
the captain's recall signal, ready to drop everything, and treat wife and
child by the same rule — valuable while granted, instantly relinquished
"if the captain calls" (Handbook 7). Property, body, spouse, and children
were never really given outright, he argues elsewhere, but granted "on the
terms on which they have been granted... for as long as it remains
possible"; complaining when they are withdrawn is effectively fighting with
the party that gave them in the first place (Discourses 4.1.100-113).

Seneca develops the same idea specifically as a resource for consoling
grief. Writing to Lucilius about a mutual friend's death, he reframes loss
as simply the return of a fortune-given gift: "fortune has taken something
from you, but it was fortune that gave [in the first place]" (Letter 63).
He generalizes the point radically: "it is not only our property that has
to go: it is ourselves too; and this very fact, if only we understand it,
is consoling. Be calm when something goes: you must go as well" (Letter
98) — since the self is likewise held only temporarily, no single loss
should be treated as an anomaly. He offers a durable formula for holding
onto what remains after loss: "we can be robbed of 'having,' but never of
'having had'" (Letter 98), elaborated in
[the Stoic approach to grief](consolation-for-grief.md).

The same reframe surfaces, independently, in Mo Gawdat's *Solve for Happy*,
written after his son's death: he describes losing his son's favorite
earring and, in the panic of searching for it, realizing "it was never mine
and never his either" — what was actually kept was not the object but the
internalized relationship ("I have all of Ali. He lives inside me"). He
generalizes this into calling life itself "a rental": "we come into it with
nothing and leave it with nothing... nothing can be gained that will not
eventually be lost," and treats accepting this explicitly as liberating
rather than depriving, since nothing that was never truly owned can be
truly stolen.

The reframe rests on the same [dichotomy of control](dichotomy-of-control.md)
that underwrites [preferred indifferents](preferred-indifferents.md): things
outside our choice were never fully ours to begin with, in the sense that
matters, so their departure is not a violation of anything we actually held.
