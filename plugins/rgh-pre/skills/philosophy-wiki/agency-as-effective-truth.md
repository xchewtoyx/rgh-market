---
type: concept
title: Agency as Effective Truth
description: >
  The claim that what we believe about our power to act becomes self-fulfilling
  — accepting impotence makes it true, while choosing to act can make change
  possible even when the odds look impossible.
sources:
  - title: "Courage Is Calling: Fortune Favors the Brave"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 5, 13, 19"
---

Peter Thiel, outed as gay by Gawker in 2007, asked friends and powerful people
what could be done; he was told repeatedly that nothing could — Gawker was
legally untouchable. Telling someone "there's nothing you can do" is a polite
way of telling them to drop it, and because Thiel believed it, it became true —
for years, despite his brilliance and fortune. Accepting he had no agency
became, in his own term, an "effective truth." Fear determines what is or isn't
possible for everyone — "If you think something is too scary, it's too scary
for you... If you aren't the captain of your fate... then fate is the captain
of you." We choose between two effective truths: that we can change our
situation, or that we're at the mercy of it; between luck and cause and effect.

Xenophon rallying the leaderless Ten Thousand Greeks trapped in Persia framed
the choice as "What is going to happen to me?" versus "What action am I going
to take?" — echoed by General James Mattis: "Never think that you are impotent.
Choose how you respond." Thiel's own line: "Courage is in shorter supply than
genius" — his fear, uncertainty, and bad advice muted his genius despite money,
connections, skill, and resources; he believed he was impotent, and so he was.
Believing you can do something doesn't guarantee success, but not believing makes
failure near-certain.

The same structure applies to hope at civilizational scale. Shackleton's hope
he'd survive against the odds and return for his men, de Gaulle's hope that his
isolation would eventually cease — this kind of hope "can become an effective
truth." Leaders are "dealers in hope" — people need a reason to continue, a "dot
on the horizon," and providing that hope is itself a heroic act. "We must reject
the heresy of despair... We have to tell ourselves a story — about history,
about our lives — that emphasizes agency, progress, the chance of redemption.
We hope against hope against hope." This connects [courage against despair and
nihilism](courage-against-despair.md) and the Stoic [dichotomy of control](dichotomy-of-control.md):
agency is exercised where choice remains, not where it does not.

Agency is taken, not given. For nearly four years after Gawker's attack,
Thiel absorbed others' verdict that nothing could be done — until a Berlin
dinner where "Mr. A" challenged him: "What would the world look like if
everyone thought that way?" Fear deprives power by making you believe you
have none — if you don't believe you can do something, it's guaranteed you
won't try. Florence Nightingale realized she would never be given what she
needed and would have to take it. Napoleon, told a problem was impossible:
"That is not French" — then went and did it. Thiel: agency "begins by
rejecting the unjust tyranny of chance." Da Vinci: few accomplished men got
there by things happening to them — "they are what has happened." Binary:
immovable object or unstoppable force, leader or follower. Agency for its own
sake matters little — what matters is the ends to which it's applied. See
[making your own luck](making-your-own-luck.md) for seeking the moments that
test you rather than waiting for them.
