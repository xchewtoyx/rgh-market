---
type: concept
title: Leadership Through Sacrifice
description: >
  The ethical claim that authority is earned by sharing deprivations and
  absorbing costs for others — not by exemption from the standards one
  imposes, and that the privilege of command is obligation, not license.
sources:
  - title: "Discipline Is Destiny: The Power of Self-Control"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 14"
---

On Christmas Day 1998, Brigadier General Jim Mattis was found personally
working an enlisted man's guard post at Quantico so that soldier could be home
with family, despite twenty years' seniority. General Viscount Slim's lesson:
a leader who is selfless and shares the same deprivations as everyone else
will be followed "to the end of the world." Mattis to a shirking lieutenant:
"The privilege of command is command. You don't get a bigger tent" — the best
commanders take the smaller tent and pass on their extra provisions. Xenophon,
leading the Ten Thousand Greeks out of Persia, was challenged by a foot soldier
for riding while the man carried a shield — Xenophon dismounted and carried the
man's shield himself.

Core distinction: "Being the 'boss' is a job. Being a 'leader' is something
you earn" through self-discipline and moments of sacrifice on others' behalf.
Success and power do not exempt a person from [temperance as self-mastery](temperance-as-self-mastery.md)
or hard work — they obligate more of both. Gregg Popovich took fines and
criticism so players could have longer careers; Harry Belafonte paid bills so
the King family could rest; Antoninus, on taking the throne, told his wife
they'd need to be more generous and stricter with themselves, not less ("Now
that we have gained an empire, we have lost even what we had before"); Marcus
Aurelius sold palace furnishings during a plague.

Counter-examples show the exemption taken instead: senators fleeing to warmer
climates while constituents froze; companies taking pandemic bailouts then
laying off staff and paying executive bonuses; officials dining lavishly while
urging public sacrifice. Plutarch: too many leaders think the "greatest benefit
in governing is the freedom from being governed themselves." Holiday's rebuttal:
the more you've done and the more you have, the higher the standard and the
more selfless you must be, "because it is the right thing to do," not for
optics. Formula: "first in line for danger, last in line for rewards. First in
line for duty, last in line for recognition... To lead, you have to bleed."
This is [virtue exercised under public power](virtue-under-public-power.md)
expressed as service rather than privilege, and pairs with [grace under
pressure](grace-under-pressure.md) when leaders absorb costs others cannot.
