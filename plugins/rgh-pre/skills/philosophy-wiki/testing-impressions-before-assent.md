title: Testing Impressions Before Assent
description: >
  The Stoic discipline of pausing to examine an impression before granting
  it belief, since assent — not the impression itself — is the one fully
  voluntary act where a mind can go right or wrong.
sources:

Stoic psychology distinguishes an impression (*phantasia* — the raw
appearance something makes on the mind, "he insulted me," "this is
dangerous," "this person is desirable") from assent (*sunkatathesis* — the
mind's endorsement of that impression as true). The impression itself
arrives largely involuntarily, the way a loud noise makes even a sage
flinch. What is genuinely within a person's power, on the
[dichotomy of control](dichotomy-of-control.md), is not whether an
impression occurs but whether to assent to it — to treat it as an accurate
report rather than a first, untested draft.

Epictetus makes testing impressions the philosopher's first job, comparable
to an assayer testing coins by sight, touch, and the ring they make when
dropped, or to a grain-measurer who must first establish a reliable unit of
measurement before anything else can be checked (Discourses 1.20, 1.17). He
recommends an explicit pause: "Wait a while for me, my impression, let me
see what you are and what you are an impression of; let me test you out"
(Discourses 2.18.24) — treating the impression as a claim to be
cross-examined rather than a verdict to be obeyed. A concrete drill:
noticing an attractive person and refusing to let the first impression run
forward into an imagined scenario is, in his view, a greater victory than
solving a famous logical puzzle, precisely because it is the same skill —
catching an impression before assent — applied where it actually matters
(2.18.15-18).

Marcus Aurelius gives the same discipline a more confrontational voice,
addressing an intrusive impression directly: "what are you doing here,
Perceptions? Get back to where you came from... it was only force of habit
that brought you... Just go away" (*Meditations* 7.17a). He also builds the
discipline into a habit of stripping a report down to its first,
unembellished layer before allowing any further inference: that someone has
insulted you is one thing; that it has actually done you harm is a separate,
unproven claim the mind is not obliged to accept (8.49).

The discipline explains why the Stoics can hold both that
[judgments, not events, are what disturb us](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md)
and that a person is not simply at the mercy of whatever thought first
occurs to them: between an impression and a settled belief there is a gap,
however narrow it feels in the moment, and that gap is where all Stoic
training actually happens. It is also the basis for the recurring Stoic
claim that even a fully wise person still experiences involuntary bodily
reactions — trembling, pallor, an intrusive thought — without this
constituting a real lapse, since the reaction is only a first impression,
not an assent (Seneca, Letters 57, 71, 74).

Holiday's "practice objectivity" exercise sharpens the same discipline for
everyday obstacles. The phrase "This happened and it is bad" is actually two
impressions: "This happened" is objective; "it is bad" is subjective. Miyamoto
Musashi distinguishes the observing eye (strong — sees events clear of
distraction, exaggeration, and misperception) from the perceiving eye (weak —
adds its own baggage, sees "insurmountable obstacles" instead of what is
actually there). Many problems come from applying judgments to things we don't
control, as though there were a way they were "supposed to be" — seeing what
we think should be there instead of what actually is.

Stoic "Contemptuous Expressions" strip ornamentation to see things plainly:
Epictetus told students to picture an impressive thinker they're quoting having
sex — deflating anyone who intimidates or makes you feel insecure. Marcus
Aurelius described glamorous things without euphemism — roasted meat is a dead
animal, vintage wine is old fermented grapes. The same technique applies to a
coveted promotion or an intimidating critic. Objectivity means removing "you" —
the subjective part — from the equation. We are naturally objective about other
people's problems but selfishly reserve pity and self-persecution for our own
lives. Exercise: take your own situation and pretend it isn't happening to you,
that it doesn't matter — you'll size up the scenario and its options faster and
more dispassionately. The more you practice, the more perception works for you
rather than against you, connecting [judgments, not events, disturb us](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md)
to a repeatable drill rather than a one-time insight.
