title: Meditation Vision and Personal Values
description: >
  The claim that sustaining meditation requires a vision truly your own —
  deep, tenacious, close to who you believe yourself to be — renewed daily so
  practice embodies what you value rather than becoming mechanical habit.
sources:
  - title: "Wherever You Go, There You Are: Mindfulness Meditation in Everyday Life"
    resource: "Jon Kabat-Zinn, Part 3"
---

Committing to daily meditation practice without some view of why you are doing
it and its value is virtually impossible, and senseless anyway. In traditional
societies this vision was culturally supplied; Western mainstream culture
offers precious little support for choosing such a personal, disciplined path —
effort but non-doing, energy but no tangible product. Superficial or romantic
notions of becoming calmer, clearer, or more compassionate do not survive
contact with the turbulence of daily life or the prospect of rising early in
the cold and dark.

A sustaining vision must be truly your own — deep, tenacious, close to the core
of who you believe yourself to be, what you value, where you see yourself
going. Only such a vision, and the motivation from it, can keep one on the path
year in and year out, willing to practice daily and bring mindfulness to
whatever happens. Meditation practice is hardly romantic — the ways we need to
grow are usually the ones we are most defended against and least willing to
admit exist. The vision has to be renewed daily, kept right out front all the
time, because mindfulness itself requires this level of awareness of purpose
and intention. The practice becomes the daily embodiment of your vision,
containing what you value most — not about trying to be different from how you
are but about bearing in mind what is most important so it is not lost or
betrayed in the heat and reactivity of a particular moment.

Vision concerns values and personal blueprint for what is most important in
life — first principles: do you manifest love, compassion, [ahimsa
(non-harming)](ahimsa-non-harming.md), kindness, wisdom, generosity, calmness,
solitude, non-doing, even-handedness and clarity, or just talk about them? This
level of intentionality keeps practice vital rather than a mechanical exercise
driven only by habit or belief. A Chinese inscription cited by Thoreau in
*Walden*: "Renew thyself completely each day; do it again, and again, and
forever again."

Worked example — anger: conscious expression of anger has real value, but
venting it uncontrollably as habit clouds the mind and intrinsically warps what
is, whether you are in the right or not. Mindfulness reveals the toxicity of
anger: there is something inadequate about anger, even on high ground — its
innate toxicity taints all it touches. If anger's energy is transmuted to
forcefulness and wisdom without self-absorption or self-righteousness, its power
to transform multiplies. Practice: expand the context of anger right as it is
arising and peaking, knowing something larger is being forgotten — touch an
awareness not attached to the anger-fire. Awareness sees the anger, knows its
depth, and is larger than the anger; it can hold anger the way a pot contains
food, helping cook and digest it, shifting from automatic reacting to conscious
responding.
