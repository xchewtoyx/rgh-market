Stoic writers offer several distinct arguments for why death, specifically,
does not deserve the terror it usually gets — arguments about the nature of
death itself, separate from the practical discipline of
[keeping death in daily view](memento-mori.md).

The most frequently repeated argument is that death is simply nonexistence,
and nonexistence cannot be experienced as bad because there is no subject
left to experience anything. Seneca puts this as a matter of something
already familiar: "Death is just nonexistence. I know already what that is
like: what will exist after me is the same as existed before me... What
difference is there between ending and simply not beginning? Both have the
same result: nonexistence" (Letter 54). He extends this into a symmetry
argument — since no one regrets the eons of nonexistence before their birth,
it is inconsistent to dread the nonexistence that follows death (Letter 54,
elaborated at Letter 99). Marcus Aurelius reasons similarly that death is
either dreamless dissolution or reabsorption, and that fearing the
"nothingness" side of that fork makes no more sense than fearing sleep
(*Meditations* 8.58).

A second argument treats death as simply one more natural process, no more
worthy of special dread than birth, digestion, or aging: "death should be
under the eyes of the young as well as the old, for we are not summoned
according to the census" (Seneca, Letter 12); death is "a process of
nature, which only children can be afraid of... not only a process of
nature but a necessary one" (Marcus, *Meditations* 2.12). Since [judgments,
not events, are what disturb us](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md), the
Stoic diagnosis is that fear of death is really fear of the *thought* of
death, magnified by imagination, rather than a response to death itself —
which is why Seneca can say "if death is ever to be feared, it is to be
feared at all times. For what time is there that is not subject to death?"
(Letter 30) and conclude that constant, deliberate exposure to the thought
is the antidote rather than the disease.

A third, related move treats death as reabsorption into a larger natural
order rather than annihilation of something separate: "the body and its
parts are a river, the soul a dream and mist"; accepting death as "the
dissolution of the elements... a natural thing. And nothing natural is
evil" (Marcus, *Meditations* 2.17).

A strikingly convergent version of the first argument appears outside the
Stoic corpus in Mo Gawdat's *Solve for Happy*, written after the sudden
death of his son: he distinguishes the *process* of dying (which can
include pain) from the *state* of death (which, having no experiencer, "will
never hurt you"), quoting Woody Allen's line that he isn't afraid of dying,
he just doesn't want to be there when it happens — arriving, from grief
rather than doctrine, at essentially Seneca's nonexistence argument. The
convergence is a useful check on how much of this reasoning is
tradition-specific rhetoric and how much is a durable argument that keeps
getting rediscovered.

None of these arguments claims that grief for others' deaths is
inappropriate — see
[the Stoic approach to consoling grief](consolation-for-grief.md) — only
that dread of one's own future death, specifically, does not survive
examination.
