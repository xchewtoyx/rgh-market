title: Preferred and Dispreferred Indifferents
description: >
  The Stoic classification of externals like health, wealth, and reputation
  as neither good nor bad in themselves, but as reasonably preferred or
  avoided while never being the basis of a good life.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), esp. Letters 66, 74, 87, 92"
  - title: "Discourses, Fragments, Handbook"
    resource: "Epictetus, Discourses, Fragments, Handbook (tr. Robin Hard), 2.5, 3.20"
---

Stoic ethics rests on a sharp division of everything that can happen to a
person into three categories: the good (virtue and virtuous action, always
and only), the bad (vice and vicious action), and the vast remaining middle
ground of "indifferents" — health, wealth, reputation, office, beauty,
strength, even life and death themselves. None of these middle things is
part of what makes a life good or bad, because none of them is guaranteed to
benefit whoever has it (a fool's wealth or a tyrant's health does him no
good); only virtue benefits its possessor unconditionally.

Calling these things "indifferent" does not mean being careless about them.
Within the indifferent class, some things accord with nature and are
reasonably to be selected — health over illness, wealth over poverty, a
friend's affection over enmity — and these are "preferred indifferents";
others are reasonably avoided and are "dispreferred." A [Stoic](dichotomy-of-control.md)
is entitled, even expected, to pursue preferred indifferents skillfully,
the way a good archer aims at the target — but the target itself is not
what makes the shot good. What makes the shot good is the quality of aim,
i.e. the virtue in the choosing. Epictetus puts the operational form of the
doctrine most compactly: "Materials are indifferent, but the use that one
makes of them is by no means indifferent" (Discourses 2.5.1). A skilled
dice-player is unconcerned about the throw itself but skillful and attentive
in using whatever falls.

Seneca develops the same point at length against the more moderate
Peripatetic view that externals are a lesser *component* of the good
life alongside virtue. He argues that admitting even "a tiny amount" of
weight to externals corrupts the whole structure of the good, since it makes
happiness hostage to chance: "he who regards other things as good is
dominated by fortune and subject to another's whim; he who limits goodness
to what is honorable is happy within himself" (Letter 74). He offers a
market analogy for keeping this straight in daily life: treat every apparent
good the way you would treat a vendor's asking price — before wanting
something, ask what it actually costs in anxiety, danger, or lost freedom,
"often the price is very high even if you get it for nothing" (Letter 42).

The doctrine explains why Stoic writers can simultaneously urge readers to
work for health, career success, and comfortable circumstances, and insist
that none of it matters to the only thing that really counts. It is the
premise behind treating loss of any external — money, position, even a
child or friend — as painful but not as evidence that life has gone wrong
at the level that matters, which is worked out further in
[the idea that everything we hold is only ever on loan](everything-is-on-loan.md)
and in the case for [virtue as the sole good](virtue-as-the-sole-good.md).
