Seneca repeatedly names inconsistency, not any single dramatic vice, as the
clearest symptom that a person's philosophy has not actually taken hold.
Letter 20 urges testing philosophical progress "not by speech or writing but
by strength of mind and by the lessening of your desires. Prove your words
through your actions." He offers a compact definition of wisdom in these
terms: "always wanting the same thing, always rejecting the same thing" —
with the implicit rider that the wanting must be for what is genuinely
right, since only for the right can a person consistently wish. The
diagnostic he recommends is a simple self-check for mismatched frugality:
are you generous with yourself but stingy with your family, frugal at
dinner but extravagant on your building projects? Any such mismatch reveals
that the underlying commitment is not settled.

Letter 34 develops the same idea through the sufficiency of willingness:
"when one is willing to become good, goodness is in large part achieved,"
because willingness, unlike an external undertaking, is entirely
mind-dependent. His working definition of the fully good person is framed
around resistance to disruption: "one who is complete; one who has been
perfected; one who would not be made to do wrong by any force, any
stricture." The test, again, is consistency: "if the actions are
inconsistent, the mind has not been set to rights."

Letter 120 supplies the fullest positive picture of what consistency
actually looks like, illustrated through historical exemplars. Horatius
Cocles, holding a bridge alone against the enemy, is praised not for a
single dramatic act but because the same underlying strength governed both
his stand and his careful, undramatic swim to safety afterward — "as
heedful of his arms as of the safety of his person." Seneca notes that
some vices closely mimic virtues on the surface (a spendthrift can look
generous, carelessness can look like good nature, recklessness like
courage) and argues that genuine virtue is distinguished from its
imitations precisely by holding steady across contexts, unlike the person
"brave in war but timorous in the forum." He illustrates the failure mode
with a satirical portrait of an erratic character — sometimes keeping two
hundred slaves, sometimes ten; lecturing on frugality one week, squandering
a fortune the next — concluding that "truth is stable and consistent; false
things do not last," and that only the wise person manages to act as one
person throughout an entire life rather than shifting through "many
different forms."

Posidonius told Pompey the Great, already twice consul and conqueror, to "be
the best and always superior to others" — meaning conquer the self, not win
more military victories: "not honors, but being honorable." Pompey missed the
point; his "insane love of glory" allied him with Caesar and set in motion the
Republic's destruction. Epaminondas, assigned the insulting post of
overseeing sewers, declared "it is not the office that brings distinction to
the man, it is the man who brings distinction to the office," and transformed
a minor post into a respected honor. Marcus Aurelius's stated aim was never
conquest or grand buildings but "perfection of character: to live your last
day, every day, without frenzy or sloth or pretense." External accomplishment
is byproduct, not goal: "conquering the world is almost easy after we have
fully conquered ourselves." True masters care little for winning, money, or
fame as such — "they are in a battle with themselves." Self-discipline "has
never been about punishment or deprivation," but about becoming the best one
is capable of becoming.

## Values clarification beyond Stoic consistency

CBT frames values as "the things in life that are most important to you:
your personal ethics, morals, philosophies, ideals, standards, and principles."
Most people enjoy life most when consistently acting in accordance with their
personal values — reporting better mood, improved self-opinion, and a sense of
well-being and of "being true to themselves." Depression, anxiety, poor
self-esteem, and other emotional problems can push interests and values to the
sidelines; recovery is framed as the time to rediscover and honor them.

Because everyone is unique, values differ between people, but people with
similar values tend to be drawn together — so examining values can enrich
social life too. A caution: beware unwittingly adopting other people's values
because self-doubt tells you your own views cannot be trusted. Let others
inspire you, but decide your own values yourself — a point Seneca's test of
consistency already implies when he warns against mismatched frugality that
reveals the underlying commitment is not settled.

Three reusable questions help reconnect when a problem has obscured one's own
opinions: What were my earlier interests before the problem overtook my
thinking? If I put others' opinions aside, what are some of my personal
principles and mottos? What pursuits do I get passionate about? Modeling via
an admired person — listing their demonstrated values, noting shared values,
and planning concrete actions that reflect them — can help remember what
matters most without substituting their values for one's own.

Knowing values well aids daily prioritization, since priorities generally
mirror the intrinsic value system. Pressures of modern living can skew what
seems important — a work meeting seeming more important than a nephew's school
play until later regret. You cannot always act on what is fundamentally most
important without unwanted consequences, but closer scrutiny usually reveals
opportunities to honor important things rather than blindly responding to
external pressures. Priorities are expected to shift with circumstances —
temporarily prioritizing work for extra money, or carving out time for an
elderly relative's convalescence — but such shifts are usually temporary and
should be reshuffled back once the precipitating crisis has passed.

Others' thoughts and views are important but not more important than your own;
self-image need not rest solely on how others seem to view you. You can reject
others' judgments entirely, partly, or accept them if accurate — "at the end of
the day, you know yourself better than anyone else does." This theme runs alongside the closely related claim that a good friend and
a person of settled character are recognizable by the same test — see
[Stoic friendship](stoic-friendship.md), where changed intention "shows
that the mind is at sea" (Letter 35) — and it supplies part of the
practical content behind the broader claim that
[philosophy is meant to be a daily practice, not merely a body of theory](philosophy-as-therapy-not-theory.md).
