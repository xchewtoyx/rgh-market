---
type: concept
title: Courage to Be Yourself
description: >
  The ethical claim that conforming out of fear — muting one's originality to
  avoid standing out — is a form of cowardice, and that integrity requires
  being oneself even when difference makes one a target.
sources:
  - title: "Courage Is Calling: Fortune Favors the Brave"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 6"
---

Frank Serpico was the odd man out in the 1960s NYPD: Italian among mostly Irish
cops, long hair, opera and ballet, lived in the Village, a pet sheepdog,
unconventional clothes, elaborate homemade street disguises — passed over
repeatedly for promotion because of it. A prosecutor complained he was
"difficult"; Serpico's reply: if he'd been less difficult and more inclined to
blend in, there wouldn't have been a corruption case at all. Each of us is by
definition original — unique DNA, unique experience — yet we push that heritage
away, choose to go along, and "mute ourselves" to avoid raising eyebrows. In the
NYPD it was easier to take bribes than to stay clean, because standing up meant
standing out, becoming a target, being different and thus alone.

Out of fear we conform, and we resent others' self-possession too — "difficult,"
"odd," "loose cannon," "troublemaker" are labels that litter dossiers compiled
by Hoover, the KGB, or the Gestapo: what cowards call the brave who threaten
their illegitimate power, and what we ourselves mutter when shamed by others'
freedom to be themselves. There is a real tension: we want people on the same
team, following instructions and dress codes, yet we also expect free thought,
invention, and extraordinary courage to flourish under pressure toward
conformity — which Holiday treats as basically impossible. Psalm 27: "Though an
army besiege me, my heart will not fear; though war break out against me, even
then will I be confident" — you have to be you regardless of who or how many
oppose you. Florence Nightingale criticized women who tried to "be like men" —
her point being not to apishly imitate or instinctually reject others, just be
yourself.

Seneca balances this: we don't need to buck the crowd on every small thing —
"petulant rebellion can be its own kind of defense mechanism" — but if outwardly
we look like everyone else, we'd better be sure that inwardly we are truly who
we want to be. The courage to be different is the courage to think differently,
to see and hear what others don't — no coincidence that so many whistleblowers
and artists were "weirdos," since their weirdness let them perceive what
everyone else missed. Whatever role one occupies, the imperative is to remain
yourself underneath and not let fear silence or suppress that. "Be original. Be
yourself. To be anything else is to be a coward. Don't let the opinion of
cowards influence what you think or do." This pairs with [parrhesia (speaking
truth to power)](parrhesia-speaking-truth-to-power.md) when integrity requires
public dissent, and with [living consistently with your values](living-consistently-with-your-values.md)
as the inward test behind outward difference.
