title: Virtue as the Sole Good
description: >
  The core Stoic thesis that virtue alone is good, is sufficient by itself
  for a happy life, and cannot be increased or diminished by circumstance.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), esp. Letters 71, 76, 85, 92"
  - title: "Discourses, Fragments, Handbook"
    resource: "Epictetus, Discourses, Fragments, Handbook (tr. Robin Hard), 1.29, 2.19"
---

The strict Stoic position, defended at length in Seneca's letters and
assumed throughout Epictetus, is not merely that virtue is the most
important good but that it is the *only* good: "the highest good is that
which is honorable... the sole good is that which is honorable; other
supposed goods are counterfeit coin" (Letter 71). Everything else people
call good — health, wealth, reputation, even the safety of one's children —
belongs to the category of [preferred indifferents](preferred-indifferents.md),
reasonably wanted but never part of what makes a life actually go well.

Seneca's argument for this runs from the nature of a human being: each kind
of thing has its own distinctive excellence — fertility for a vine, speed
for a stag — and a human's distinctive excellence is reason, since reason
is the one property that separates humans from animals. "What is special
about a human being? Reason. When that is set straight and made complete,
it achieves the blissful fulfillment of human nature. This perfected reason
is called virtue, and is also the same as the honorable" (Letter 76). Since
only reason perfects and completes a human being, only reason can be the
thing whose presence or absence determines whether a human life has gone
well — which is why the criterion for genuine praise or blame is character
alone: a wealthy, healthy, well-regarded person of bad character is
condemned outright, and a poor, sick, disgraced person of good character is
approved outright.

A consequence many readers find hardest to accept is that virtue does not
admit of degrees: "just as truth does not increase, virtue does not
increase either" (Letter 71). A single honorable act and a lifetime of them
are equally, not more or less, honorable, the way a straight line is not
"straighter" for being longer. This is why Seneca can insist that Cato's
courage in defeat is exactly as great as Cato's courage would have been in
victory (Letter 71), and why an hour of virtuous action and eighty years of
it are, as far as the good is concerned, the same size. The corollary is
that the wise person's happiness cannot be added to by good fortune or
subtracted from by bad fortune, only obscured from view the way clouds
obscure but do not dim the sun (Letter 92).

This thesis is what licenses the more startling Stoic claims that appear
throughout the letters and discourses — that a beggar and an emperor, if
equally virtuous, are equally happy; that dying well is not worse than
living long; and that no external loss, however severe, can touch what
actually matters. It sits behind the practice of treating
[adversity as the material virtue works on rather than as harm](adversity-as-training-ground.md),
and it is the reason the Stoics argue for
[eliminating rather than merely moderating the passions](extirpation-of-the-passions.md) —
since if externals are not really goods or evils, the passions that respond
to gains and losses of externals as if they were are always, at bottom,
mistakes of judgment.
