title: Patience as Discipline
description: >
  The virtue of waiting well — distinct from hustle — that prevents premature
  action, lets creation and judgment mature, and makes the fruit of effort
  sweeter because it was not snatched too early.
sources:
  - title: "Discipline Is Destiny: The Power of Self-Control"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 8"
  - title: "Wherever You Go, There You Are: Mindfulness Meditation in Everyday Life"
    resource: "Jon Kabat-Zinn, Part 2"
---

Aristotle: "Patience is bitter, but its fruit is sweet." Patience is a
discipline distinct from — and possibly harder than — hustle and hard work:
"the waiting is the hardest part." It requires waiting for news, for the right
opportunity, for things to settle, for a solution to come, for people to come
around, for checking one's assumptions, for reconsidering. Through patience "we
come to possess... our souls" — it prevents acting on insufficient information,
picking the wrong option, forcing outcomes, rushing or giving up on people, and
reaching the wrong conclusion.

Joyce Carol Oates is prolific, but "prolific cannot be a euphemism for sloppy."
Her mental discipline moderates her drive to create: every manuscript, after the
first draft, goes in a drawer for a year or more to gestate while she works on
other projects, reads, researches, and lives — not because the drafts are bad,
but because "we must always doubt our first burst of excitement and indeed,
anything that comes easily." Most resulting edits are minor, but the
precautionary process is treated as essential to any meaningful act of creation.
Lincoln repeatedly set aside the Emancipation Proclamation while drafting it,
waiting for the right political and military moment, "touching it up here and
there, anxiously watching the progress of events" — like a painter revisiting
sketches.

Edison exemplifies patience as "a primary ingredient of genius" — not just
flashes of inspiration, but the patient commitment to test repeatedly, set an
experiment aside until better materials were available, and navigate years of
politics to actually deliver underground electricity to New York City after
inventing the light bulb. It is "impossible for an impatient person to work with
others" or to avoid errors of judgment and timing, since "almost everything that
matters takes longer than it should." Leonardo da Vinci's analogy: patience is
clothing against cold — "as the cold increases," one must "grow in patience
when you meet with great wrongs, and they will then be powerless to vex your
mind."

The demand extends beyond day-to-day waiting to "long patience" — "Shackleton
level patience" — to let a manuscript gestate, let compounding interest work,
let an idea ahead of its time be vindicated. If things went exactly as we
wanted, if it did not demand discomfort, sacrifice, and patient endurance, then
no discipline would be required, and everyone would do it — then the fruit
would not just be less sweet; someone would have already eaten it. Patience
connects [temperance as self-mastery](temperance-as-self-mastery.md) — restraint
before acting — to [stages of moral progress](stages-of-moral-progress.md),
which licenses patience with one's own incomplete development without licensing
complacency.

Kabat-Zinn frames patience as a fundamental ethical attitude supporting
mindfulness — "obedience to the unenforceable," done for inner reasons, not
because someone is keeping score. If you are not trying to get anywhere else in
the moment, patience takes care of itself; things unfold in their own time
("The seasons cannot be hurried. Spring comes, the grass grows by itself").
Scratch impatience and anger often lies beneath — not wanting things as they
are. From patience's perspective, nothing has an absolute root cause: if hit
with a stick, anger targets the person, not the stick or the arm — but looking
deeper, even the person "literally doesn't know what he is doing" in that
moment. The Dalai Lama, asked about lack of anger toward Chinese policy in
Tibet: "They have taken everything from us; should I let them take my mind as
well?" — inner cultivation of compassion that does not surrender clarity. One
can hurry patiently, mindfully, when hurry is chosen.
