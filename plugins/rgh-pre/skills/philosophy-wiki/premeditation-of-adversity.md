The Stoics called this exercise *premeditatio malorum* — "premeditation of
evils." Before traveling, giving a speech, or enacting policy, Seneca would
rehearse the plan, then mentally go over what could go wrong: storm, ill
captain, pirates, a booing audience, delays. His test for a wise person:
"Nothing happens to the wise man against his expectation... Nor do all
things turn out for him as he wished but as he reckoned — and above all he
reckoned that something could block his plans." A leader who says "I did not
think that would happen" has failed a basic duty: if it can happen, it will —
the task is being ready when it happens, "fitted... for defeat or victory."

Modern business has rediscovered the same move under the name *premortem*:
before a project starts, imagine it has already failed spectacularly and ask
what went wrong — probing weaknesses so failures can be perceived, addressed,
or endured in advance. The Delphic Oracle's inscription — "Offer a guarantee
and disaster threatens" — counsels humility and preparation: every plan must
make concessions to reality, since other people will make mistakes and
disrupt plans. The only guarantee is that things will go wrong; the only
variable we control completely is ourselves. Being called a pessimist does not
matter — better to seem a downer than be blindsided. Anticipation is like
training on hills or at altitude: it does not make the race easier, but
prepares you for how hard it actually is.

The worst outcome is not something going wrong but something going wrong and
catching you by surprise — unexpected failure is discouraging in a way
rehearsed failure is not. Someone who has mentally run through what could go
wrong will not be as discouraged, will not shirk the task, and will not err
in the face of it. Building expectations up in the imagination only sets up
disappointment; "chimeras are like bandages — they hurt only when torn away."
A pleasant surprise beats an unpleasant one. When nothing can be done about a
feared outcome, the Stoic practice is to manage expectations: "It will suck
but we'll be okay. We'll endure it. We won't be broken by it." With
anticipation, a person can endure — prepared for failure and ready for
success.

A distinct Stoic technique from simply accepting hardship after it arrives
is deliberately imagining it in advance, in enough detail that it loses its
power to blindside. Seneca describes the underlying mechanism: fear is worse
than the thing feared because "we do not demand evidence of the things that
frighten us... we quail, and take to our heels, like the army that breaks
camp because of a dust cloud kicked up by a herd of cattle" (Letter 13).
His remedy, responding to a friend's anxiety about a pending lawsuit, is not
to reassure him with optimistic possibilities but to have him mentally
rehearse the feared outcome as though it will definitely happen and measure
it honestly: "you will soon realize that what you fear is either no great
matter or not long lasting" (Letter 24).

He generalizes this into standing advice: "we should let nothing catch us
unprepared. We should try to anticipate everything and reflect on what's
possible rather than what usually happens," because catastrophe strikes
fastest exactly where it is least expected — "it takes time for things to
grow but little or no time for them to be lost" (Letter 91, written to
console a friend after his city burned down overnight). Applied to smaller,
everyday setbacks — a slave running off, a delayed meal, an insult — the
same logic holds: "everyone has greater fortitude in arriving at a
situation for which he has long prepared himself... constant reflection
will ensure that you do not face any trouble as a raw recruit" (Letter 107).

Epictetus builds the same rehearsal into ordinary rituals rather than
reserving it for major crises: before going to the baths, mentally preview
the friction you are likely to encounter (splashing, jostling, petty theft)
and add the explicit qualification, "I want to take a bath and ensure at
the same time that my choice remains in harmony with nature" (Handbook 3).
The point of the qualification is not pessimism but insurance: expecting
friction in advance means it cannot then also disturb your settled
[judgment](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md) when it shows up.

Premeditating adversity is closely related to, but distinct from,
[memento mori](memento-mori.md): where memento mori keeps one particular
certainty — death — constantly in view to sharpen present attention,
premeditation of adversity is a general-purpose exercise applied to any
foreseeable hardship, small or large, as training for
[treating adversity as material to work with rather than as disaster](adversity-as-training-ground.md).

Undefined fear is what defeats us: what we fear, we often do not actually
know — it looms large but indistinct, in the future or churning vaguely. We
fear something bad happening, things not working out, consequences, what
people think — but can't answer what, where, when, how, who, because we
haven't looked. Fears are "shadows, illusions, refractions" picked up
secondhand rather than examined. Tim Ferriss's modern "fear setting" —
defining and articulating the nightmares, anxieties, and doubts that hold us
back — is the same exercise Seneca named: "Exile, war, torture,
shipwreck... all the terms of the human condition could be on our minds" —
not held in fear, but in familiarity. How likely? What might cause it? How
are we prepared to handle it? Unexpected blows land heaviest; expecting and
defining what can happen makes it less scary and less dangerous at once.

John D. Rockefeller asked himself "Suppose the oil fields gave out!?" to stay
un-complacent, which let him act boldly during nineteenth-century financial
panics. Napoleon believed a commander should ask several times a day, "What
if the enemy were to appear now to my front, or on my right, or on my left?"
— not anxiety but preparedness. The two-thousand-year-old military maxim:
the only inexcusable offense for an officer is to be surprised — "I didn't
think that would happen" is never an excuse. Douglas MacArthur summed up
failures of war and life in two words: "Too late" — too late preparing, too
late grasping intentions, too late securing allies. Aristotle: the optimistic
are most vulnerable, because "when the result does not turn out as expected,
they run away" — better pessimistic and prepared. Foresee the worst to perform
the best.

Walk everyday fears through actual worst-case outcomes and note they're often
no worse than risks already accepted (losing a job you were unhappy in; dying,
which you risk crossing a street). "Nothing human should be foreign to us.
Nothing possible should be alien." When fear is defined, it can be defeated.
When downside is articulated, it can be weighed against upside. When the
wolves are counted, there are fewer of them. Mountains turn out to be
molehills; vague fear alone is sufficient to deter us — the more it's
explored, the less power it holds. See [testing impressions before
assent](testing-impressions-before-assent.md) and [involuntary scare versus
chosen fear](involuntary-scare-versus-chosen-fear.md) for the perceptual side
of the same deflation.
