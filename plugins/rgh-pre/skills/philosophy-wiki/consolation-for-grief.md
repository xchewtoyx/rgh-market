---
type: concept
title: The Stoic Approach to Grief
description: >
  Seneca's position on mourning: real, involuntary tears are permitted and
  even healthy, but sustained, self-indulgent, or performative grief is a
  choice that should be corrected, not honored as devotion.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), Letters 63, 99"
---

Seneca's letters on the death of a friend (63) and the death of a child
(99, quoting an earlier consolation letter to his friend Marullus) work out
a middle position between two extremes he rejects: demanding total
emotional suppression, and treating unlimited, sustained grief as a mark of
love. "Not grieve at all? That I will not venture to ask of you, though I
know it would be better... [but] you should cry, but not wail" (Letter
63). He notes that even Homer allows Niobe, grieving all her children, only
one lawful day of mourning before she must eat again.

He is sharp about the difference between involuntary and cultivated grief.
The initial shock of loss produces real, physical, non-culpable tears —
"tears fall even when we try to suppress them, and shedding them is a
relief to the mind" — and these are fully legitimate: "that is
inhumanity, not virtue" if a person is asked to suppress them outright
(Letter 99). What deserves correction is not the first reaction but the
deliberate choice to keep reopening the wound afterward: "let them fall,
but not summon them up." He diagnoses much visible, prolonged mourning as
at least partly performative — "we are trying by our tears to prove our
sense of loss: it is not that grief forces us but that we are exhibiting
grief to others... even in grief there is competition" — and notes that
grief which continues once no one is watching typically resolves quickly,
which he takes as evidence of how much of it was for an audience: "the
sobs are louder when someone is looking... without a spectator, grief comes
to an end."

His central positive technique is to make the memory of the dead pleasant
rather than painful, quoting the philosopher Attalus approvingly: "the
memory of friends who have died gives a pleasure like that of apples that
are both tart and sweet, or like the pleasing acidity of an old wine"
(Letter 63). His own variant is one of the collection's most quoted lines:
"when I had friends, I had them as one who would lose them; now that I have
lost them, I am as one who still has them" — anticipated loss while alive
converts, after death, into ongoing possession in memory. This rests on the
same reframe developed in
[everything we hold being only ever on loan](everything-is-on-loan.md): "we
can be robbed of 'having,' but never of 'having had'" (Letter 99).

He explicitly and sharply rejects one further move some Epicureans made —
treating grief itself as containing "a pleasure akin to grief that one
ought to go hunting for," which he calls more shameful than even the
harshest austerity, since deliberately savoring one's own pain is a
different thing from letting a fond memory become sweet over time (Letter
99). The overall aim is proportion, not stoicism in the popular sense of
numbness: "one can be tranquil and composed even in the midst of tears"
(Letter 99) — recommending active, positive remembering (retelling fond
anecdotes) over either performative collapse or forced amnesia.
