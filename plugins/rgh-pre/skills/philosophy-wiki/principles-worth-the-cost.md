title: Principles Worth the Cost
description: >
  The Stoic and classical claim that some goods — dignity, justice, truth —
  justify real sacrifice, and that refusing to pay the price is a worse moral
  failure than the risks courage demands.

A recurring test of [courage as the form of every virtue at the testing
point](stoic-cardinal-virtues.md) is whether a person will pay a real price
for what they claim to value. John Stuart Mill's counter to comfortable
pacifism: war is ugly, but "the decayed and degraded state of moral and
patriotic feeling which thinks that nothing is worth a war, is much worse."
You have to care enough to draw a line somewhere. Epicurus's pathetic logic —
what good are principles if they cost you your life? — is answered by
exemplars who chose death over betrayal: Cato resisting Caesar, Thrasea and
Seneca opposing Nero, Socrates refusing to bribe his way out, Spartans
choosing to fight free rather than live as rich slaves.

Seneca's formula on the body: "We should cherish the body with the greatest
care... We should also be prepared, when reason, self-respect, and duty
demand the sacrifice, to deliver it even to the flames." Fear asks "But what
if...?" and tallies the cost to the self; courage accepts the bill. The
question is not pure self-sacrifice for its own sake — Hillel's balance
applies: "If I am only for me, who am I?" — but standing up for both self
and others when the moment demands it.

The price is not always ultimate. A leader stepping aside for the next
generation, a politician sacrificing position to pass necessary legislation,
an artist walking away from a lucrative patron for creative freedom, a company
voluntarily giving up billions in revenue on principle — these are lower-
stakes versions of the same structure. Cowardice thinks of itself; courage
asks "If not now, when?" and "If not me, then who?" The aim is not martyrdom
but refusing to trade [living consistently with your values](living-consistently-with-your-values.md)
for comfort when [virtue under public power](virtue-under-public-power.md)
or private conscience requires the harder bill to be paid.
