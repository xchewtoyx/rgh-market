Epicurus is popularly remembered as a hedonist because his garden promised
"pleasure," but his actual teaching was ascetic: pleasure means "the absence
of pain in the body and of trouble in the mind" — not "an unbroken succession
of drinking-bouts and merrymaking," but sober reasoning that searches out the
motives of every choice and banishes beliefs that disturb the soul. He once
asked a rich friend for nothing more than "a small pot of cheese" — the mark,
Diogenes Laërtius says, of the man "who laid down that pleasure was the end
of life." The person satisfied by a small pot of cheese is better able to
find good in every situation. Excess is not refrained from because it is a
sin but because it produces "a hellish existence right here while we're
alive — a hell of our own making." Nobody enslaved to appetites is free;
Musonius Rufus held that "by the standard of pleasure, nothing is more
pleasant than self-control."

Seneca draws a careful terminological and substantive line between
"pleasure," in strict Stoic usage a fault because it depends on and tracks
external circumstance, and "joy," the sage's proper and stable positive
state, which arises entirely from awareness of one's own good character:
"pleasure is a fault" in strict usage, while "joy" is "the elevation of a
mind toward goods that are real and its own" (Letter 59). He concedes he
uses "pleasure" loosely in everyday speech, the way anyone does, but
insists on the distinction when precision matters.

Letter 23 states the foundational move: "don't rejoice in empty things" —
real prosperity is "finding prosperity in that which no one else can
control." Even hope for an all-but-certain good produces anxiety, because
it is still oriented outside the self. The positive instruction is to
relocate the source of gladness inward: "I want it to be born in your own
home... Other delights do not fill the heart; they are trivial feelings
that merely smooth the brow." He is explicit that this is not asceticism —
the joy available to someone who "despises death, opens his home to
poverty, reins in pleasure" is not thin or grim but "a serious matter,"
deep rather than showy, using the image of shallow versus deep mines:
"shallow mines yield but a little; the most precious lodes are hidden
deep." Any joy "imported from elsewhere" — from externals — necessarily
"lacks foundation," because it stands or falls with something outside the
person's own [choice](dichotomy-of-control.md).

Letter 27 makes the same point through the satirical case of Calvisius
Sabinus, a wealthy but ignorant man who bought educated slaves to memorize
poetry on his behalf so he could seem learned at dinner — the joke being
that excellence of mind, unlike wine or slaves, "cannot be borrowed or
bought." Genuine joy, unlike bought status, is something no one else can
supply or remove on your behalf.

Letter 59's positive definition supplies the diagnostic test: "the wise
person is filled with joy, cheerful and calm, unalarmed; he lives on equal
terms with gods... if your mental state is even and consistent night and
day... then you have indeed attained the fullness of the human good."
Seeking pleasure "amid riches and accolades," by contrast, is really
"seek[ing] joy in the midst of anxiety," since pleasure of that kind is
inherently unstable and costly — "like drunkenness, that pays for a single
hour's cheery insanity with a long-lasting hangover." Letter 72 adds the
crucial durability clause: the still-progressing person's cheerfulness "is
still subject to interruption," but the wise person's joy is "seamless" —
"his happiness lives at home. If it came into the mind from elsewhere, it
would also depart — but it is born there."

This account only makes sense against the wider claim that
[virtue is the sole good](virtue-as-the-sole-good.md): if virtue is the one
thing whose value cannot fluctuate with fortune, then joy grounded purely
in virtue is the one positive state that similarly cannot fluctuate — unlike
pleasure, tied to [preferred indifferents](preferred-indifferents.md), which
always can.

James Clear, drawing on Caed Budris and Viktor Frankl, offers a parallel
formulation in habit psychology: happiness is not the achievement of pleasure
(that is joy or satisfaction) but the absence of desire — the state you enter
when you no longer want to change your state. It is fleeting because a new
desire always follows. Frankl: happiness cannot be pursued; it must ensue.
Desire is pursued; pleasure ensues from action. Suffering, correspondingly, is
the space between craving a change in state and getting it. Seneca's "Being
poor is not having too little, it is wanting more" supplies the same arithmetic:
when wanting outpaces liking, dissatisfaction is constant. Peace occurs when
you observe a cue without craving to act on it — observation without craving is
the realization that nothing needs fixing.
