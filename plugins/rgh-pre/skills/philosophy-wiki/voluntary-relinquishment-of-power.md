---
type: concept
title: Voluntary Relinquishment of Power
description: >
  The classical and Stoic ideal that true temperance and greatness show in
  willingly surrendering or sharing power one could keep — because appetite
  and ego, not office, are what corrupt.
sources:
  - title: "Discipline Is Destiny: The Power of Self-Control"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 15"
---

George Washington, having defeated the British, resigned his military
commission rather than claim the king-like power available to him — prompting
King George III to tell Benjamin West that if Washington did that, "he will
be the greatest man in the world." Washington studied the cautionary tales of
Alexander the Great and Julius Caesar and modeled himself on Cincinnatus, the
Roman given near-unlimited power in a crisis who relinquished it after saving
the country and returned to his farm. Managing ambition is one thing;
willingly turning down or sharing power that is "supposed to corrupt
absolutely" is [temperance as self-mastery](temperance-as-self-mastery.md)
embodied — "the rarest thing in the world."

The contrast case is instructive. Dov Charney founded American Apparel on fair-
labor principles but, as success came, clung to control, refused counsel to
bring in competent operators, and — offered a graceful exit retaining stock
and salary when the board sought to remove him — chose to destroy the company
rather than let anyone else hold even slight control. Holiday's framing: power
does not just corrupt, it reveals; the person who cannot resist it is "an
addict" who does not have power — "power has them" — and cannot build
organizations with sustained success because they cannot plan succession or
empower others.

The American founders' separation of powers reflected the same insight:
concentrated power is dangerous; leadership is a burden best distributed.
Washington later served as president only reluctantly, after two popular
elections, then resigned after two terms — setting an unwritten norm of
restraint honored for 150 years before becoming the Twenty-Second Amendment.
In Rome, both Marcus Aurelius and Antoninus deferred to the popular vote for
consul rather than simply claiming it — instances of [virtue exercised under
public power](virtue-under-public-power.md) where restraint mattered more
than display.

Plato's test for the best leaders: they do not want power because they do not
need it — having conquered appetite and ego, they are "stronger, more
independent, less corruptible, calmer, kinder, more focused on what matters."
Churchill, offered a dukedom after the war, declined: "I remembered that I
must die as I have always been, Winston Churchill." Greatness, on this view,
is not what you have — title, power, wealth, control — but who you choose to
become, or remain, when you could take more than your share.
