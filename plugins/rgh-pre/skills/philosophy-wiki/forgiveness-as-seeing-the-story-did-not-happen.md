type: concept
title: Forgiveness as Seeing the Story Did Not Happen
description: >
  The claim that full forgiveness means realizing what you thought happened
  did not — that suffering comes from uninvestigated beliefs about wrongs,
  not from the events themselves, and that everyone acts from the beliefs
  they could not help holding in the moment.
sources:
  - title: "Loving What Is, Revised Edition: Four Questions That Can Change Your Life"
    resource: "Byron Katie, ch. 12"
---

The Work is "about 100 percent forgiveness." Most people want to be forgiving
"up to a point," but when their identity as "the one who was wronged" is
threatened they resist letting go — the unspoken logic: "I'm the victim here...
if I don't suffer, it lets the perpetrator off the hook." Katie has done
inquiry with people on rape, war, torture, concentration-camp internment, the
death of a child, and prolonged illness. "Nothing terrible has ever happened
except in our thinking. Reality is always good, even in situations that seem
like nightmares. The story we tell is the only nightmare we have lived." She
is literal: "The worst that can happen to you is your uninvestigated belief
system."

A woman whose brother was murdered believed she had forgiven both the murderer
and God until his parole hearing flooded her with bitterness again. Katie:
"I love how this world is set up for people who want peace of mind" — she
wouldn't let herself get away with anything less than freedom. The question:
"If the universe is always friendly... why is it a good thing that this man
may get out of prison? Why is it good for him, for you, and for the world?"
Inquiry answers must be genuine, not summoned "spiritual" compassion — "you
can't fake it. Only the real deal will set you free."

You don't blame the bed you stub your toe on in the dark — it's just an
object doing its job. "In the end it all comes down to taking responsibility
for what you're believing." Loving-kindness meditation that widens compassion
outward can soften tone but leave the story intact — sending love to "my story
of my mother, not to my mother herself... sitting in a puddle of his own
imagination." A man who believed "My mother ruined my life" — identity fused
to "I am the man whose mother ruined my life" — only after inquiry recalled a
sixth-birthday party and discovered "an entirely different woman from the one
he thought he had grown up with." Love "appeared by itself" once no story
stood between them. Katie's gloss: "forgiveness means realizing that what you
thought happened, didn't."

The regret exercise: recall an action you knew not to do but did anyway.
Given what you were believing in the moment just prior, "you couldn't have
done anything different from what you did." The sense that something else
"could have happened" is "just a thought you're having now about a then."
Realizing your thinking caused the action, and that you had no choice about
believing what you believed, is how you "find your innocence." A father who
hit his child insisted he could have held back; Katie: examine the thoughts
operating ("My son should do what I tell him," "Children should be
respectful," "The only way he'll learn is if I hit him") — "Either we believe
our stressful thoughts, or we question them. There's no other choice."

"We're all doing the best we can." No choice means everyone is innocent —
"the murderer and the rapist are as well, as were the Romans who crucified
Jesus." "They didn't know what they were doing. They couldn't help it. People
aren't people; there is only mind." "It's not what they said or did that
causes our suffering — it's what we believe about what they said or did."
"No one can hurt me — that's my job." This parallels [no one errs
willingly](no-one-errs-willingly.md) and [arguing with reality causes
suffering](arguing-with-reality-causes-suffering.md) — wrongdoing as mistaken
judgment, suffering as resistance to what is — while pushing further toward
dissolving the victim identity that blocks [living consistently with your
values](living-consistently-with-your-values.md).
