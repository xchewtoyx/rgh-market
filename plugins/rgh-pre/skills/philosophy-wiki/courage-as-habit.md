---
type: concept
title: Courage as Habit
description: >
  The virtue-ethical claim that bravery must be cultivated through repeated
  small acts of moral courage — not reserved for emergencies — or it will not
  be available when the stakes are highest.
sources:
  - title: "Courage Is Calling: Fortune Favors the Brave"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 11"
---

"We can't just hope to be brave when it counts. It has to be something we
cultivate." Courage cannot be reserved only for when it counts — it must be
practiced through repeated small acts, or it won't be available in the big
moment.

Harry Burns, Tennessee state legislator in 1920, was an ordinary 25-year-old
with no history of bold votes. He had publicly reassured political bosses that
"My vote will never hurt you," voted twice to tab ratification, and wore the
anti-suffragette red rose. On August 18, 1920, he unexpectedly cast the "aye"
that ratified the Nineteenth Amendment — facing threats of mob violence and
loss of reelection. The deciding factor was a note from his mother. Burns
"closed his eyes and leapt into the unknown," with no prior experience of that
kind of fear — "He was not steeped in courage."

John McCain, ~100 years later, cast the decisive "no" vote against his own
party's ACA repeal effort. Unlike Burns, McCain "felt far less trepidation...
He was not conflicted at all" — because "he had made a career of surprising
people... sticking with principle even when it's probably not in your best
interest." His mother had raised him from birth to do the harder thing. "He
had made courage a habit... there was pleasure in it."

Same category of act — a "few seconds of courage" vote — radically different
internal experience, because one had practiced courage and the other hadn't.
No athlete expects to hit the game-winning shot without practicing it thousands
of times. Ralph Waldo Emerson: "Always do what you are afraid to do." William
James: we want to "make our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy"; the
person "in whom nothing is habitual but indecision" is the most miserable —
worse still is "the person who has made cop-outs and cowardice their go-to
decision." The philosophical point is character formation: [living consistently
with your values](living-consistently-with-your-values.md) requires that courage
become disposition, not exception. See [stoic cardinal virtues](stoic-cardinal-virtues.md)
for how every choice between brave and afraid trains the whole character.
