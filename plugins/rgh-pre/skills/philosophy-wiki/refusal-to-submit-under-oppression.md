---
type: concept
title: Refusal to Submit Under Oppression
description: >
  The ethical claim that submission does not cure abuse or injustice — that
  resistance, even partial or costly, breaks oppression for oneself and for
  those who come after.
sources:
  - title: "Courage Is Calling: Fortune Favors the Brave"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 11"
---

"The old doctrine that submission is the best cure for outrage and wrong does
not hold good on the slave plantation." "We cannot tolerate abuse, constraints,
or injustice. We can't hide from our problems. We can only step to them."
Submission does not cure oppression — resistance, even partial or costly, is
what breaks it, both for the individual and for those who come after.

Frederick Douglass, age seventeen, fights Edward Covey, a notoriously abusive
"slave breaker." Douglass grabs Covey by the throat; when Covey calls for
reinforcements, Douglass kicks the first man hard in the chest. They fight for
two hours — Douglass "fighting savagely for his life, for his very dignity as
a human being" — until Covey lets him go. Every slave "knew that to lay a hand
on a white man meant death," which makes the act itself extreme courage
regardless of outcome. Douglass afterward: "I felt as I never felt before. It
was a glorious resurrection, from the tomb of slavery, to the heaven of freedom.
My long-crushed spirit rose, cowardice departed, bold defiance took its place;
and I now resolved that, however long I might remain a slave in form, the day
had passed forever when I could be a slave in fact."

At age eight, Douglass watched an enslaved woman named Nelly fight back against
an overseer with nails, fists, and screams — "her spirit remained unbroken" even
as she was beaten. This scene "implanted itself in the boy's memory and instilled
in Frederick Douglass a seed of courage" that bloomed in the Covey fight. His
maxim: "He is whipped oftenest who is whipped easiest, and that slave who has
the courage to stand up for himself against the overseer, although he may have
many hard stripes at the first, becomes in the end a freeman, even though he
sustain the formal relation of a slave." Personal motto: "You can kill me but
you can't whip me." He was never whipped again after the Covey fight.

MLK Jr.: "There comes a time when people get tired of being trampled over by
the iron feet of oppression" — and when we straighten our backs, "we might be
beaten — but we can't be ridden." The method may differ — Douglass's literal
physical fighting versus King's nonviolent resistance — but both express the
same underlying refusal to submit. "We must draw the line, somewhere — if not
right now, then very soon. We must demand our sovereignty." This extends
[Stoic slavery and human dignity](stoic-slavery-and-human-dignity.md) and
[defiance under coercion](defiance-under-coercion.md) into the ethics of
withholding consent under systemic oppression.
