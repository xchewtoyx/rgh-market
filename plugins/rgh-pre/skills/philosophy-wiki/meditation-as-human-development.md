title: Meditation as Human Development
description: >
  The Buddhist-influenced claim that meditation (bhavana) is development through
  mental training — a natural extension of maturing that uses fairy tales,
  shadow work, and repetitive inner labor to recover golden promise and become
  a fully developed adult rather than a childhood innocence already past.
sources:
  - title: "Wherever You Go, There You Are: Mindfulness Meditation in Everyday Life"
    resource: "Jon Kabat-Zinn, Part 3"
---

In Pali, the Buddha's original language, there is no single word for
meditation, though it evolved to an extraordinary degree in ancient Indian
culture. The word frequently used is *bhavana*, translating as development
through mental training — meditation really is about human development, a
natural extension of maturing: cutting teeth, growing an adult body, working,
raising a family, going into debt of one kind or another, and realizing you too
will grow old and die. At some point one is practically forced to sit down and
contemplate your life and question who you are and where the meaning lies.

Old fairy tales — per interpreters Bruno Bettelheim, Robert Bly, Joseph
Campbell, and Clarissa Pinkola Estés — are ancient maps offering guidance for
the development of full human beings, distilled through millennia of oral
telling. Their characters — kings, queens, princes, princesses, dwarfs, witches
— are not merely personages out there but aspects of our own psyches, strands of
our own being, groping toward fulfillment. We house the ogre and the witch, and
they have to be faced and honored or they will consume us. These stories point
toward a state of harmony that lets us live happily ever after — which really
means in the timeless here and now.

A recurrent theme: a young prince or princess loses a golden ball. We each
contain both prince and princess regardless of sex or age, and each once radiated
with the golden innocence and infinite promise carried by youth — a radiance we
still carry or can recover if development is not arrested. Bly notes losing the
golden ball happens around age eight, but recovering it or even recognizing the
loss can take thirty or forty years — whereas in fairy tales it takes only a day
or two. A bargain must first be made with suppressed shadow energies,
symbolized by a frog or hairy wild man under the pond. Before that bargain, one
must know these figures exist; conversing with instinctively avoided aspects of
the psyche is a prerequisite and can be frightening.

Tibetan Buddhism developed artistic expression of terrifying psyche aspects —
grotesque demonic beings honored as members of the pantheon. These deities are
not gods in the usual sense but represent different mind states, each with its
own divine energy which has to be faced, honored, and worked with for true
development. Their wrathful appearance is a disguise adopted by deities
embodying wisdom and compassion to foster greater understanding and kindness.

Reaching the wild man under the pond requires bucketing out the pond — per Bly,
repetitive inner work over a long time, with nothing glamorous about it. This
repetitive work of coming to know one's own psyche is its own initiation, a
tempering process usually involving heat; the discipline to tolerate the heat
and persevere yields mastery and non-naivete — an inward order unattainable
without the discipline, the heat, the descent into darkness and fear. Interior
defeats serve this tempering. Jungians call this soul work.

Meditation can guide us through the maze, keeping us on the path even in the
darkest moments — a roadmap to our radiant selves, not to gold of childhood
innocence already past, but to that of a fully developed adult. But meditation
requires us to encounter darkness and despair when they arise and face them,
over and over again if need be, without running away or numbing ourselves. Pair
with [soul versus spirit](soul-versus-spirit.md), [admitting you do not
know](admitting-you-do-not-know.md), and [meditation as a way of
being](meditation-as-way-of-being.md).
