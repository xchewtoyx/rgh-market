title: Strict With Yourself, Tolerant With Others
description: >
  The Stoic and classical rule that moral rigor applies inward — holding
  others to your private standard is unfair, counterproductive, and a form
  of intemperance disguised as superiority.
sources:
  - title: "Discipline Is Destiny: The Power of Self-Control"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 14"
  - title: "Meditations"
    resource: "Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (tr. Gregory Hays)"
---

Marcus Aurelius's working rule: "Tolerant with others, strict with yourself."
The only person you get to be hard on is you — their life is not in your
control, and holding others to standards they never signed up for is both
unfair and counterproductive. Cato the Younger held himself to austere
standards — indifferent to wealth, ordinary dress, walking Rome barefoot,
sleeping on the ground with troops, never lying — yet his brother Caepio loved
luxury, perfume, and company Cato would never have kept. The Roman expression
captures the point: "We can't all be Catos." Cato the Elder's version: "I am
prepared to forgive everybody's mistakes, except my own." Ben Franklin:
"Search others for their virtues, thyself for thy vices."

The failure mode is visible in high performers who drive teammates as hard as
they drive themselves — alienating the supporting cast that could help them
win. Holiday's claim: the number one cause of angry outbursts from successful
people is others not measuring up to a standard those others never agreed to.
Counter-models of restraint: Gandhi never judged friends for less-strict
choices. Colin Powell, sleeping in his office before the Gulf War, hid it from
staff so they wouldn't feel pressure to match his sacrifice. Lincoln's
secretary reported that he "never asked perfection of anyone, he did not even
insist, for others, upon the high standards he set for himself." Marcus, from
Antoninus, treated escaping one's own faults as hard enough work for a
lifetime — no time left to nitpick others.

Cato loved his brother rather than lording over him; Marcus found things to
appreciate in his stepbrother Lucius Verus, using Verus's vices to improve
himself rather than condemning them. [Temperance as self-mastery](temperance-as-self-mastery.md)
should produce compassion and connection, not loneliness — "superiority is not
a weapon you wield on other people"; using it that way is egotism, a form of
intemperance. Each person is on their own strict path; others are on theirs,
doing their best with what they've been given. "It's not our place to judge. It
is our place to cheer them on and accept them." This pairs with
[philosophical friendship with oneself](philosophical-friendship-with-oneself.md):
strict inward standards need not become harsh outward judgment.
