---
type: concept
title: Arguing With Reality Causes Suffering
description: >
  The claim that the only time we suffer is when we believe a thought that
  argues with what is — wanting reality to differ from how it is now is
  hopeless, while ceasing to oppose what is makes action simple and effective.
sources:
  - title: "Loving What Is, Revised Edition: Four Questions That Can Change Your Life"
    resource: "Byron Katie, ch. 1, 12, 13"
---

"The only time we suffer is when we believe a thought that argues with what
is." Wanting reality to be different than it is right now is compared to
trying to teach a cat to bark — hopeless, no matter how long you try.
Everyday examples: "People should be kinder," "My neighbors should take better
care of their lawn," "The line at the grocery store should move faster." What
you think shouldn't have happened *should* have happened, "because it did
happen, and no thinking in the world can change it" — this is not condoning or
approving, just seeing without resistance. Arguing with reality "means arguing
with the story of a past. It's already over, and no thinking in the world can
change it."

The objection that accepting reality is disempowering or passive misses the
contrast between "I wish I hadn't lost my job" and "I lost my job; what
intelligent solutions can I find right now?" Seeing reality clearly does not
mean passivity — it means acting "in the kindest, most appropriate, and most
effective way," including clearly restating what you ordered when the waiter
brings braised ox tongue instead of grilled lemon sole. If you were perfectly
clear, "what you'd want is braised ox tongue, because that's what the waiter
brought" — that does not mean you have to eat it; the problem only arises when
you add a further belief ("I have to eat it," "there's not enough time to
reorder," "there's been an injustice"). Without that layer there is no
problem, though there may still be a reaction to work with.

Katie's formulation after her 1986 realization: "When I argue with reality, I
lose — but only 100 percent of the time." She is "a lover of what is... because
it hurts when I argue with reality"; opposing reality produces tension and
frustration, while not opposing it makes action "simple, fluid, kind, and
fearless." "Reality is always kind" in the sense that fighting what already is
only adds suffering on top of what is. Even war, rape, poverty, and violence
exist until they don't — believing they shouldn't exist when they do is where
suffering begins; the personal question is whether one can end the war in
oneself and stop "raping myself and others with my abusive thoughts." This
parallels the Stoic diagnosis that [it is judgments, not events, that disturb
us](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md) and the practice of [the art of
acquiescence](art-of-acquiescence.md) — accepting what cannot be changed as
prelude to effective action, not resignation.

At the deathbed, the same structure appears in stark form. A dying woman
insists her swollen cancerous leg is the problem; inquiry locates suffering
in the belief that the leg should look like the other one. Until we stop
opposing how things are — including our own dying bodies — we add story-
layer suffering on top of what is. See [fear of death as identity
fear](fear-of-death-as-identity-fear.md) for how death dread often masks
dread of losing the constructed self.
