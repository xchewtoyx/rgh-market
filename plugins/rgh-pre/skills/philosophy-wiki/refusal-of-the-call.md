title: Refusal of the Call
description: >
  The claim that meaningful vocation is almost always preceded by fear-driven
  delay — and that the defining battle of a consequential life is whether one
  eventually answers what one already knows one is called to do.
sources:
  - title: "Courage Is Calling: Fortune Favors the Brave"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 2"
---

The Hero's Journey pattern: the call to adventure is almost always followed by
refusal of the call — because it's too hard, too scary, because surely the
wrong person was picked. Florence Nightingale received her first "call" at
sixteen — a mysterious sense that something was expected of her, a summons to
service different from the "constraining and underwhelming roles available to
women in her time." For eight years it sat unaddressed while the era's real
stakes mounted: hospital mortality often exceeded outside-hospital mortality; in
the Crimean War, of ~100,000 troops, 1,800 died of wounds but more than 16,000
died of disease. Yet "the fear was greater" — the banal pressures of china,
reading to her father, finding a husband, gossip — "nothing to do, and that was
all that a woman of means was allowed to do: nothing."

At twenty-five, offered a chance to volunteer at Salisbury Hospital, she let
her mother squelch it. A second call asked whether reputation would hold her
back from service; her parents wept and raged. Samuel Gridley Howe told her:
"it would be unusual, and in England whatever is unusual is thought to be
unsuitable; but I say to you 'go forward'... Choose, go on with it, wherever
it may lead you." Fear of being unusual kept her home for years. Biographer
Cecil Woodham-Smith: "The bonds which bound her were only of straw, but she did
not break them." Nightingale later: "How very little can be done under the
spirit of fear." When she finally took the leap: "I must expect no sympathy or
help from them... I must take some things, as few as I can, to enable me to live.
I must take them, they will not be given to me."

Each of us receives our own call — to service, to risk, to challenge the status
quo, to run toward what others run from — met with pressure to suppress it.
"Fear will make itself felt. It always does." The question is whether we let it
stop us from answering, or inch closer until we're ready to do what we were put
here to do. What we're called to do "comes from somewhere beyond us; it's bigger
than us." There is nothing worth doing that is not scary. This pairs with
[refusal to surrender](refusal-to-surrender.md) once the call is answered and
[courage to be yourself](courage-to-be-yourself.md) when social convention is
what holds the bonds in place.
