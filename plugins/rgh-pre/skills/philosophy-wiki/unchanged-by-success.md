---
type: concept
title: Unchanged by Success
description: >
  The discipline of refusing to let power, position, or changed fortune
  inflate ego or buy exemption from the rules — when external checks fall
  away, self-mastery becomes the only remaining constraint.
sources:
  - title: "Discipline Is Destiny: The Power of Self-Control"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 16"
---

On the night the Berlin Wall fell, Angela Merkel had one beer and went home to
work the next day. As chancellor she kept living in the same rent-controlled
apartment for twenty-three years, sat in and paid for regular seats at the
Philharmonic, chided aides for laughing too hard at her jokes, and shopped for
her own groceries. Asked if it bothered her that people in her hometown still
called her "the pastor's daughter," she said: "That's who I am" — unchanged
regardless of what else changed. Plutarch's Cato the Elder persisted in early
austerity into old age, "even when he was old and gray headed and had served as
a consul and celebrated a triumph... like a champion athlete, to observe the
rules of his training to the end." Holiday's paradox: "Precisely when we think
we've earned the right to relax our discipline is exactly when we need it most"
— success brings more temptation, more distractions, more opportunities, and
answering that requires "even more self-mastery."

What Merkel and Cato share is refusing to use power or position to buy what
most people in their place buy: ego, or exemption from the rules. At Prince
Philip's funeral, ~95-year-old Queen Elizabeth II sat alone in St. George's
Chapel — the Royal Family had been offered more invitees but she declined,
judging it unfair to Britons who'd observed pandemic protocols throughout;
"she might have been able to escape the consequences... but not the dishonor."
Marcus Aurelius's "Lift me up and hurl me, wherever you will. My spirit will
be gracious to me there — gracious and satisfied" expresses how self-containment
produces tranquility "amid both success and adversity." Merkel admitting one
million refugees during the Syrian crisis despite political cost — acting "as a
pastor's daughter... not the politician she had become" — shows the only thing
success changed was "her ability to direct world events — and that power she
seized," not her character.

Seneca sharpens why success makes discipline harder, not easier: "People of
humble station have more leeway when it comes to using force, bringing suits,
rushing into quarrels, and indulging their anger... blows traded by equals do
little harm. But for a king, even raising his voice to use intemperate language
is at odds with his majesty." Once others will make excuses for you, [temperance
as self-mastery](temperance-as-self-mastery.md) becomes the only remaining
constraint — external checks having fallen away — and it is exactly at the
height of one's powers that a "clear mind" not "blinded by substances or a
sense of superiority" is most needed. Self-mastery "demands more of itself
while not necessarily begetting more of itself" — the rewards of success are "a
million seductive reasons (and dollars) to let up," but the aim is to prove the
victory wasn't a fluke: "You will show that success has not changed you.
Except that it has made you better." This extends [adversity as training
ground](adversity-as-training-ground.md)'s symmetric claim that abundance
brings its own temptations demanding equal virtue, and pairs with [unchanged
character under public power](virtue-under-public-power.md).
