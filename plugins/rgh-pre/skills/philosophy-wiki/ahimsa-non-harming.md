type: concept
title: Ahimsa (Non-Harming)
description: >
  The ethical orientation of living so as to cause as little damage and
  suffering as possible — to others and to oneself — tested most when fear
  tempts harm, not when no one threatens you.
sources:
  - title: "Wherever You Go, There You Are: Mindfulness Meditation in Everyday Life"
    resource: "Jon Kabat-Zinn, Part 7"
---

Ahimsa (non-harming) sits at the heart of yoga practice, the Hippocratic Oath,
and Gandhi's revolution — but its value is in the living, not in holding the
view. The practical question is simple: why not try to live so as to cause as
little damage and suffering as possible? That orientation would reduce the
violence that dominates public life and private thought, and it applies to how
you treat yourself on and off the cushion — when you put yourself down, gossip,
push past bodily limits, or cause others pain.

The test is not how you behave toward someone who does not threaten you. The
test is how you relate when you do feel threatened. Willingness to harm
ultimately comes out of fear. Non-harming therefore requires seeing your own
fears, understanding them, and owning them — taking responsibility so fear does
not completely dictate your vision. Only [mindfulness of clinging and
rejecting](pain-versus-suffering.md), and a willingness to grapple with those
states however painful the encounter, can free you from the circle of suffering
that harm perpetuates. Without daily embodiment, lofty ideals succumb to
self-interest.

Gandhi insisted ahimsa must be practiced in all departments of life or it has no
practical value — not a weekend virtue but a soul attribute for every affair.
He also counseled expanding care in concentric circles: put another's welfare
first even briefly, then widen the circle. That is [courage for
neighbors](courage-for-neighbors.md) scaled outward, and it pairs with
[nonviolent restraint under assault](nonviolent-restraint-under-assault.md)
when harm is offered to you rather than by you.
