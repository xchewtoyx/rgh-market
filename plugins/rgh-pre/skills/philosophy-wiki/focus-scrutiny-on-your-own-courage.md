type: concept
title: Focus Scrutiny on Your Own Courage
description: >
  The Stoic and classical injunction not to judge another person's fear or
  cowardice — you cannot know their wrestling — and to redirect moral scrutiny
  inward, changing others silently by example.
sources:
  - title: "Courage Is Calling: Fortune Favors the Brave"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 4"
  - title: "Discourses, Fragments, Handbook"
    resource: "Epictetus, Discourses, Fragments, Handbook (tr. Robin Hard)"
---

James Baldwin, reflecting on his father's death, realized he had only ever seen
the man's outsides — beneath failures as a parent lay an internal struggle no
other person can fully comprehend. The preacher's line struck hardest: "Thou
knowest this man's fall; but thou knowest not his wrassling." It is easy to
judge and hard to know — what another person is going through, what interrelated
risks they manage, whom and what they try to protect.

Nikita Khrushchev, denouncing Stalin before the Politburo, received an
anonymous note: "Yes, but where were you at the time?" After a pause he
replied, "I was where you are now" — in the audience, anonymous, doing nothing,
like everyone else. We do not know why someone chickened out or could not quite
get there; "It's difficult for people to understand what their salary depends
on them not understanding." People can be bold in one part of life and exhibit
extreme moral cowardice in another — compartmentalization and rationalization
are universal. The battle against [involuntary scare versus chosen fear](involuntary-scare-versus-chosen-fear.md)
is a full-time job; none of us handle it well enough to spend much time
monitoring how others are doing with theirs.

The counterfactual test is humbling: if you had lived during slavery,
imperialism, the rise of antisemitism, Soviet Russia, or Mao's China, would
you have resisted the tides of your time? Fear is the swing vote. No one can
truly occupy a different time and place — but per Khrushchev's answer, the
honest response to "what would you have done back then?" is "the same thing
you're doing today." The right question is not "what would I do in their
shoes?" but "what am I doing now?"

Epictetus — shaped by empathy cultivated across thirty years in slavery — held
that until we know someone's reasons, we do not even know that they acted
wrongly. Louis Zamperini, POW at Naoetsu: "Only the laundry knew how scared I
was." This is not a case against accountability for action or inaction; it
means we currently have plenty of our own work rather than condemnation of
"distant bums and fools." Pair with [strict with yourself, tolerant with
others](strict-with-yourself-tolerant-with-others.md): if we indict anyone for
cowardice, let it be silently, by example. "Waste not a second questioning
another man's courage. Put that scrutiny solely on your own."
