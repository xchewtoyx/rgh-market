title: The Dichotomy of Control
description: >
  The Stoic division of everything in life into what is "up to us" (our own
  judgments, desires, and choices) and what is not, as the starting point for
  a settled life.

Epictetus opens the *Handbook* with what he treats as the single most useful
distinction a person can make: some things are "up to us" (*eph' hēmin*, Greek
*ta eph'hemin*) — opinion, motivation, desire, aversion, and in general
whatever is our own doing — and some things are not up to us (*ta ouk eph'hemin*)
— body, property, reputation, office, the weather, the economy, other people's
judgments, disasters, and in general whatever is not our own doing. The Serenity
Prayer's modern formulation — accept what you cannot change, change what you
can, wisdom to know the difference — restates the same partition for a later
audience.

The practical payoff is not a metaphysical claim about free will so much as a
rule for where to invest concern. What is up to us is "our playing field" —
emotions, judgments, creativity, attitude, perspective, desires, decisions,
determination. What is not up to us sets the rules and conditions of the game;
winning athletes make the best of those conditions without arguing against
them, because there is no point. Treating something in the "not up to us"
category as if it were within your power produces lament, blame, and
disturbance whenever it goes wrong, because you have staked your peace of
mind on something you cannot actually secure. Treating what genuinely is up
to you — the use you make of your own impressions, your desires and
aversions, your choices — as the only proper domain of concern makes you, in
Epictetus's phrase, invulnerable: "no one will ever harm you because no harm
can affect you" (Handbook 1). A tyrant can chain your legs or cut off your
head, but he cannot touch your [power of choice](stoic-inner-citadel.md)
(Discourses 1.1, 1.29).

The diagnostic move in any obstacle is to ask: is there *any* chance, however
slight, that something here is up to me? Funding denied is not up to you;
improving the pitch is. Someone stealing your idea is not up to you; pivoting
or fighting for what is yours may be. To argue, complain, or give up are
themselves choices — and choices that usually fail to move you forward.
Focusing exclusively on what is in your power magnifies that power; every ounce
of energy directed at what you cannot influence is wasted. Even in prison, mind
and time remain one's own: Rubin Carter, wrongly convicted, refused to surrender
the last thing he controlled — himself — and treated the ordeal as something he
would decide how to affect. Having little power is not the same as being
powerless.

This is the master key beneath most other Stoic techniques. Once a person
sees clearly that health, wealth, reputation, other people's opinions, and
even the survival of loved ones are not up to them, the reasonable response
is neither to abandon them (they can still be reasonably preferred and
pursued) nor to treat their loss as calamity, but to hold them as
[preferred indifferents](preferred-indifferents.md) — worth pursuing with
care, never worth being made wretched over. Marcus Aurelius restates the
same rule from the inside: "Choose not to be harmed — and you won't feel
harmed. Don't feel harmed — and you haven't been" (*Meditations* 4.7),
and diagnoses most disturbance as arising precisely from misassigning what
belongs to fortune to the category of what belongs to us (6.41).

The dichotomy is also the reason Stoic writers keep returning to a short
diagnostic question in the face of any disturbing event: is this, in fact,
up to me? Epictetus turns this into a daily drill — on leaving the house
each morning, test every impression that arrives (a handsome person, a
grieving mourner, a consul) against the question "does this lie within the
sphere of choice, or outside it?" and discard concern for whatever falls
outside it (Discourses 3.3). The habit of running this test is what
[the discipline of testing impressions before assent](testing-impressions-before-assent.md)
trains, and it is the ground on which the closely related insight that
[judgments, not events, are what disturb us](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md)
rests.
