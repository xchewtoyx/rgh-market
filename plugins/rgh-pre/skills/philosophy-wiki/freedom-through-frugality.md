title: Freedom Through Frugality
description: >
  The Stoic and classical argument that stripping away the superfluous
  secures autonomy — not because poverty is good, but because neediness
  and excess both enslave.
sources:
  - title: "Discipline Is Destiny: The Power of Self-Control"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 3"
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), Letters 17, 18, 20"
---

Cato the Elder's line — "Nothing is cheap, if it is superfluous" — states the
core claim: frugality is not about liking deprivation but about refusing
dependence. Cato drank the same wine as his slaves, declined expensive gifts,
and kept political work simple so bribery had no purchase; his hero Manius
Curius, found boiling turnips by would-be bribers, showed that a person
satisfied with little "could never be tempted." A Spartan king answered that
Spartan austerity reaps freedom. Michelangelo refused patron gifts he did not
want to owe anyone for — "real wealth, he understood, was autonomy."

The disciplined life is often *easier*, not harder: less to worry about, fewer
rings to kiss, nothing for envy to target. The overextended person with debts
or a job they cannot afford to lose is "less free than the person trapped on
the treadmill moving faster and faster but going nowhere." Maxwell Perkins's
inscription captures the inversion: "The more a man is, the less he wants."
Strip away the unnecessary and excessive, and "what's left is you... what's
left is what's important."

Diagnostic questions for spotting the superfluous: How hard are others pushing
this on you? Is your interest driven by keeping up or fear of missing out?
Has humanity survived without it? How long did the last craving's pleasure
last versus the buyer's remorse? Wrongful imprisonment offers an extreme test:
Rubin Carter stripped himself of prison amenities so guards had no leverage —
"by being a little hard on ourselves, it makes it harder for others to be
hard on us."

This practice complements Seneca's [voluntary poverty as training](voluntary-poverty-as-training.md)
and the wider Stoic classification of wealth as a [preferred indifferent](preferred-indifferents.md):
the point is not to worship austerity but to ensure comfort has not become
[slavery to craving](slavery-to-passions.md). "The less you desire, the richer
you are, the freer you are, the more powerful you are" — because need, not
the specific object craved, is what binds.
