title: Experiencing Self Versus Remembering Self
description: >
  The distinction between the self that lives through moments and the self
  that keeps score by memory — and why duration-weighted well-being may
  diverge from peak-end evaluation when choosing a life.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking, Fast and Slow"
    resource: "Daniel Kahneman, ch. 37, 40"
---

Kahneman closes *Thinking, Fast and Slow* with a harder problem than it first
appeared: the possible conflict between the **remembering self** (which keeps
score and makes choices) and the **experiencing self** (which does the living).
In the cold-hand study, duration neglect plus the peak-end rule led subjects to
choose repeating the trial that left the better memory even though it involved
objectively more pain — a mistake if one favors the experiencing self. The same
biases make evaluations of an opera or of "Jen's life" indefensible: it makes
no sense to judge an entire life by its last moments or to give no weight to
duration.

The remembering self composes stories and retains them for future reference. In
*La Traviata*, Kahneman realized he did not care at all about the length of
Violetta's life (a year longer or shorter wouldn't have moved him), but the
last 10 minutes — whether the lover arrived in time — mattered enormously; nor
would the emotional weight of the reunion have changed if it had lasted a week
instead of 10 minutes. "A story is about significant events and memorable
moments, not about time passing. Duration neglect is normal in a story, and
the ending often defines its character." The same features recur in memories of
colonoscopies, vacations, and films. We want the life stories of others to end
well too, and judge this independently of anyone's felt experience — pity for a
man who died believing in his wife's love, on learning she had a lover for years
(he never experienced the humiliation, but we still pity him); humiliation felt
on a dead scientist's behalf when a discovery is posthumously disproved. "Most
important, of course, we all care intensely for the narrative of our own life
and very much want it to be a good story, with a decent hero."

Ed Diener's "Jen" studies tested whether duration neglect and the peak-end rule
govern evaluations of entire lives. In the baseline version Jen was extremely
happy throughout a life lasting either 30 or 60 years. Doubling the length of
her uniformly happy life had no effect at all on desirability or total-
happiness judgments — her life was represented by a prototypical slice of time,
not a sequence of time slices, so "total happiness" meant the happiness of a
typical period, not a sum over her lifespan. Consistent with an average
substituting for a sum, adding 5 slightly-happy years to a very happy life
substantially lowered evaluations — a less-is-more effect. Kahneman himself
doubted people would rate the longer, slightly-diminished life as worse; he was
wrong. Duration only appears to matter in labor pain and vacations because the
quality of the ending changes with the episode's length — what we track is
trajectory and how it feels at the end, not duration per se.

The remembering self is a construction of deliberate reasoning, but its
distinctive evaluation rules (duration neglect, peak-end weighting) originate
in fast intuitive judgment and do not necessarily match what people say they
value — "we believe that duration is important, but our memory tells us it is
not." Because time is the ultimate finite resource, rules governing evaluation
of the past are poor guides for decisions. Combined bias favors a short period
of intense joy over a long period of moderate happiness, and its mirror makes
people fear a short period of intense-but-tolerable suffering more than a much
longer period of moderate pain. Duration neglect also makes people accept long
mild unpleasantness if the ending improves, and give up a long happy period
likely to end poorly. The common advice "Don't do it, you will regret it"
sounds wise because anticipated regret is the remembering self's verdict — but
that perspective is not always correct.

A proposed alternative is **duration-weighted well-being**: treat all moments
alike, memorable or not — though a moment can weigh more if people spend real
time dwelling on it, or if it alters many subsequent moments (an hour of violin
practice enhancing years of listening; a brief awful event causing PTSD
weighted by the total duration of misery it causes). Whether a moment was
"memorable" or "meaningful" can only be determined after the fact — "I will
always remember this" is often a sincere but false prediction.

Duration weighting is logically compelling but not a complete theory of the
good life: people identify with their remembering self and care about their
story, so a theory ignoring what people want cannot be sustained — but neither
can one that ignores what actually happens in people's lives. Both selves must
be considered because their interests do not always coincide. This tension
sits alongside older philosophical debates about [what makes a life worth
living](virtue-as-the-sole-good.md) — virtue-centered accounts, hedonic
accounts, and narrative identity each weight duration, peaks, and meaning
differently. Policy choices — how much to invest in treating blindness,
deafness, or chronic pain — can rank severity differently depending on
whether one measures feared memory, experienced suffering, or desire for
relief. The focusing illusion and miswanting (overrating purchases whose
appeal fades while underrating activities that retain attention, like social
groups or learning an instrument) show the same split: the life one chooses
under peak-end memory is not always the life the experiencing self would have
wished to live.
