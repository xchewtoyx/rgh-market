---
type: concept
title: Pain Is Inevitable, Suffering Is Optional
description: >
  The distinction between pain — a transient, useful signal produced by an
  actual event — and suffering, the self-generated repetition and
  amplification of pain through rumination, which continues even once the
  triggering event is over.
sources:
  - title: "Solve for Happy"
    resource: "Mo Gawdat, Solve for Happy, Part 1, ch. 1"
---

Mo Gawdat's *Solve for Happy*, written in the aftermath of his son Ali's
sudden death from medical error, draws a distinction that closely parallels
older Buddhist and Stoic accounts of suffering while arriving at it through
a modern, engineering-influenced route. Physical or emotional discomfort —
hunger, a sprained ankle, grief at a real loss, exam anxiety, guilt — is
*pain*: a signal that prompts corrective action and then subsides on its
own once the underlying issue has been registered or resolved, "we
hurt — we heal." *Suffering* is what happens when pain is allowed to
linger and replay in imagination, magnified with an added layer of
self-narrative ("I'm an idiot... I deserve to be punished") that serves no
adaptive function: "Suffering offers no benefit whatsoever. None!"

The mechanism Gawdat proposes is that suffering is produced by *thought
about* an event, not by the event itself — closely related to the wider
claim, developed independently in the Stoic tradition, that
[judgments, not events, are what disturb us](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md).
His illustrative technique (the "Blank Brain Test") asks a reader to hold
an upsetting thought in mind, then deliberately replace it with something
else entirely (a puzzle, a piece of music) — and notes that the felt
unhappiness disappears the moment the thought is dropped, even though
nothing about the external situation has changed. From this he draws the
practical conclusion: "no event in your life ever has the power to make
you unhappy until you choose to grant it that power by turning it into a
thought."

Applied to his own grief, Gawdat frames the choice explicitly as one
between two options that both leave the underlying loss unchanged: suffer
indefinitely (which will not bring his son back) or feel the real pain,
decline to run suffering-amplifying thoughts on top of it, and move
forward while still honoring the loss (which likewise will not bring his
son back, but "make the world just a little bit easier to endure"). He
reports choosing the second explicitly and self-consciously as an ongoing
choice, not a one-time trick: the pain of missing his son remains real and
expected to last, but the suffering — the resentment, the sense of being
cheated, the blame — is treated as separable from it and something he
declines to generate.

The distinction is a modern, plainly-stated version of an old family of
ideas rather than a wholly new claim — it sits alongside the Buddhist image
of the "second arrow" (the arrow of reaction added on top of the first,
unavoidable arrow of pain) and the Stoic case that
[a passion, once triggered by a genuine external loss, escalates through
added judgment rather than through the loss itself](extirpation-of-the-passions.md).
Its value here is as a plain, contemporary articulation of the same
structural insight, arrived at independently through grief rather than
inherited doctrine.
