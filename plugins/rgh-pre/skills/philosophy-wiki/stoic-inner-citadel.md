A recurring Stoic image treats the mind's own [power of choice](dichotomy-of-control.md)
as a fortress that cannot be taken by any external force, because the only
thing capable of conquering it is itself. Epictetus states the underlying
claim starkly: "how is a citadel destroyed, then? Neither by iron, nor by
fire, but by judgements" (Discourses 4.1.86). A tyrant can imprison the
body, confiscate property, even take a life, but "it isn't what you're
threatened with that compels you, but your own judgement that it is better
to do this or that than to die" (1.17.25) — so your own choice is the only
thing that can ever actually constrain your choice. If it were otherwise,
he adds, "he would no longer be God" — that is, the claim that a rational
being's core freedom is inviolable is treated as close to definitional of
what it means to be a rational agent at all.

Marcus Aurelius gives the same idea its most compact formulation: "The mind
without passions is a fortress. No place is more secure. Once we take
refuge there we are safe forever. Not to see this is ignorance. To see it
and not seek safety means misery" (*Meditations* 8.48). Elsewhere he
locates the same refuge in the present moment and inside oneself rather
than in any external circumstance: people fantasize about escaping to the
country or the coast, "but you can get away from it anytime you like. By
going within... Nowhere you can go is more peaceful... than your own soul"
(4.3).

Nobody is born with a steel backbone or an inner citadel — both must be
forged. Theodore Roosevelt, by twelve, suffered nightly near-death asthma
attacks; his father told him he had the mind but not the body, and offered
tools to make it — "hard drudgery" requiring determination. Roosevelt's
reply: "I'll make my body." Five years of daily training essentially
defeated the asthma and prepared him for what he called "the Strenuous
Life" — loss, political defeat, war, assassination attempts. Physical
training and mental practice reinforce each other: *mens sana in corpore
sano* — the ancient philosophers saw themselves as mental athletes training
the brain through repeated exercises until muscle memory produced intuitive
response to obstacles. Communities deprived of physical temples sometimes
rebuilt within the mind — a metaphysical fortress drawn on wherever
dispersed.

The citadel must be built and actively reinforced during good times so it
can be depended on during difficult ones: "We protect our inner fortress so
it may protect us." The way to strengthen an arch is to put weight on it —
tension binds the stones together, and only with tension does it hold weight.
"The path of least resistance is a terrible teacher." You'll have far better
luck toughening yourself up than trying to take the teeth out of a world
that is — at best — indifferent to your existence. Preparation is armor
plating: it does not make you invincible, but readies you for when fortune
shifts, "and it always does."

The practical corollary is that external assault — insult, threat,
imprisonment, even torture — can damage the body or possessions but cannot,
by itself, damage the one thing that actually constitutes a person's good or
ill. Epictetus dramatizes this with a scripted response to threats: told
"I'll throw you into chains," the correct reframe is "it is my hands and
feet that he is threatening"; told "I'll have you beheaded," the reframe is
"my head" — every threat, examined closely, turns out to target only the
body, never the choosing self (Discourses 1.29.5-8). This is why the
Stoics can maintain that [wrongdoing only really harms the wrongdoer](no-one-errs-willingly.md):
if the citadel can only be breached from within, then someone who wrongs
you has, at most, damaged your body or your property, while doing genuine
damage to their own judgment — the one thing that was actually theirs to
protect.
