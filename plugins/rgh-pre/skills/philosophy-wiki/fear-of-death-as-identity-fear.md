---
type: concept
title: Fear of Death as Fear of Identity Loss
description: >
  The claim that dread of dying is often dread of the dissolution of a
  constructed self-story — and that when death and identity are seen as
  concepts rather than threats, the fear collapses into acceptance or even
  love of what is.
sources:
  - title: "Loving What Is, Revised Edition: Four Questions That Can Change Your Life"
    resource: "Byron Katie, ch. 12"
---

"When the mind thinks of death, it looks at nothing and calls it something,
to keep from experiencing what it — the mind — really is." Until death is
understood as equal to life, "you'll always try to control what happens, and
it's always going to hurt." Katie's central claim at the deathbed: "The fear
of death is the last smoke screen for the fear of love." We think we fear the
body's death, but really fear "the death of our identity"; inquiry reveals
both death and identity as concepts, ending fear. "Loss is another concept."

A woman dying of cancer summoned Katie, who said "I don't see a problem."
The woman pulled off the sheet to reveal one leg swollen to twice normal size:
"you must be blind." Katie: "Oh, now I see the problem. You're suffering
from the belief that that leg should look like this one. Who would you be
without that thought?" The woman laughed, fear "poured out through her
laughter," and she said it was the happiest she'd ever been. Another hospice
patient: "I'm so frightened. I don't know how to die." Katie reframed dying
via a familiar act: "Do you know how to take a nap? ... You close your eyes
every night, and you go to sleep. People look forward to sleeping. That's all
death is." On fear of an afterlife she "won't know what to do" in: "Can you
really know that there's something to do?" — "There's nothing you have to know,
and it's always all right. Everything you need is already there for you."
She was "describing life to her, of course, not death."

Katie reports sitting with many people on their deathbeds who, after inquiry,
"always tell me that they're fine." "There's no sadness without a story that
opposes reality." At her grandson's birth crisis — initially not breathing —
she loved that he "didn't have to breathe for me to love him": "I knew that
even without a single breath, he had lived a full life. I love reality, not
the way a fantasy would dictate but just the way it is, right now." Her
daughter, seeing Katie's smile amid the panic, felt "a wave of peace" — "it
was okay with me."

This converges with Stoic arguments that [death is not to be
feared](death-is-not-to-be-feared.md) because dread attaches to imagination
and identity, not to death-as-such — and with [arguing with reality causes
suffering](arguing-with-reality-causes-suffering.md) when we oppose how the
body actually is at the end. See [impermanence of self and
non-attachment](impermanence-of-self-and-non-attachment.md) for the broader
claim that the self is not a fixed thing to defend.
