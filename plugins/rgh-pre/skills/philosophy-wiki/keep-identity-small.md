---
type: concept
title: Keep Identity Small
description: >
  The claim that clinging tightly to any single identity makes you brittle
  when life challenges that role, while defining yourself by flexible traits
  lets you adapt like water around an obstacle.
sources:
  - title: "Atomic Habits: Tiny Changes, Remarkable Results"
    resource: "James Clear, ch. 20"
---

Repeating a habit builds evidence of a desired identity, but once established,
those same beliefs can hold you back from the next level of growth. Identity
creates a kind of pride that makes you deny weak spots and blocks real growth —
one of the greatest downsides of building habits. The more sacred an idea is
to you — the more deeply tied to identity — the more strongly you defend it
against criticism.

Paul Graham's counsel: keep your identity small. Avoid letting any single
aspect become an overwhelming portion of who you are. The more one belief
defines you, the less capable you are of adapting when life challenges you. If
everything is tied up in being "the point guard" or "the partner at the firm,"
losing that facet is devastating. When you cling too tightly to one identity,
you become brittle; lose that one thing and you lose yourself.

Mitigation: redefine identity around traits that survive role change — "I'm an
athlete" becomes "I'm the type of person who is mentally tough and loves a
physical challenge"; "I'm the CEO" becomes "I'm the type of person who builds
and creates things." Identity can be flexible rather than brittle, working with
changing circumstances like water around an obstacle. Lao Tzu: whoever is stiff
and inflexible is a disciple of death; whoever is soft and yielding is a
disciple of life. Since [everything is impermanent](impermanence-of-self-and-non-attachment.md),
periodic reflection on whether old identities still serve you is essential. Pair
with [selfing](selfing.md) on the constructed, unstable nature of the "I."
