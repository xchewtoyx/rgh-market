title: The Three Topics of Stoic Training
description: >
  Epictetus's structure for practicing philosophy in the right order —
  first governing desire and aversion, then appropriate action, and only
  last the fine points of logical assent — because most people neglect the
  first two for the sake of the third.
sources:
  - title: "Discourses, Fragments, Handbook"
    resource: "Epictetus, Discourses, Fragments, Handbook (tr. Robin Hard), 1.4, 2.17, 3.2, 3.12"

Epictetus organizes practical Stoic training into three ranked areas
(*topoi*), stated most fully at Discourses 3.2.1-5:

1. **Desire and aversion** — the most urgent area, since passions like
   fear, grief, and frustrated longing arise only when desire and aversion
   are attached to things outside [what is up to us](dichotomy-of-control.md).
   Training here means learning to desire only what is genuinely achievable
   and to direct aversion only at genuine faults within one's own choice.
2. **Motivation to act and appropriate action** — governing how a person
   actually behaves toward others in their various social roles (son,
   brother, citizen), so that action is well-timed, fitting, and consistent
   with one's relationships.
3. **Freedom from error and hasty judgment**, i.e. the discipline of
   [assent](testing-impressions-before-assent.md) — reserved for those
   already advanced, aiming at constancy of judgment even under fatigue,
   drink, or low mood.

Epictetus insists on this order because he sees his contemporaries
inverting it: "philosophers nowadays neglect the first and second areas of
study to concentrate on the third," obsessing over logical puzzles like
"the Liar" paradox while remaining visibly undone by money, an attractive
face, or envy of a neighbor's inheritance (3.2.6-10). Elsewhere he singles
out desire and aversion as the area to master first among the three,
because it is the direct cause of passion: working through the Medea
episode, he asks pointedly, "do you presently desire what is possible? Why,
then, are you frustrated?" (2.17.14-18).

He also gives concrete training advice matched to each topic. For desire
and aversion, the discipline is largely negative at first — for now,
suppress desire almost entirely (since what is rightly desirable is not yet
securely within reach) and confine aversion only to genuine faults within
one's own power (Handbook 2). For appropriate action, the discipline is
positive and situational — deriving specific duties from one's specific
relationships and social roles (Discourses 2.10). And training in the third
area, when a person is ready for it, should proceed through targeted
counter-habit exercises: someone prone to irritability trains by
deliberately enduring minor provocations, someone prone to appetite trains
with moderate exposure before attempting a serious test — "pot and stone
don't belong together," so a beginner should not test himself against a
strong temptation prematurely (3.12.1-12).

This structure is Epictetus's own version of what later scholarship (via
Pierre Hadot) calls Stoicism's "three disciplines" and maps closely onto
Marcus Aurelius's own recurring triad of accepting events, treating people
rightly, and examining impressions with care (*Meditations* 7.54, 9.6). It
functions as the organizing scaffold behind most of the other specific
Stoic techniques in this bundle — the discipline of desire and aversion
underlies [preferred indifferents](preferred-indifferents.md) and
[the dichotomy of control](dichotomy-of-control.md); the discipline of
action underlies [Stoic cosmopolitanism](stoic-cosmopolitanism.md) and the
duties that follow from it; and the discipline of assent underlies
[testing impressions before assent](testing-impressions-before-assent.md)
itself.
