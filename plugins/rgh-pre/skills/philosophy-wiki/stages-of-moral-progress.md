description: >
  The Stoic distinction between the fully wise sage — a near-mythical ideal
  — and the "progressor" who is still working toward wisdom, with a ranked
  account of how far along the path a person has come.

Stoic writers are candid that the fully wise person — the sage, who has
achieved complete and permanent virtue — is vanishingly rare, "born perhaps
once every five hundred years, like the phoenix" (Seneca, Letter 42, echoed
at Letter 9). This is not false modesty; it is a deliberate feature of the
doctrine, meant to keep the ideal aspirational rather than to suggest most
people, including most Stoic teachers, have actually arrived.

Between ordinary vice and full sagehood, Seneca lays out a ranked account
of moral progress, most fully in Letter 75. Progressors are still,
technically, counted among "the foolish," yet meaningfully distinguished
from one another by how much irrational emotion still moves them: (1) those
who have shed their faults and emotions and are "in that place from which
there is no backsliding," but who do not yet know or trust that they have
arrived; (2) those who have shed the deep, ingrained *infirmities* (settled
bad judgments like greed or ambition) but still experience *emotions*
(sudden, unjustified, but not yet hardened disturbances of mind), with the
risk that repeated unchecked emotions eventually harden back into
infirmities, "just as a single cold in the head... if it happens
repeatedly... brings on the wasting disease"; (3) those free of many
serious faults but not all — free of greed but still angry, free of lust
but still ambitious, brave about death but still afraid of pain. Seneca
counsels aiming realistically at this third tier: "it is sufficient
achievement for us if we are not among the worst," while admitting his own
ongoing struggle even as a teacher: "we have already been taken over: we
are in the grip of faults even as we strive for virtue."

Elsewhere he distinguishes the progressor from the sage more sharply: "the
progressor moves, but does not shift position; he merely bobs in place. The
wise person does not move at all" (Letter 35) — even a person genuinely
advancing can still fluctuate in day-to-day resolve without losing overall
direction, whereas the sage's stability is total and no longer subject to
lapse (Letter 72). This matters practically because it licenses patience
with one's own incomplete progress without licensing complacency: Seneca
openly admits "I am still persuading myself of the principles I extol; I am
not fully convinced" (Letter 71), using the analogy of a dye that must soak
in over time rather than merely tint the surface.

Epictetus frames the same distinction operationally, warning against
mistaking book-knowledge for progress: true progress is found "in someone
who turns from external things to cultivate his own power of choice" and
applies it daily, not in someone who can quote Chrysippus (Discourses
1.4.18-21) — a caution closely related to the wider Stoic insistence that
[philosophy is therapy, not theory](philosophy-as-therapy-not-theory.md).
