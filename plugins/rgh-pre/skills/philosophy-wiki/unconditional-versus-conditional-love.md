---
type: concept
title: Unconditional Versus Conditional Love
description: >
  The distinction between love that depends on a reason ("I love you
  because...") and is therefore vulnerable to that reason changing, and
  love without a precondition, which cannot generate the disappointment
  that conditional attachment can.
sources:
  - title: "Solve for Happy"
    resource: "Mo Gawdat, Solve for Happy, Part 4, ch. 12"
---

Mo Gawdat's *Solve for Happy* draws a sharp line between conditional love —
which follows the implicit structure "I love you because you make me feel
X" — and unconditional love, which has no reason, precondition, or
expectation attached at all: "I love," full stop. He treats most other
positive feelings toward people (admiration, liking, respect) as
thought-generated and therefore conditional in the same way: they depend on
an ongoing evaluation of the other person's traits, and so are structurally
vulnerable to disappointment whenever that evaluation changes or the
underlying trait shifts. Conditional love, on this account, is one major
source of relationship suffering precisely because it is built to
fluctuate with its own justification.

Unconditional love is treated as functionally different because, having no
expectation attached, it cannot generate a gap between what was expected
and what occurred — "no expectation never turns into a missed expectation."
He explicitly rejects the common frame "I deserve to be loved" as itself a
subtly conditional, ego-driven claim, and locates the actual reward of
unconditional love in the giving of it rather than in receiving it back:
"the true joy of true love is in giving it." Applied to the death of his
son, he distinguishes the durability of the two kinds of love directly:
ordinary attachment to a person's presence, habits, or company is
vulnerable to loss, but a love held without precondition is not, since it
was never contingent on the other person's continued presence in the first
place — "Ali's love will always remain in my heart even though he moved on
and left our physical world."

The distinction functions as a practical guide as much as a description:
his "instructions" for cultivating it recommend extending unconditional
regard even to people who are difficult or unlikeable, on the theory that
much of what looks like an unlikeable trait is really a defensive posture
("mask") that softens once met with acceptance rather than judgment; and
extending the same unconditional stance toward oneself, treating harsh
self-criticism as a habit rarely extended to anyone else and therefore
worth questioning.

The idea sits close to several older traditions without being identical to
any one of them: it echoes the Stoic case that what deserves love in
another person is their character rather than their utility (see
[Stoic friendship](stoic-friendship.md), where friendship built on
expedience is explicitly distinguished from friendship built on shared
virtue), while framing the durability question in terms of the *lover's*
expectations rather than the *beloved's* virtue. It is also closely related
to the broader case that
[loss is easier to bear once a possession or relationship is understood as
held rather than owned outright](everything-is-on-loan.md), since
unconditional love, on this account, never depended on ownership or
permanence to begin with.
