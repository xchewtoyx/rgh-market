type: concept
title: Megalopsychia (Greatness of Soul)
description: >
  The Stoic and classical ideal of heroic courage — risking oneself for
  others or a larger cause, where love overrides self-preservation and
  courage goes beyond the call of duty.
sources:
  - title: "Courage Is Calling: Fortune Favors the Brave"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 15, 16, 14"
---

If ordinary [courage as the form of every virtue at the testing
point](stoic-cardinal-virtues.md) is "putting your ass on the line," the
heroic is risking oneself for someone else — for a person, an idea, a
country, the vulnerable — not for one's own benefit. The Stoics called this
*megalopsychia* — "greatness of soul," glossed as "courage plus." De Gaulle,
asked what he meant by France's "grandeur," answered: "The road one takes to
surpass oneself" — the rarest, most profound form of bravery.

Thermopylae dramatizes the structure. Leonidas and three hundred Spartans —
each father of at least one living son — held a narrow pass against Xerxes's
vast army not because victory was likely but because valor — "not just
courage but a commitment to something larger than themselves" — made the
mission worth attempting. Leonidas refused Xerxes's bribes: "The Greeks have
learned from their fathers to gain lands, not by cowardice but by valor."
Asked how so few could face so many: "If you men think that I rely on
numbers, then all Greece is not sufficient... but if on men's valor, then
this number will do." Their stand bought Greece time and confidence; uncommon
valor became, for a brief moment, a common virtue.

Steven Pressfield's question — what is the opposite of fear? — is not
fearlessness. You can be courageous for selfish reasons; that is meaningful
but not the same thing. Worse, you can be spectacularly brave for a wrong
or hollow cause — see [courage requires a worthy cause](courage-requires-a-worthy-cause.md)
for why the *why* behind the deed, not its cost alone, separates virtue
from madness. The true opposite of fear is love: love for one
another, for ideas, for country, for the vulnerable and weak, for the next
generation. Leonidas's parting words to his wife — marry a good man, bear
children, live a good life — express marrow-deep love that lets a person rise
above self-preservation. Not all selflessness requires the ultimate sacrifice,
but there is no selflessness without sacrifice.

If courage alone is unreasonable, this higher form of love is "baffling in
its majesty" — real human greatness, transcending logic, self-interest, and
millions of years of biology. The Spartans stand in for anonymous resisters
for all time: trial witnesses facing reprisals, people beaten for registering
to vote, athletes playing through career-ending injuries for team or family.
What we are willing to give transforms us from brave to heroic — the
expression of [principles worth the cost](principles-worth-the-cost.md) at
its fullest pitch. Viktor Frankl: "Man is pushed by drives. But he is pulled
by values." Altruism and selflessness are harder to explain than ordinary
courage — historian T. R. Fehrenbach: "Human folly is easier to explain than
human valor." Ordinary courage has a clear reward structure; sacrificing
oneself, or sacrificing deeply for something, does not fit that logic.

Those without a strong sense of self are unlikely to be brave, yet the highest
form of bravery demands a selflessness that can be, in some cases, suicidal —
perhaps inexplicable in words, like feats of superhuman strength. A craven
leader looking over a military cemetery and asking "What was in it for them?"
reveals sincere bafflement in the transactional and selfish; for others the
question comes from humility and awe. This capacity is essential to survival
"as the good guys" — why art celebrates it and heroes' names remain touchstones.
Heroism is rarer than mere courage, "so powerful we have trouble staring it
directly in the face." Medal of Honor recipients insist "I just did what
anyone would have done" — if that were true, we would not make such a fuss.
True heroism shames and humbles us, moves us beyond reason because it came
from something beyond reason. The survival rate of those who touch this
greatness is low — "in some cases, they died so that we could live" — and we
fail them and ourselves if we do not wrestle with the meaning of that sacrifice.
