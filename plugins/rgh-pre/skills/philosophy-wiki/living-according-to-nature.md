title: Living According to Nature

  The Stoic formula for the goal of life — that the human good consists in
  living in accordance with reason, both one's own nature and the rational
  order of the whole cosmos.

The Stoic school's own summary formula for the goal of life was "living
according to nature," where "nature" refers simultaneously to a person's
own specific nature as a rational being and to the rational order governing
the cosmos as a whole — the two are meant to converge, since human reason
is understood as a fragment or portion of the same rational principle that
organizes the universe.

Seneca supplies the compressed argument: "What, then, is reason? The
imitation of nature. What is the highest good of the human being? Behaving
as nature intended" (Letter 66). He grounds this in an explicit
mind-body-cosmos analogy — the human mind should govern the body the way
divine reason governs the material universe: "let the inferior parts serve
the better: let us be brave against the strokes of fortune; let us not
tremble at injuries, or wounds, or fetters, or want" (Letter 65). He treats
the doctrine of an indwelling divine element in every person as the
metaphysical backing for this claim: "a sacred spirit dwells within us, and
is the observer and guardian of all our goods and ills... no one is a good
man without God" (Letter 41) — human reason, properly used, just is the
presence of the rational, cosmic order operating locally.

Epictetus builds the same idea into his argument from design: animals get
by on mere use of impressions, but humans additionally *understand* their
own use of impressions, and this extra capacity is not optional decoration
but the actual point of being human — "for humans mere survival (eating,
drinking, procreating) is not enough; we must live 'in accordance with our
own specific nature,' namely contemplation, understanding, and harmony with
nature" (Discourses 1.6.12-22).

A significant qualification, worked out at length in Seneca's Letter 90
against the historian-philosopher Posidonius, keeps the formula from
collapsing into simple primitivism. Posidonius had claimed that early
humans, living a simple communal life without private property or
technology, were themselves "wise" in the full Stoic sense. Seneca
disagrees sharply: these people behaved *as if* wise, from mere natural
innocence, but were not actually wise, because genuine virtue requires
trained, deliberate judgment, not accidental disposition — "there is a
great difference between refusing to do wrong and not knowing how to do it...
real virtue belongs only to a mind that has been trained, thoroughly
instructed, and brought to the highest condition by constant practice. We
are indeed born for this, but not born with it." "Nature," in the Stoic
formula, therefore does not mean an untutored, primitive state to be
returned to, but a rational excellence that has to be actively achieved.

This formula underwrites nearly every other Stoic doctrine in the corpus:
it is the basis for treating
[virtue as the sole good](virtue-as-the-sole-good.md) (since only reason,
"nature's" defining human excellence, can be that good), for
[Stoic cosmopolitanism](stoic-cosmopolitanism.md) (since shared rational
nature is what makes all humans fellow citizens), and for
[willing acceptance of fate](amor-fati.md) (since agreeing with the
rational order of the whole just is what living according to nature
requires).
