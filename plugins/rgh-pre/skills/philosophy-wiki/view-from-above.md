type: concept
title: The View from Above
description: >
  A perspective-taking exercise, used by Marcus Aurelius and Seneca, of
  mentally rising above one's immediate circumstances to see them at the
  scale of the whole cosmos or of history, so as to shrink their apparent
  size and urgency.
sources:
  - title: "Meditations"
    resource: "Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (tr. Gregory Hays), 7.47-48, 9.32, 12.24, 6.36"
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), Letter 91"
---

Marcus Aurelius repeatedly practices a deliberate change of scale: imagine
looking down on human life from a great height, and watch its usual
weightiness dissolve. "Watch the courses of the stars as if you revolved
with them... Thoughts like this wash off the mud of life below" (7.47).
Seen from above, ordinary human activity resolves into "herds, armies,
farms; weddings, divorces, births, deaths... a harmony of opposites"
(7.48) — the same events that feel urgent and singular from inside them
turn out to be instances of endlessly repeating patterns once the observer
steps back far enough.

The same move is applied to time as well as space. "A grape seed in
infinite space. A half twist of a corkscrew against eternity" (10.17) —
whatever a person is currently anxious about is measured against the
"vastness of time before and after" (12.7) and against the observation that
"observing life for forty years is as good as a thousand," since the same
patterns simply recur (7.49). He runs a version of this exercise
specifically against fear of death and desire for lasting fame: imagining
vanished eras — the age of Vespasian, the age of Trajan — in which people
did "the exact same things" (marrying, scheming, hoarding money, falling in
love) and are now almost entirely forgotten, so that even the most
celebrated Roman names are "fading into obscurity" (4.32-33, 6.47).

Seneca uses the same technique to console a friend after the total
destruction of his home city by fire, framing catastrophe not as a unique
personal outrage but as one recurring instance of a cosmic pattern:
earthquakes and disasters have leveled cities throughout history and
throughout nature, "since nature's own works are not unassailed, we should
not complain about the destruction of cities... whatever we mortals
construct is condemned to be mortal" (Letter 91). He also retells the
anecdote of Alexander the Great being taught geometry and, on grasping the
true scale of the cosmos relative to his own conquests, being made to feel
"puny" rather than great — proof, in Seneca's reading, that scale itself is
a corrective to inflated self-importance (Letter 91).

The view from above is best understood as a deliberate technique for
supporting the claim that [judgments, not events, disturb us](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md):
if a present trouble looks unbearable at ordinary scale but negligible at
cosmic scale, the trouble itself has not changed — only the frame the mind
is using to judge it. It works alongside
[remembering one's own mortality](memento-mori.md) as one of the two main
Stoic perspective-shifting exercises, one shrinking space and time, the
other shrinking the presumed permanence of the self doing the worrying.
