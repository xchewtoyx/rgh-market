title: No One Errs Willingly
description: >
  The Socratic-Stoic claim that wrongdoers act from mistaken judgment about
  what is good, not from clear-eyed malice, and so deserve pity and
  correction rather than hatred.
sources:
  - title: "Discourses, Fragments, Handbook"
    resource: "Epictetus, Discourses, Fragments, Handbook (tr. Robin Hard), 1.18, 1.28, Handbook 42"
  - title: "Meditations"
    resource: "Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (tr. Gregory Hays), 2.1, 7.22, 11.18"
---

The Stoics inherited from Socrates the claim that no one does wrong
knowingly — that every person acts toward what they judge, in the moment,
to be to their own advantage, so wrongdoing is always the result of a
mistaken judgment about what is actually good, never a clear-eyed embrace
of what the wrongdoer correctly recognizes as bad. Epictetus makes the
logical structure explicit: "everyone assents to what they judge to be so,
and acts toward what they judge advantageous — you cannot judge one thing
advantageous and desire another" (Discourses 1.18.1-2). It follows that
thieves and adulterers should be pitied rather than hated, since they have
"fallen into error" about good and bad in the same way a blind person has
lost sight — reframing "should this thief be put to death" as equivalent to
"should this blind man be put to death" exposes, in his view, the
inhumanity of pure anger at wrongdoers (1.18.3-8). He extends this to the
literary case of Medea, whose famous line — "I know that what I intend to
do is bad, / But anger is master of my plans" — is read not as someone
overridden by a separate faculty but as someone who judged revenge more
beneficial than her children's safety, in error (1.28.7-9).

The practical corollary is a rule against anger at wrongdoers: since good
and bad reside only in a person's own [choice](dichotomy-of-control.md),
someone who wrongs you has already injured only himself, and the question
"should I injure him back?" reduces to "should I also injure myself?"
(Discourses 2.10.24-27). Handbook 42 gives the compressed practical form: a
wrong or an insult is done "because [the offender] regards that as being
the proper thing for him to do"; since he can only act on what seems right
to him, and if that judgment is wrong he alone suffers the harm, the
appropriate response is not outrage but something closer to "that is how
it seemed to him."

Marcus Aurelius opens his second book with a version of the same principle
built into a morning routine: expect to meet people who are "meddling,
ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous, and surly" today, "because they
can't tell good from evil" (*Meditations* 2.1) — and treats affection for
people even in their mistakes as a distinctively human capacity, achieved
by recognizing shared humanity, involuntary ignorance, and shared mortality
(7.22). His most developed treatment is the "ninefold" checklist for
managing anger at others (11.18), which works through the same claim from
several angles: if the offender is actually wrong, they act "involuntarily,
out of ignorance," and even the angry party's own certainty is suspect,
since "you have to know an awful lot" to judge others fairly.

This principle sits close to, but is distinct from, the claim that
[the inner citadel](stoic-inner-citadel.md) cannot be breached by anyone
else's action — that is a claim about what wrongdoing can and cannot touch;
this is a claim about why wrongdoing happens at all, and what response it
therefore warrants.
