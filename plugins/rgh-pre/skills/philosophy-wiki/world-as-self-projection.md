---
type: concept
title: World as Self-Projection
description: >
  The claim that inner and outer always match — that we perceive the world
  through uninvestigated stories and see ourselves in what we call reality,
  not a neutral given independent of the mind that interprets it.
sources:
  - title: "Loving What Is, Revised Edition: Four Questions That Can Change Your Life"
    resource: "Byron Katie, ch. 1, 13"
---

"The world is your perception of it. Inside and outside always match; they are
reflections of each other... you are the confused thinker looking out and seeing
yourself." Even Jesus or Buddha standing in front of a confused listener would
be heard as confused, because "confusion would be the listener." Katie as
projection: "I am the story of who you think I am, not who I really am... your
uninvestigated story, your own myth." Basic perception participates — "It's a
tree" — is that true? "Who told you it was a tree? Who was the original
authority? How did they know?"

Stories are thoughts or thought-sequences we convince ourselves are real, about
past, present, and future, about what things should or could be or why they are.
Stories are "untested, uninvestigated theories" about what events mean,
appearing constantly — we don't realize they're just theories. Operating on
uninvestigated theories without awareness of it is being in "the dream," which
can become a nightmare. An illustrative case: assuming a woman in a restroom
was "rude" for leaving the toilet seat wet, mentally rehearsing confrontation,
then flushing and the water spraying up — revealing the seat's wetness had an
innocent cause. Small stories breed bigger ones; bigger stories breed "major
theories about life" that one is too frightened to leave the bedroom.

"There is only one problem, ever: your uninvestigated story in the moment." This
does not deny that events occur; it locates disturbance in the layer added to
bare fact — the same move Epictetus makes when he says [it is judgments, not
events, that disturb us](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md). Katie's version
emphasizes that the listener's story about what is said "is the only way you can
hurt yourself" — how you receive another person's words determines whether you
help or harm yourself with them. The philosophical point is epistemological and
ethical together: before asking what to do about the world, notice that the
world you are acting on is already shaped by the stories you have not examined.
