---
type: concept
title: Unity Over Self
description: >
  The Stoic and practical claim that focusing on a cause larger than oneself
  — especially service to others under shared hardship — diminishes fear,
  self-pity, and the urge to quit when personal troubles feel overwhelming.
sources:
  - title: "Courage Is Calling: Fortune Favors the Brave"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 16"
---

LeRoy Percy: "A man's job is to make the world a better place to live in, so
far as he is able—always remembering the results will be infinitesimal—and to
attend to his own soul." James Stockdale, shot down over North Vietnam in 1965,
contemplated imprisonment and torture on the way down — but "the second Stockdale
hit the ground, that contemplation stopped." He had a mission. Contrasted with
Korean War POW camps where individual self-preservation turned prisoners against
each other, Stockdale chose leadership, support, and direction for fellow
prisoners over seven years — two in leg irons in solitary. He attempted suicide
not to escape suffering but to refuse being used as a tool against his men's
common cause. His watchword: "U.S.—Unity over Self." John McCain refused early
release offered to embarrass his family and the U.S., staying tortured by
choice rather than undermine the cause — "their cause was their men," not zeal
for the war itself.

Core mechanism: focusing on others or on setting a good example makes personal
fears and troubles diminish — no time left for them. "Shared purpose gives us
strength." The urge to quit or compromise principles feels selfish once you
consider who else it would affect. When stuck on an intractable problem,
reframe: "If I can't solve this for myself, how can I at least make this better
for other people?" — assume nothing in it for you and ask how the situation
could benefit others, family, those you lead, or people who'll later face
something similar. Making it "all about you" (Why did this happen to me?)
doesn't help; reframing toward mission ("now we have something to do") lifts
hopelessness. Stop putting "I" in front of events — that inflates your own role
and importance and is why losses feel personal and isolating.

What can and can't be broken: "Pride can be broken. Toughness has its limits.
But a desire to help? No harshness, no deprivation, no toil should interfere
with our empathy toward others. Compassion is always an option. Camaraderie as
well." This is "a power of the will that can never be taken away, only
relinquished." Against self-pity: your trouble is not uniquely picked out for
you — believing so is myopia that makes you feel like the center of the
universe, when others have dealt with worse and everyone is subject to random,
incomprehensible events. Historical continuity — someone stood where you stand
a century ago and felt the same things — is "exhilarating," a way of being part
of a larger whole. This extends [courage requires a worthy cause](courage-requires-a-worthy-cause.md)
from battlefield abstraction to ordinary solidarity, pairs with [courage for
neighbors](courage-for-neighbors.md), and connects to [making others better by
example](making-others-better-by-example.md) when service becomes the path
through one's own obstacle.

In summer 1969, forty-six and brutally beaten in the Hanoi Hilton, Stockdale
gashed his own forehead with a razor rather than appear on camera saying all
was well — "He wasn't a prisoner of war. He was a prisoner at war." When torture
of his comrades escalated that fall, he tied himself to a chair, waddled to the
prison's one paned window, broke it, and slit his wrists with a shard. His own
account: "The last thing the North Vietnamese needed was me dead... Prison
torture, as we had known it in Hanoi, ended for everybody that night." The
guards expected fear and pain to pit prisoners against each other via "but what
about me?" logic but found instead "a sincere belief in an idea, an idea as old
as the scriptures. This idea is you are your brother's keeper... That's the
flip side of 'What's in it for me?'" — distinguishing loving thy neighbor
from sacrificing for them, quoting John 15:13: "Greater love hath no man than
this, that a man lay down his life for his friends." Fellow POWs signaled "U"
and "S" to each other — not "United States" but "Unity over Self" — when
lonely, being taken for torture, or self-recriminating over what they'd said
under duress. Thích Quảng Đức's self-immolation in complete stillness and
self-command, protesting persecution of Buddhist citizens, illustrates the same
structure: not defiance for its own sake, but love of the innocent and of a
future one won't live to see. The root of "courage" means "heart."
