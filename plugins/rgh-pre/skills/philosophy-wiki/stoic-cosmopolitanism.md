type: concept
title: Stoic Cosmopolitanism
description: >
  The Stoic doctrine that every rational being is first and foremost a
  citizen of the universe, sharing reason in common with all humanity and
  the gods, prior to and beyond any local city or nation.
sources:
  - title: "Discourses, Fragments, Handbook"
    resource: "Epictetus, Discourses, Fragments, Handbook (tr. Robin Hard), 1.9, 2.10"
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), Letters 28, 68, 95"
  - title: "Meditations"
    resource: "Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (tr. Gregory Hays), 4.4, 6.44"
---

The Stoics held that rational beings — human and divine alike — belong to a
single natural community grounded in shared reason, prior to any local
citizenship. Epictetus makes the point through Socrates's own reported
answer to "where are you from?": not a local ethnic label but "I'm a
citizen of the universe" (Discourses 1.9.1-3). The universe itself is "this
society in which human beings and God are associated together," and it is
specifically the possession of reason that lets a being enter into this
wider community at all (1.9.4-6).

Seneca uses the same idea to make sense of exile and displacement: "I was
not born in any one spot; my homeland is this entire world" (Letter 28),
and he treats the sage's true "state" as the whole cosmos rather than any
particular polity — so that stepping back from local political office is
not really a withdrawal from public service, since "he is taking up a seat
in heaven" and continuing to serve the larger community by other means
(Letter 68). In the paired letters on precepts and principles, he grounds
practical ethics directly in this cosmopolitan claim: "this universe that
you see, containing the human and the divine, is a unity; we are the limbs
of a mighty body," using the image of an arch whose stones depend on each
other's support to explain why mutual human obligation runs deeper than any
local law (Letter 95) — a formulation he also uses to argue that basic
duties of aid (to the shipwrecked, to strangers, to the starving) follow
directly from this shared nature rather than from custom.

Marcus Aurelius works out the logical structure of the claim most
explicitly: since thought is shared, and reason is shared, humanity must
share a common law, and therefore be fellow citizens of a shared "state"
that can only be the world itself, since nothing smaller could contain all
rational beings (*Meditations* 4.4). He resolves the potential conflict
between local and cosmic citizenship by holding both together: "my city and
state are Rome — as Antoninus. But as a human being? The world. So for me,
'good' can only mean what's good for both communities" (6.44).

Cosmopolitanism is the metaphysical backing for two more practical Stoic
commitments: the case that
[Stoic friendship](stoic-friendship.md) rests on a natural sociability
wider than personal advantage, and the uneasy Stoic position on
[slavery and human dignity](stoic-slavery-and-human-dignity.md), where equal
possession of reason grounds a claim to equal moral worth that the Stoics
never fully carried through into a demand for equal legal or political
status.
