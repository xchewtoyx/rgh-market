title: Present-Moment Intimacy
description: >

  intimate relationship with it — through continual attending with care and
  discernment — is the root of Buddhism, Taoism, yoga, and comparable Western
  voices, and the direct opposite of taking life for granted.

The key to paths found at the root of Buddhism, Taoism, and yoga — and echoed
in Emerson, Thoreau, Whitman, and Native American wisdom — is appreciation for
the present moment and cultivation of an intimate relationship with it through
continual attending with care and discernment. This is the direct opposite of
taking life for granted.

Habitually ignoring the present in favor of moments yet to come leads to a
pervasive lack of awareness of the web of life in which we are embedded,
including lack of understanding of our own mind and how it shapes perceptions
and actions. That ignorance severely limits our sense of what it means to be a
person and how we are connected to each other and the world. Part One opens
with Thoreau's epigraph from *Walden*: "Only that day dawns to which we are
awake."

This intimacy is not escapist stillness divorced from life — it grounds
[everyday life as the path](everyday-life-as-the-path.md) and the work of
[mindfulness as the art of living](mindfulness-as-art-of-living.md). In
[interconnectedness](interconnectedness.md), Kabat-Zinn notes that awareness of
impermanence makes now "everything" because everything we contact connects us
to the whole world in each moment, and things are only here temporarily. The
Stoic inner citadel also locates refuge in the present moment — see [the inner
citadel](stoic-inner-citadel.md) — though the Buddhist-influenced frame here
emphasizes intimacy and fullness rather than fortress imagery.
