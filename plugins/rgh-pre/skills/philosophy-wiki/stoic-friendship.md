title: Stoic Friendship
description: >
  Seneca's account of friendship as grounded in shared virtue and natural
  sociability rather than utility, distinguishing it sharply from the
  Epicurean view that friendship is ultimately self-interested.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), Letters 3, 6, 9, 35, 48, 109"
---

Seneca's letters return repeatedly to friendship, developing a picture
sharply distinct from the Epicurean position he otherwise draws on freely.
Where Epicureans (in his summary) grounded friendship in a calculation of
mutual advantage — wanting a friend "to sit beside him in illness" — Seneca
insists that a friendship built on expedience collapses the moment
expedience runs out: "he who begins being a friend for the sake of
expediency will also stop for the sake of expediency." The alternative
motive he proposes is close to self-sacrifice for its own sake: "why make a
friend? To have someone I can die for, someone I can accompany into exile,
someone whose life I can save, even by laying down my own. What you
describe [utility-friendship] is a business deal, not a friendship" (Letter
9).

This letter also works out a puzzle the Stoics inherited from rival
schools: if the sage is genuinely self-sufficient, why would he need a
friend at all? Seneca's answer distinguishes two senses of self-sufficiency,
via Chrysippus: the sage "lacks nothing" yet "has a use for many things,"
while the fool "has a use for nothing" yet "lacks everything," because lack
implies need and the sage needs nothing even while making good use of what
he has — including friends. Friendship, on this view, arises from
[natural sociability](stoic-cosmopolitanism.md), the same innate drive that
makes solitude itself uncomfortable, not from deficiency.

Practical advice on friendship in the letters is characteristically blunt.
On admitting people to one's confidence: "they should judge first, then
love" — weigh carefully before extending trust, but once someone is
admitted, hold nothing back, and "live in such a way that anything you
would admit to yourself could be admitted even to an enemy" (Letter 3). On
the value of company for moral progress, Seneca argues direct example
teaches better than written precept: "learning by precepts is the long way
around. The quick and effective way is to learn by example" (Letter 6) —
Cleanthes was shaped by living alongside Zeno, not merely by hearing him.
He also treats the practical measure of real friendship as a test of
character rather than sentiment: "observe whether your intentions are the
same today as they were yesterday. A change of intention shows that the
mind is at sea" (Letter 35), tying friendship to
[the broader Stoic emphasis on consistency of character](living-consistently-with-your-values.md).

A later letter takes up the harder question of whether one already-wise
person can meaningfully help another. Seneca's answer is that the wise
mutually "activate" each other's virtue through exercise and comparison,
much as wrestlers or musicians sharpen skill against equals — virtue, like
a fire that needs continued fuel to stay lit, needs continual exercise in
the company of the similarly virtuous to persist, even once fully attained
(Letter 109).
