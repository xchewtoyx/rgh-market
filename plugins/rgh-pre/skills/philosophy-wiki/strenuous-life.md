---
type: concept
title: The Strenuous Life
description: >
  The claim that bodily discipline and physical training are not vanity but
  preparation for virtue — hardening the body so the mind can endure hardship
  and act when life demands it.
sources:
  - title: "Discipline Is Destiny: The Power of Self-Control"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 2, 3"
---

Theodore Roosevelt, born a sickly, asthmatic, nearsighted boy, rebuilt himself
through exercise — walking, rowing, boxing, wrestling, hiking, hunting,
horseback riding, football — coining "the strenuous life." As president: "I
always tried to get a couple of hours' exercise in the afternoons." The contrast
with King George IV — gluttonous, too fat to sleep lying down, surprised by
death after decades of bodily abuse — and Babe Ruth's excess illustrate the
inverse: "The pleasure of excess is always fleeting... self-discipline is not a
rejection of pleasure but a way to embrace it" — bodily discipline is "the work
for which pleasure is the reward."

Humans are "an apex predator, a freakishly elite product of millions of years
of evolution," and letting that atrophy is not just personal but civic. Musonius
Rufus: "the philosopher's body should be well prepared for physical activity,
because often the virtues make use of this as a necessary instrument for the
affairs of life. We use the training common to both when we discipline ourselves
to cold, heat, thirst, hunger, meager rations, hard beds, avoidance of pleasures
and patience under suffering. For by these things... the body is strengthened
and becomes capable of enduring hardship, study and ready for any task."
Exercise is a rare "contained space" of pure self-determination — work and life
may not go well, but "working out is in our control." Seneca on treating the
body rigorously "so that it may not be disobedient to the mind."

Lou Gehrig treated baseball as "a higher calling" requiring control of and care
for the body — "both the obstacle and the vehicle for success." "Fitness was
almost a religion to him." He was "not born an athlete. He made himself one" —
through a German gymnastics club after being teased, through dependability and
toughness made "nonnegotiable assets" by a father's chronic excuses. "There
were players with more talent, with more personality, with more brilliance; but
nobody outworked him." He had "the same voice in his head that all of us do...
He just cultivated the strength — made a habit — of not listening to it.
Because once you start compromising, well, now you're compromised." The body is
"a metaphor. It's a training ground, a proving ground for the mind and the
soul." See [temperance as self-mastery](temperance-as-self-mastery.md) and
[patience as discipline](patience-as-discipline.md) for the wider virtue frame.
