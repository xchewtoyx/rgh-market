title: Temperance as Self-Mastery

  The cardinal virtue of self-restraint understood as command over oneself —
  not deprivation — without which freedom, the other virtues, and a stable
  life collapse into dependency or excess.

The body is untrustworthy about its own limits — it wants to eat past full,
drink past drunk, get what it wants now and deal with consequences later.
Temperament must intervene before the overshoot. Epictetus's banquet metaphor
supplies the working rule: "conduct yourself in life as if at a banquet. As
something being passed around comes to you, reach out your hand and take a
moderate helping. Does it pass you by? Don't stop it. It hasn't yet come?
Don't burn in desire for it, but wait until it arrives in front of you" —
applied to children, spouse, position, wealth, until one day it makes you
worthy of a banquet with the gods. The test for any pleasure is what it will
take out of you, not just what it gives: how you will feel in the refractory
period, hungover, months later in the mirror. Timotheus on dining at Plato's
house: "Your dinners are enjoyable not only when one is eating them but on the
morning after as well!" Abstinence (avoidance) is not the same as restraint
(responsibility) — restraint means knowing "the right length and no further,"
calibrated to your own body and life. Discipline is not punishment but a way
to avoid punishment; restraint is self-love, not self-denial, and actually
heightens enjoyment. "Seek yourself, not distraction. Be happy, not hedonistic.
Let the mind rule, not the body."

Part I of Holiday's account treats the body as one vehicle for discipline;
Part II argues the inner domain matters more. Thales's answer to who is
happy — "he who has a healthy body, a resourceful mind, and a docile
nature" — names temperament alongside physique. History is full of talented
people who mastered diet or waking time yet remained "profoundly a mess
everywhere else": riven by distraction, at the mercy of biases and bad
moods, given over to temptation and impulse. Command over the body does not
matter if intemperance of mind and mood condemns a person never to reach
full potential. An observer called Franklin Roosevelt a man with a "second-
class intellect and a first-class temperament" — given what polio took from
his body, offered as proof that temperament can outweigh raw intellect when
chaos demands steadiness. Head and heart together form a command system built
by evolution; the philosophical question is whether one commands them as
tools or is "jerked around like puppets." True self-control, on this view,
means moderation not only in what one does but in how one thinks, feels, and
comports oneself amid chaos.

Temperance — *temperantia*, *sophrosyne*, *enkrateia*, the Buddhist middle
way, Confucian *zhongyong*, Islamic *wasat* — names a recurring cross-
cultural law: a person must keep themselves in check or risk ruin through
imbalance, dysfunction, and dependency. The ancient diagnosis of the inner
conflict is *akrasia*: what you *can* do versus what you *should* do; what
you can get away with versus what is best. Temperance is not deprivation but
*command* of oneself physically, mentally, and spiritually — "giving
everything you have... and knowing what to hold back."

This virtue sits in a tight reciprocal relation with freedom. Privilege and
power are only blessings when paired with self-restraint; mistaking liberty
for license produces drift, vulnerability, and disorder. Eisenhower's gloss
captures the political version: freedom is "the opportunity for self-
discipline." Seneca's political version runs parallel: "Most powerful is he
who has himself in his own power." The charioteer image makes the same
point: speed without control guarantees a crash on the hairpin turns.

Without temperance as moderating force, the other cardinal virtues curdle
into vice. Courage without restraint becomes recklessness; justice without
restraint becomes fanaticism; wisdom without restraint becomes clever
rationalization of excess. Cicero's formula is blunt: supplement courage with
self-control, and "every ingredient for the happy life is yours" — courage
as defense against fear, self-control as liberation from immoderate craving.
The American founders argued similarly that republican government itself is
impossible without virtue in the people; a population without self-control
will break "the strongest cords of our Constitution."

Temperance is cultivated, not inherited. It is "predictive and
deterministic" in a philosophical sense: discipline makes good outcomes more
likely, and regardless of outcome ensures that you remain intact — while lack
of discipline endangers not only success but character. The undisciplined life
looks enviable from outside and is miserable within: greed moves the
goalposts so nothing can be enjoyed; recklessness and boundless ambition
produce short triumphs and long dependence. [Frugality toward the
superfluous](freedom-through-frugality.md) and [freedom from passion-
slavery](slavery-to-passions.md) are two of temperance's most concrete
expressions; [virtue exercised under public power](virtue-under-public-power.md)
is one of its hardest tests.

Tolstoy: "Those who think that they can live a high spiritual life whose bodies
are filled with idleness and luxuries are mistaken." Because we are mortal,
sustaining life requires certain physical acts — eating, sleeping, moving — and
how well we do these determines our condition. Temperance is explicitly not
about a life without pleasure; rather, self-discipline is partly practiced so
we might live longer, or — since longevity is not fully in our control — so we
might live well while we live. James Baldwin: "People pay for what they do...
and still more, for what they have allowed themselves to become. And they pay
for it simply: by the lives they lead." "The body keeps score" — daily
decisions are recorded in who we are, what we look like, how we feel.

Bodily temperance affects the mind, and physical intemperance prevents the
mind from working properly. Neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett's "body budget"
concept: the brain regulates the body, but a physically bankrupt body prevents
the brain from doing its job. Bad decisions, lack of resilience, distraction,
fear, and being caught in extreme emotion trace back to the body. The body is
the first step in self-mastery — "we treat it rigorously. We restrain it. We
dominate it. We treat it like a temple" — not for its own sake but so the body
may not overrun or override the mind; physical constraint is undertaken "to
free ourselves mentally and spiritually." No one enslaved to urges or sloth,
lacking strength or a good schedule, can build a great life or be of much use to
others; "those who tell themselves they are free to do anything will,
inevitably, be chained to something." "Discipline is how we free ourselves.
It is the key that unlocks the chains." This connects [voluntary discomfort as
inoculation](voluntary-discomfort-as-inoculation.md) to the wider claim that
[slavery to passions and craving](slavery-to-passions.md) begins in the body
before it colonizes the will.

Two ancient dictums form the operative pair: know thyself — physically and
mentally — and nothing in excess. Talent, brilliance, success, or power is not
a free pass to relax; the opposite. Because of our talents, resources, and
responsibilities, we must be *more* in command of ourselves, consciously and
constantly checking impulses. Working hard, thinking hard, and holding hard to
high standards consistently produces happiness and productivity; in the rare
instances of failure, we will be all right — not only because we know we did
our best, but because we will have the strength and character to endure
setbacks and the determination and balance to get back up. If we fall into
excess, lapse on standards, become careless, lazy, sloppy, or weak, and stop
attending to our improvement, Epictetus warns that we will stop making progress
and "live and die as ordinary, disappointing people."

Intemperance is not only self-harm. Plutarch on Alcibiades — once-promising
student of Socrates — whose lack of discipline and audacious way of life
destroyed him and "deprived the city of all his benefits on account of his
extravagance and licentiousness." Those who depend on us bear the cost when
gifted people squander themselves. Self-discipline is therefore not only
personal destiny but obligation: to our potential, our country, our cause, our
families, our fellow human beings, those who look up to us, and those who come
after us. Pete Carril's line captures the competitive form: "Rarely does a
person who competes with his head as well as his body come out second."
