---
type: concept
title: An Eclectic Approach to Wisdom
description: >
  Seneca's stance of drawing usable truth from rival philosophical schools,
  including ones he otherwise disagreed with, on the ground that good ideas
  belong to whoever can use them rather than to the school that first
  produced them.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), Letters 8, 12, 33, 45, 80"
---

Although Seneca identifies firmly as a Stoic, his letters quote Epicurus —
the leading rival school, whose ethics he otherwise rejects — more often
than any other single source, especially in the early letters, and he is
explicit about why. Defending the practice directly, he asks: "is there any
reason why you should consider [good sayings] to belong to Epicurus rather
than to the public?... the best sayings are held in common" (Letter 8),
and repeats the point almost verbatim closing Letter 12: "whatever is true
is my own." He frames the practice as similar to a senate procedure: "when
someone has stated a judgment that pleases me in part, I ask him to divide
his opinion, and I follow the part I approve" (Letter 21) — extracting the
sound component of a rival's view without adopting the whole system it
came from.

He is equally direct that this openness does not extend to conceding
authority over him to any predecessor, including Stoic ones. Letter 33
gives his fullest statement of intellectual independence, prompted by a
request to resume quoting pithy maxims: mature students should stop
leaning on memorized sayings and "stand on his own feet... Let there be
some distance between you and the book! How long will you be a pupil? Now,
be a teacher as well." He distinguishes remembering from knowing — "keeping
track of something you have committed to memory" versus "making all those
things your own, not having to depend on a model" — and states his stance
toward the philosophical tradition as a whole: "I will indeed use the
ancient road — but if I find another route that is more direct... I will
stake out that one. Those who advanced these doctrines before us are not
our masters but our guides. The truth lies open to all; it has not yet been
taken over. Much is left also for those yet to come."

Letter 45 restates the same independence even more bluntly: "I am nobody's
freedman; I bear no one's name but my own. I have great faith in the
opinions of the great, but I make some claim for my own views as well. For
even those great ones left us with questions, not answers." He also
justifies his frequent, approving citation of Epicurus on strategic
grounds: quoting Epicurus's own genuinely austere teaching is a way of
exposing people who invoke Epicurus's name as cover for self-indulgence,
since the real historical fare at Epicurus's Garden — porridge and water —
was as plain as anything a Stoic would recommend (Letter 21).

This stance is not casual eclecticism; it rests on the same underlying
value examined throughout the letters — that
[philosophy is judged by whether it actually improves how a person lives](philosophy-as-therapy-not-theory.md),
which makes school loyalty a poor criterion for accepting or rejecting an
idea compared to whether the idea itself holds up and helps.
