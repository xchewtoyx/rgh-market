---
type: concept
title: Admitting You Do Not Know
description: >
  The claim that openly admitting you do not know your way — rather than
  rushing with narrow ambition — makes available inner and outer allies from
  soulfulness and selflessness, while arrogant presumption of knowledge leads
  to impasse.
sources:
  - title: "Wherever You Go, There You Are: Mindfulness Meditation in Everyday Life"
    resource: "Jon Kabat-Zinn, Part 4"
---

Kabat-Zinn retells the Grimm fairy tale of three princes seeking the water of
life to cure their dying father. The two eldest brothers, greedy and
self-serving, each set out secretly hoping to become King. Each encounters a
dwarf on the road who asks where he is going; each treats the dwarf with scorn,
presuming he already knows the way. As punishment, each brother's path narrows
into a ravine where he becomes stuck — unable to go forward, back, or turn
around.

The dwarf is read symbolically as the higher powers of the soul — an inner
faculty the arrogant brothers cannot approach with kindness and wisdom. The
youngest brother, kind and caring, stops, dismounts, explains his father's
illness, and openly admits he has no idea where to look. The dwarf then tells
him where the water is and how to get it, and this brother listens carefully
and remembers.

It is useful to admit you do not know your way and to be open to help from
unexpected places; doing so makes available "inner and outer energies and
allies that arise out of your own soulfulness and selflessness." All three
brothers are internal figures of the same psyche — self-cherishing and
arrogance, ignoring the larger order of things, lead to an impasse where you
cannot go forward, back, or turn around, and you stay stuck, potentially
forever. The work of mindfulness is honoring and heeding our own dwarf energy
rather than rushing headlong with a mind driven by narrow ambition and personal
gain.

Seneca's stance in [an eclectic approach to wisdom](eclectic-approach-to-wisdom.md)
that great thinkers left questions, not answers, and [what is my way](what-is-my-way.md)
on resting in not-knowing rather than condemning oneself for it, extend the
same orientation into daily inquiry. Pair with [meditation as a way of
being](meditation-as-way-of-being.md) and [soul versus spirit](soul-versus-spirit.md)
on fairy tales as maps of inner development.
