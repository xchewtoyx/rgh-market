---
type: concept
title: Every Event Has Two Handles
description: >
  Epictetus's image for deliberately choosing which aspect of a difficult
  situation to take hold of — the aspect that can be carried, not the one
  that can't.
sources:
  - title: "Discourses, Fragments, Handbook"
    resource: "Epictetus, Discourses, Fragments, Handbook (tr. Robin Hard), Handbook 43"
---

"Everything has two handles," Epictetus writes, "one by which it can be
carried, one by which it can't" (Handbook 43). A brother's wrongdoing, for
instance, can be picked up by the handle "he is wronging me" — which
offers no usable grip, since dwelling on the wrong only produces anger — or
by the handle "he is my brother, he was brought up with me" — which is
just as true, and which actually gives something to act on. The situation
itself does not change depending on which handle is chosen; what changes is
whether the person handling it can do anything constructive with it.

The image is a compressed version of a wider Stoic point: most difficult
situations are not simple, single-description facts but bundles of true
descriptions, some of which invite a spiral into resentment or despair and
some of which invite constructive engagement, and which description gets
emphasized is a choice, not a discovery. It sits close to the doctrine that
[judgments, not events, are what disturb us](judgments-not-events-disturb-us.md) —
but where that principle diagnoses the general mechanism of disturbance,
the two-handles image supplies a concrete habit: before reacting, ask which
true thing about this situation is actually usable, and deliberately reach
for that one.

The same move appears throughout Epictetus in less compressed form. Facing
a request from an unsympathetic person, he recommends framing it not as "he
is being unreasonable" but by whatever framing lets you actually act
well — and facing insult, "abuse the person can only get a handhold" on
you through your own investment in externals; strip away that investment
and the insult has nowhere to attach (Discourses 1.25.28-30, developed
further in [the inner citadel](stoic-inner-citadel.md)). The habit of
looking for the workable handle rather than the unworkable one is also what
distinguishes treating a setback as
[training ground for virtue](adversity-as-training-ground.md) from simply
being defeated by it.
