title: Prepare for None of It to Work
description: >
  The Stoic disposition of trying with full effort while accepting that plans
  may fail entirely — holding yourself to your best, not the impossible, and
  moving on when the verdict arrives.
sources:
  - title: "The Obstacle Is the Way"
    resource: "Ryan Holiday, ch. 26"
  - title: "Discourses, Fragments, Handbook"
    resource: "Epictetus, Discourses, Fragments, Handbook (tr. Robin Hard)"

Epictetus: "You are invincible if nothing outside the will can disconcert you."
Perceptions can be managed, actions can be directed, but the world itself
cannot be controlled to the degree we'd like. We can perceive well and act
rightly and still fail. Nothing can ever prevent us from trying — but after
trying, with all creativity and dedication applied, some obstacles turn out to
be genuinely impossible to overcome. Some actions are rendered impossible,
some paths impassable; some things are simply bigger than us, and some
problems just keep recurring.

It's to our advantage that life doesn't always go our way. Emerson: a person
whose life is cushioned falls asleep, but a person who is "pushed, tormented,
defeated" gets "a chance to learn something," is put "on his wits, on his
manhood," gains facts, learns his own ignorance, is "cured of the insanity of
conceit," and gains "moderation and real skill." Every obstacle can be turned
upside down by using it as an opportunity to practice some other virtue or
skill — even if that virtue is simply accepting that bad things happen, or
practicing humility. "An infinitely elastic formula": whatever blocks the path
presents a new path with a new part of us to develop. If someone you love hurts
you, that's a chance to practice forgiveness; if your business fails, now you
can practice acceptance; if there's nothing else you can do for yourself, you
can at least try to help others.

The standard to hold yourself to is just your best — not the impossible. This
requires being willing to roll the dice and lose, and to prepare, at the end of
the day, for none of it to work. Anyone in pursuit of a goal runs into this
repeatedly: no amount of planning, effort, or patient persistence guarantees
that things will work out. The chapter rejects martyrdom ("the world could use
fewer martyrs") in favor of a disposition: try to get things done with
everything you've got, and whatever the verdict, accept it instantly and move
on to what's next. This extends [premeditating adversity](premeditation-of-adversity.md)
from rehearsing specific failures to accepting that total failure remains
possible after maximum effort, and pairs with [the art of acquiescence](art-of-acquiescence.md)
when the [dichotomy of control](dichotomy-of-control.md) has done all it can
and what remains is to accept the outcome without bitterness.
