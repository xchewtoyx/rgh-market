---
type: concept
title: Nightly Self-Examination
description: >
  The Stoic practice of reviewing one's own conduct at the end of each day
  — praising what went well, correcting what did not — as a concrete
  method for turning philosophical conviction into an actual habit.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), Letter 28, 83"
  - title: "Discourses, Fragments, Handbook"
    resource: "Epictetus, Discourses, Fragments, Handbook (tr. Robin Hard), 3.10"
---

Seneca describes and endorses a specific daily discipline, attributed
elsewhere to the philosopher Sextius (one of his own early teachers): a
practice of interrogating oneself each night about the day's conduct. He
frames this not as gentle reflection but as a formal internal proceeding:
"bring an accusation against yourself, as stringently as you can. Then
conduct the investigation. Take the role of the accuser first, then the
judge, and let that of the advocate come last. Offend yourself sometimes!"
(Letter 28). He connects this directly to the underlying Stoic diagnosis
that most people fail to improve simply because they never examine
themselves honestly enough to notice what needs fixing — self-knowledge is
treated as a precondition for [self-examination](living-consistently-with-your-values.md),
not something a person can be assumed to already have.

He describes his own daily routine in Letter 83, alongside a related
practice — recommending Lucilius keep a full daily diary — on the principle
that a life examined as if in the sight of others tends to be lived better:
"our lives should indeed be like that, lived as if in the sight of others...
God is in our minds; God enters into the midst of our thoughts." Reviewing
the day is presented as a natural consequence of taking that scrutiny
seriously: "no one looks back over his life... yet plans for the future
depend on the past."

Epictetus recommends the identical practice by name, quoting the
Pythagorean verses that describe it directly: "Let not sleep descend on
your weary eyes / Before having reviewed every action of the day... Where
did I go wrong? What did I do?" (Discourses 3.10.1-4). He frames this as
part of keeping context-specific judgments ready in advance — table
judgments at the table, bed judgments at bedtime — so that no situation
catches the mind unrehearsed, which connects the nightly review to the
broader Stoic habit of [premeditating adversity](premeditation-of-adversity.md)
in advance of the situations most likely to test one's principles.

The underlying point of the practice, across both writers, is that Stoic
ethics is not treated as a set of conclusions to be assented to once but as
a discipline that has to be renewed daily, since — as Seneca puts it
elsewhere — "a thing is never said too much when it has not been well
enough learned" (Letter 27). Nightly review is the concrete mechanism by
which the wider claim that
[philosophy must be lived rather than merely known](philosophy-as-therapy-not-theory.md)
actually gets put into practice.
