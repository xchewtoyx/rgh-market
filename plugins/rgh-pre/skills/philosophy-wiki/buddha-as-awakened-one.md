In Buddhism, the most important point is to be yourself, not to try to become
anything you are not already. Buddhism is fundamentally about being in touch
with your own deepest nature and letting it flow out unimpeded — waking up and
seeing things as they are. The word "Buddha" simply means one who has awakened
to his or her own true nature.

Mindfulness therefore does not conflict with any beliefs or traditions,
religious or scientific, and is not selling a new belief system or ideology. A
student's line captures the shift: "When I was a Buddhist, it drove my parents
and friends crazy, but when I am a buddha, nobody is upset at all." The
orientation is [trust your own experience](trust-your-own-experience.md) over
deferring to authority for validation, and [living consistently with your
values](living-consistently-with-your-values.md) as embodied character rather
than adopted doctrine.

This complements [courage to be yourself](courage-to-be-yourself.md) and
Seneca's insistence in [an eclectic approach to wisdom](eclectic-approach-to-wisdom.md)
that truth lies open and one should stand on one's own feet rather than lean
on memorized sayings. [Soul versus spirit](soul-versus-spirit.md) adds Zen's
"If you meet the Buddha, kill him" — conceptual attachment to Buddha or
enlightenment misses the mark; the path comes full circle to the ordinary and
everyday.
