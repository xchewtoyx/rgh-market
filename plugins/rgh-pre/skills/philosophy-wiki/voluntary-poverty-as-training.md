---
type: concept
title: Voluntary Poverty as Training
description: >
  Seneca's practice of periodically living on minimal food and rough
  clothing by choice, to test whether one's ease with wealth is real or
  merely untested, and to inoculate against the fear of poverty.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (tr. Graver and Long), Letters 17, 18, 20"
---

Seneca recommends a concrete practice, distinct from mere philosophical
approval of frugality: periodically and deliberately living for several
days on minimal, cheap food and rough clothing, "let your pallet be a real
one, your blanket really burlap, your bread actually hard and coarse"
(Letter 18). He is explicit that this is not to be confused with
performative asceticism — playing at "dinners of Timon" or "paupers' cells"
as a kind of theatrical self-display — and cites Epicurus's own practice of
periodic minimal eating as precedent, done specifically to test whether it
affected his "complete and maximal pleasure" (Letter 18). The resulting
state, he argues, is "not trivial and fleeting... but a stable and sure
pleasure," reached by bringing oneself to "that state which no adverse
fortune can undo."

The purpose is explicitly inoculation by direct trial, on the model of a
soldier training in peacetime: "he constructs a palisade even when no enemy
is at hand" (Letter 18). Someone who has never actually tested his ease
with poverty cannot know whether his professed indifference to wealth is
real or merely convenient. Letter 20 sharpens the test: given that Seneca
himself was wealthy, he raises directly the objection "can't one despise
wealth while it is in one's pocket?" and answers that yes, in principle,
being unspoiled by riches one didn't seek is itself a mark of some
strength — but the real test is whether a person "relishes his poverty" or
merely "declines to relish his wealth" under actual trial. Mere voluntary
austerity proves little unless pursued "by preference rather than of
necessity."

Letter 17 makes the broader case that poverty is not merely tolerable but
in some ways preferable for anyone serious about philosophy: it is
"untrammeled, carefree," since no one loots a poor person's house or fears
for belongings in a fire; "hunger is cheap; it is the palate that is
expensive." His sharpest line targets the common excuse that one should
achieve financial security first and turn to philosophy later: "even if
something prevents you from having a good living, nothing prevents you from
having a good death" — the one preparation that actually matters, readiness
for a good end, is being deferred by exactly the excuse that claims to be
securing the conditions for eventually pursuing it.

The practice is a concrete instance of the wider Stoic conviction that
[wealth and poverty are preferred or dispreferred indifferents](preferred-indifferents.md),
neither good nor bad in themselves — voluntary poverty is the experimental
method by which a person actually tests, rather than merely asserts, that
conviction, and it functions as a specific application of
[premeditating adversity](premeditation-of-adversity.md) to one particular
feared loss.
