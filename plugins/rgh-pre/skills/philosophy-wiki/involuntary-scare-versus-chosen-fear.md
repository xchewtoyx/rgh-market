William Faulkner draws the line cleanly: "Be scared. You can't help that.
But don't be afraid." A scare is an involuntary rush — forgivable, often
useful. Fear as a way of living is different: a state one lets rule after
"the shine of that feeling wore off." The Stoics called the immediate,
precognitive appearance of a threatening thing *phantasia* — the raw
impression that makes even a sage flinch at a loud noise. That first
appearance is not trusted and not something one is faulted for having; what
matters is whether one [tests the impression before assent](testing-impressions-before-assent.md)
or lets it harden into a governing mood.

"Be not afraid" is among the most repeated warnings in scripture — not a
denial that danger exists, but an injunction not to let *phantasiai* rule
the day. A Hebrew prayer from the early 1800s puts the same point in a
bridge metaphor: "The world is a narrow bridge, and the important thing is
not to be afraid" — easy to panic if you look down instead of forward.
FDR's 1933 inaugural, amid bank failures and mass unemployment, named "fear
itself" as the real enemy because fear would destroy what remained — turn
people against each other and block the cooperative solutions still
possible. Fear framed as choice, not inevitability.

As a ruling state, fear is a liability in an uncertain world. It disables
competence and connection: who does good work while afraid, sees clearly,
helps others, or loves well? The receiver who flinches drops the ball; the
artist who trembles before critics cannot deliver; the politician who fears
polls rarely decides rightly. Pericles, finding Athenian troops paralyzed
by thunder's portents, smashed two rocks together to show thunder is just
clouds doing the same — not full science, but close enough to break fear
down logically. When an eclipse blacked out the sun as his fleet departed,
he covered a steersman's head with his cloak and asked how the eclipse
differed except that "something larger than my cloak" caused the darkness —
defusing panic with analogy rather than oratory. Sobriety circles compress
the method as F.E.A.R.: "False Evidence Appearing Real" — explore the
impression, break it down, go to the root.

Pericles told the Athenians that bravery meant staying calm, rational, and
clear — learning "the meaning of what is sweet in life and what is
terrible, and then go out, undeterred, to meet what is to come." The
catastrophizing mind that extrapolates the worst case and underestimates
one's capacity to handle it is neither friend nor accurate reporter.
Marcus Aurelius's counsel matches: attend "not what your enemy sees and
hopes that you will, but what's really there." The philosophical move is
logical examination of the feared impression — the same discipline as
[testing impressions before assent](testing-impressions-before-assent.md),
applied where fear would otherwise become a permanent weather system rather
than a passing cloud.
