---
type: concept
title: Life Evaluation vs. Experienced Well-Being
description: >
  Two distinct constructs for judging whether life is going well — global
  retrospective satisfaction versus moment-to-moment affect — that diverge
  enough that optimizing one can miss or distort the other.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking, Fast and Slow"
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 39"
---

**Life evaluation** is a global judgment ("how satisfied are you with your
life as a whole?"). **Experienced well-being** is the quality of affect
across lived moments — what people actually feel hour by hour, not what they
say when asked to summarize.

These are not imperfect measures of one underlying thing; they track genuinely
different constructs. The same circumstance can move them in opposite
directions or by very different amounts: education raises life evaluation but
not experienced well-being (and correlates with more reported stress); living
with children costs more in daily feelings than in global satisfaction; being
rich improves life satisfaction well beyond the income level where experienced
well-being stops rising (~$75k household in high-cost areas, with zero average
gain in experience above that despite more purchasable pleasures).

For a PRE system judging whether an intervention "worked," this split is
load-bearing. An intervention that raises how someone *evaluates* their life
— through goal achievement, status, or a salient positive fact they retrieve
when surveyed — may do little for how they actually *experience* their days.
Conversely, reducing daily suffering (lower U-index: less time in negative
affect) may not move global satisfaction if the person's stated goals remain
unmet. Neither verdict alone answers whether sustainable quality of life
improved; see also
[thriving vs. surviving as the target objective](thriving-vs-surviving.md),
which is a related warning against mistaking absence-of-crisis for the real
bar.

Life-evaluation answers are also vulnerable to substitution heuristics: when
the global question is hard, people often answer an easier one (current mood,
whatever is salient — marriage status, a recent lucky find). A spike in
reported life satisfaction around marriage may reflect changing *probability
of thinking about marriage* when asked, not a multi-year tide of raised
happiness that later "adapts" away. Moment-to-moment measures (day
reconstruction, experience sampling) stay tied to present activities and
environment and are harder to game with a single salient fact.

Policy and personal PRE both need a hybrid stance: reduce experienced
suffering (especially depression and severe poverty, which amplify other
misfortunes in the currency of daily feelings) *and* take stated goals
seriously when judging whether a life is going well — see
[goal attainability as a satisfaction driver](goal-attainability-as-satisfaction-driver.md)
and [U-index for experienced suffering](u-index-for-experienced-suffering.md).
"Control your use of time" is among the most direct levers on experienced
well-being; goal alignment is among the most direct levers on life evaluation.
