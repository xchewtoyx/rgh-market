---
type: concept
title: Sustainable Effort Ceiling (the ~85% Rule)
description: >
  Deliberately operating below maximum perceived effort, treating slack as
  built-in recovery capacity rather than waste, produces better sustained
  output than running at 100% until burnout forces a collapse.
sources:
  - title: "The Canary Code: A Guide to Neurodiversity, Dignity, and Intersectional Belonging at Work"
    resource: "The Canary Code (Ludmila N. Praslova), Chapter 7"
  - title: "Discipline Is Destiny: The Power of Self-Control"
    resource: "Discipline Is Destiny (Ryan Holiday), ch. 6"
---

There's an intuitive but false equation between maximum visible effort and
maximum results: expecting sustained 100% effort looks like the
output-maximizing choice, but it reliably leads to burnout and *lower*
performance once the collapse arrives. Operating at something closer to 85%
of perceived maximum capacity — leaving deliberate slack rather than filling
every available unit of time/energy — is more sustainable and, measured over
any real time horizon longer than a single sprint, produces more total
output.

The reframe this requires: slack is not laziness or wasted capacity to be
optimized away — it *is* the recovery mechanism that keeps the system able
to keep delivering. A PRE system that judges itself only by
this-week's-output-maximized will systematically undervalue the slack that
protects next month's capacity, the same way a service that runs its
capacity at 100% utilization has no buffer for the unexpected and degrades
badly under any load spike.

This is a direct, general-purpose instance of the sustainable-quality-of-life
vs. short-term-output trade-off: an intervention that increases this week's
output by eliminating slack is not a net win if it borrows against next
month's capacity to do it. See also
[restorative rest vs. cheap rest](restorative-rest-vs-cheap-rest.md) for the
matching question on the recovery side of the same trade-off.

**Load management** is the disciplined version of this ceiling: sometimes the
harder choice is to rest rather than push — deliberately sitting star players
despite broadcast outrage because the wiser decision beats the popular one.
Skipping recovery and overtraining land in the same place: a short-term bargain
paid with interest later (the "falsest of economies" / indiscipline of
overwork). Teams that peak too early, rushed deliverables, and careers cut
short by preventable burnout share the same failure mode: undisciplined
refusal to manage load. Counter-models include pulling yourself when
performance slips mid-game and treating off-seasons as structural, not guilty
luxury. Absolute activity, of whatever kind, ultimately leads to bankruptcy.
