---
type: concept
title: Peak-End Rule and Duration Neglect
description: >
  The remembering self scores episodes by peak intensity and ending quality
  while largely ignoring duration — a System 1 bias that conflicts with
  stated values and produces systematically bad choices when used to plan
  future well-being.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking, Fast and Slow"
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 37, ch. 40"
---

The **remembering self** keeps score and drives many choices; the
**experiencing self** lives through the moments. Their interests do not
always coincide. Two System 1 rules govern how episodes are stored and
evaluated:

- **Peak-end rule**: an episode is judged mainly by its most intense moment
  and how it ended, not by the whole profile over time.
- **Duration neglect**: how long an episode lasted gets little or no weight
  in retrospective evaluation.

Together these biases favor a short burst of intense joy over a long stretch
of moderate happiness, and make people fear a brief intense-but-tolerable
suffering more than a much longer moderate pain. People accept long mild
unpleasantness if the ending improves; they abandon long happy periods likely
to end badly. In the cold-hand study, subjects chose to repeat the trial that
left the better *memory* despite objectively more total pain — a mistake if
the goal is minimizing suffering for the experiencing self.

"We believe that duration is important, but our memory tells us it is not."
Because time is the ultimate finite resource, rules that govern evaluation of
the *past* are poor guides for *decisions* about how to spend the future.
Advice framed as "you will regret it" invokes anticipated regret — the
remembering self's verdict — which is not always correct for total
well-being.

For PRE evaluation of interventions, peak-end effects explain why a sprint
that ends on a high note can feel like a win in retrospect while the
experiencing self paid a high cumulative cost, and why a sustainable routine
that never produces a dramatic peak can be undervalued.

The same rules scale to **whole lives treated as stories**. Doubling the
length of a uniformly happy fictitious life had no effect on desirability
judgments — "total happiness" was read as a typical period, not an integral
over years. Adding five mildly happy years to a very happy life *lowered*
evaluations (a less-is-more effect): the slightly weaker ending dragged the
whole narrative down. We care whether estranged relatives reconciled before
death, whether a marriage "ended badly," whether a dead person's reputation
was later humiliated — narrative facts that never entered their experienced
moments. "A story is about significant events and memorable moments, not
about time passing. Duration neglect is normal in a story, and the ending
often defines its character." When duration seems to matter (longer labor,
longer vacation), what actually moved judgment was usually changed ending
quality from the longer trajectory, not duration itself.
