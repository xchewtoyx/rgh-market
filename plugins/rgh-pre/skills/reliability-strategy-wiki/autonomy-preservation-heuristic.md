---
type: concept
title: Autonomy-Preservation Heuristic (the "Burrito Test")
description: >
  A concrete litmus test for whether a support structure genuinely
  preserves a person's autonomy or has quietly slid into paternalism —
  ask whether they can do an ordinary, harmless, self-directed thing
  (heat up a burrito at midnight) without needing permission.
sources:
  - title: "Neurodiversity For Dummies"
    resource: "Neurodiversity For Dummies (John Marble, Khushboo Chabria, Ranga Jayaraman), Chapter 9"
---

Support and care structures (caregiving arrangements, group homes, but
equally any system built to help someone — accommodations, well-meaning
oversight from family or employer) can drift from empowering a person into
controlling them, often without anyone involved noticing the shift, because
each individual restriction looks reasonable ("it's for their own good,"
"it's more convenient this way").

The "Burrito Test," developed within the intellectual-disability support
community: **can the person heat up and eat a microwave burrito at midnight
if they want to?** A trivial, harmless, self-directed act. If the honest
answer is no — if some rule, schedule, or gatekeeper stands between the
person and an ordinary autonomous choice that costs no one anything — that's
a signal the support structure has stopped serving the person's autonomy and
started substituting the caregiver's judgment for it.

The value of the test is its concreteness: "does this system respect
autonomy" is easy to answer yes to in the abstract while failing on the
ground. A small, checkable, low-stakes probe surfaces the gap between stated
intent and actual effect. It generalizes beyond formal care settings to any
arrangement — a well-meaning employer accommodation, a family's
"protective" habits — that is nominally in service of someone's wellbeing:
if it can't survive the question "would this let them do the burrito-at-midnight
equivalent in this context," the arrangement is protecting something other
than the person's autonomy, whatever it's called.
