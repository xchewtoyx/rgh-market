title: Restorative Rest vs. Cheap Rest
description: >
  Distinguishes recovery activities that genuinely rebuild capacity from
  low-effort activities that give fleeting relief but leave a person more
  depleted, as a way to judge whether a self-care intervention is a real
  investment in sustainability or a short-term patch.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Megan Anna Neff), Chapter 3 (Emotional Self-Care)"
---

The test for whether an activity counts as real self-care is not whether it
matches a social template for rest (spa days, vacations) but whether it
actually rejuvenates the person doing it. Two activities that look similar
from the outside — both "resting" — can have opposite effects on recovery
capacity:

- **Restorative rest**: activities that genuinely replenish energy, even if
  they look unconventional (reading, a favorite podcast, hiking, time with a
  special interest). The marker is that the person comes out of the activity
  with more capacity than they went in with.
- **Cheap rest**: quick-fix activities (e.g. bingeing television) that
  deliver momentary gratification but leave the person more drained
  afterward — output without genuine recovery.

The distinction gives a concrete evaluation criterion for any proposed
recovery intervention: don't ask whether it *looks* restful or matches a
social norm of self-care, ask whether it measurably rebuilds capacity
afterward. Treating "I did something during my break" as sufficient — without
checking the after-state — lets cheap rest silently substitute for
restorative rest, which compounds toward burnout even though the calendar
shows "rest" was taken.

This also reframes "pushing through" without adequate rest as a case of
prioritizing visible short-term output (staying busy, not falling behind)
over the sustainable capacity that real recovery protects.
