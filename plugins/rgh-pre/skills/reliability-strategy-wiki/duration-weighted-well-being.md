---
type: concept
title: Duration-Weighted Well-Being
description: >
  A decision-oriented conception of well-being that weights every lived
  moment by its duration (plus time spent dwelling on it or downstream
  misery it causes), rather than letting memorable peaks and endings
  dominate the score.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking, Fast and Slow"
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 40"
---

Duration-weighted well-being treats **all moments alike**, memorable or not.
A moment can still count for more if:

- people spend real time **dwelling** on it afterward (that dwelling time
  adds to its duration), or
- it **alters many subsequent moments** (an hour of violin practice
  enhancing years of listening; a brief trauma weighted by the total duration
  of PTSD-driven misery it causes).

Whether a moment was "memorable" or "meaningful" can only be judged after
the fact — sincere predictions like "I will always remember this" are often
wrong.

This framework is logically compelling for policy and personal PRE but not a
complete theory: people identify with their remembering self and care about
their story, so well-being cannot ignore what people *want* — but neither
can it ignore what actually happens in their lives. Rankings of how bad a
condition is (blindness, deafness, kidney failure) can differ depending on
whether severity is measured by fear, experienced suffering, or intensity of
desire for relief.

Use duration weighting when asking whether an intervention improved
**sustainable** quality of life: sum the moments, don't let a strong finish
or a single salient peak launder a long middle of strain. See also
[peak-end rule and duration neglect](peak-end-rule-and-duration-neglect.md)
for the biases this corrects, and
[life evaluation vs. experienced well-being](life-evaluation-vs-experienced-well-being.md)
for the broader two-verdicts frame.
