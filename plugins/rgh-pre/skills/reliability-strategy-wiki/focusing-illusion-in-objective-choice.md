---
type: concept
title: Focusing Illusion in Objective Choice
description: >
  When choosing goals or interventions, attention to a salient change makes
  its future impact on well-being look larger than it will be — especially
  for one-time purchases or status shifts whose novelty fades — while
  ongoing attention-demanding activities are less overrated.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking, Fast and Slow"
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 40"
---

**Miswanting** (Gilbert & Wilson): bad choices from errors in affective
forecasting — predicting poorly how future circumstances will feel.
**Focusing illusion** (focalism): anything you focus on seems more important
than it will be once attention moves on (WYSIATI applied to the future).

Classic contrast: a new car versus joining a weekly social group. Both are
novel and exciting at first, but people overrate the car's long-term benefit
because attention to it fades, while poker nights, book clubs, tennis, or
learning cello keep demanding attention and are not overrated the same way.
The illusion biases choices toward goods and experiences that are initially
exciting even when their appeal adapts away, because time and adaptation are
neglected in the forecast.

For PRE objective-setting and intervention triage, this is a failure mode
when evaluating trade-offs: protecting or pursuing a salient upgrade (title,
purchase, relocation) can look like a high-return move for life quality while
underweighting durable levers on experienced well-being (time use, recurring
connection, skill practice). Before committing scarce capacity to a focal
change, ask whether its benefit is mostly **attention novelty** or sustained
daily experience — and whether the remembering self's anticipated pride
outweighs the experiencing self's cumulative hours. See also
[goal attainability as a satisfaction driver](goal-attainability-as-satisfaction-driver.md)
and [outcomes vs. activity as the evaluation signal](outcomes-vs-activity-signal.md).
