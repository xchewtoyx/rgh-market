---
type: concept
title: Reward-Trap vs. Genuine Duty
description: >
  A diagnostic for deciding whether to keep pouring capacity into a
  demanding commitment — ask whether staying is actually required, or
  whether it's the rewards (status, income, momentum) doing the holding,
  since the latter is a voluntary trade dressed up as an obligation.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius, Letters 17, 19, 22 (trans. Graver & Long)"
---

People who stay in an unsustainable, demanding role well past the point it
serves them rarely frame it honestly as "I like what I'm getting from
this." They frame it as necessity: "I have to," "duty requires it," "I can't
leave yet." Seneca's diagnosis of this pattern (Letter 22): it is usually
not duty but reward that holds people in place — status, income,
momentum, the fear of losing what the role has built up around them — and
"their detention is voluntary" even when it doesn't feel that way.

The practical test: separate what the commitment actually *requires* of you
from what you'd be giving up by leaving. If the honest answer is that
leaving costs you rewards rather than violates an obligation, the
commitment is a trade you're choosing to keep making, not a duty binding
you — and it should be evaluated as a trade (is this still worth it?) rather
than treated as fixed.

Two follow-on moves once the trap is named:

- **Stop before you can't.** Don't wait for a dramatic exit; the more
  immediate and available move is to simply stop taking on *new*
  entanglements while winding down existing ones — "content yourself with
  the tasks you have already taken on... don't go out of your way to take
  on more, or you'll lose your excuse" (Letter 22). Escalating commitment
  makes the eventual disentanglement harder every time it's deferred.
- **Prefer loosening the knot to cutting the rope.** Where a clean, total
  exit isn't available or wise, a gradual, deliberate withdrawal toward
  safety is the intermediate move — matching the level of commitment down
  in steps rather than treating "stay at full commitment" and "quit
  everything at once" as the only two options.
