**Experiential avoidance** is the habitual avoidance of one's own emotions,
thoughts, feelings, and body sensations — walling them off, suppressing,
numbing, or pretending they are not there. It often begins as a
understandable response to painful moods: treating unhappiness as a threat
activates the brain's avoidance system, dampening curiosity and engagement
while driving the mind to avoid its own productions.

The effect disconnects a person not only from negative feelings but possibly
from the full range of feeling, positive and negative — reinforcing a sense
of being out of touch with the experience of being alive. Research across
100+ studies links many forms of emotional disturbance to unhealthy efforts
to escape emotions.

Analogy: turning up the car radio to blank out a strange engine noise —
effective at blanking the noise, ineffective at preventing the engine
seizing up. In the long run, unpleasant feelings remain and still trigger
habitual reactions that can turn passing unpleasantness into persistent
suffering — unless awareness catches them.

**Calibration impact.** A person out of touch with bodily signals may not
notice emotional reactions until they have already pulled mood downward —
too late for timely action. Others may see tension in posture, face, or
blood pressure while the person reports "passing annoyances" or wears a
tight smile resembling a grimace. State of mind is then controlled by body
and emotion to a degree that would alarm the person if they could read it.

When depressed, people may feel **aversion to the body's own signals**
(tension, exhaustion, inner chaos) and hope turbulence subsides without
engaging — but avoidance produces more unconscious contraction in body and
mind. Bodies function as highly sensitive emotion detectors giving moment-
to-moment readouts, yet many people are "too busy thinking" to notice,
having learned to pursue goals through the head rather than attend to the
body for learning or healing.

Illustrative pattern: driving home with white-knuckle grip and tensed
shoulders without noticing the tension; a traffic incident then triggers
hot face, honking, and resentful rumination that deepens the state — the
body was already signaling threat before the mind owned an emotion.
