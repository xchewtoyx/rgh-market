title: Stim vs. Compulsion Signal Distinction
description: >
  Repetitive behavior can be self-soothing sensory regulation or
  anxiety-driven compulsion; distress to the person doing it — not observer
  discomfort — is the key discriminator for state reading.
sources:
  - title: "Is This Autism? A Guide for Clinicians and Everyone Else"
    resource: "Is This Autism? A Guide for Clinicians and Everyone Else (Donna Henderson, Sarah Wayland, Jamell White), ch. 9"
---

Autism and OCD both involve repetition, intense thoughts, sensory
hyper-responsivity, and a "not right" feeling — so the same outward behavior
can be read two ways:

- A **[stim](stimming-as-state-signal.md)** does not distress the person
  doing it (though it may distress observers); it may be pleasurable or
  self-soothing.
- A **compulsion** distresses or interferes with the person's life; it
  temporarily relieves anxiety tied to intrusive thoughts.

Example: repeatedly opening and closing a door — in OCD without autism, a
threat-warding ritual; in autism without OCD, it may be pleasurable
regulation. An autistic person with both conditions may struggle to tell
whether [sensory craving](sensory-craving.md) or compulsion is driving the
behavior.

Sensory reaction can **originate** compulsive-looking behavior without an
obsessive thought: a child averse to fleece texture triggered repeated
handwashing that then acquired OCD-like momentum.

Misreading a stim as pathology — or an interest as an "obsession" — degrades
state telemetry. Ask: does this behavior distress **the person performing
it**, or only others? Does stopping it raise or lower their arousal? See
[distinguishing anxiety from sensory overload](distinguishing-anxiety-from-sensory-overload.md)
when the driver may be environmental rather than cognitive.
