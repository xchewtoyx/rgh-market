Masking and trauma can intensify [alexithymia](alexithymia.md) even when
they didn't cause it in the first place: long-term suppression of authentic
expression, chronic hypervigilance, and dissociation all make it harder to
feel internal signals and to trust them once felt. This is part of why the
[primary vs. secondary alexithymia](primary-vs-secondary-alexithymia.md)
distinction matters for confidence calibration — a reading that has grown
noticeably harder to make than it used to be is itself informative.

Masking also works at the behavioural level, independent of alexithymia:
suppressing natural self-soothing responses (avoiding eye contact, stimming,
moving) to look less conspicuous increases sensory overload and body
disconnection, pulling a person further from the sensations self-care and
self-attunement rely on. Masking strains executive function — remembering
others' behaviour to mimic, applying social scripts, planning for new
situations — on capacity that is already limited in many neurodivergent
people, which partly explains why camouflaging is so exhausting.

Being in a near-constant fight-or-flight state from masking leaves no
capacity to ask "How am I feeling right now?" Helpful self-reflection needs
a calm, safe environment without looming "danger." In safe settings,
[unhelpful self-monitoring](unhelpful-self-monitoring.md) of eye contact and
tone can release, freeing space to monitor what actually matters: how one
feels, what one needs, what would help. See [self-attunement](self-attunement.md) for the
practice of deliberately reconnecting with what a masked signal is actually
saying.

Culturally, the same dynamic shows up as a lifelong habit of reflexively
answering "I'm fine" to any check-in, regardless of the true internal
state.

**Camouflaging** — changing outward presentation to blend in, also called
masking or "pretending to be normal" — includes studying others, following
scripts, forced eye contact, hiding stims, and ignoring sensory sensitivities.
Everyone camouflages sometimes; many autistic people do so far more
frequently, for longer, and with far greater effort, with planning before
interactions and fatigue persisting long after. Devon Price frames it less
as choice and more as burden from others' expectations plus internalized
shame. Analyzing social situations consumes energy that would otherwise
support noticing internal state — see [unhelpful self-monitoring](unhelpful-self-monitoring.md).
Camouflaging during assessment or therapy itself is possible; clinical
awareness of camouflaging aims at balance between functional use and being
able to drop the mask, not teaching more camouflaging.

Practiced for long enough, reflexive "I'm fine" doesn't just hide the state
from others — it stops the person noticing it themselves, because the honest
answer is never rehearsed. Cultural norms around stoicism and "fake it
till you make it" actively train this habit, particularly in workplace
settings, producing a lifelong performance of "I'm fine" that becomes
progressively harder to distinguish from an actual reading, even for the
person giving it. See [affect labeling](affect-labeling.md) for a direct
counter-practice (naming the actual current state instead of the reflexive
default). Interrupting self-critical thoughts during mindfulness practice
often replay the chiding voices of parents, teachers, coaches, or preachers
who tried to socialize you into someone you're not — anxiety "speaking up
for your mask." Labeling ("Thinking," "Worrying," "Planning") without guilt
builds a positive association with noticing thoughts rather than a
Pavlovian negative one. A second intrusive source is evolutionarily wired
dissatisfaction (desire) — especially noisy for autistics in a world past
survival scarcity; practice includes recognizing when you are actually
satisfied and separating that from thoughts telling you otherwise.
