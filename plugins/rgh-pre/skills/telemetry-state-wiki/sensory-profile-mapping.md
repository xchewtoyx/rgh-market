A sensory profile has two components, mapped separately across **eight**
sensory systems — the familiar five (vision, hearing, touch, smell, taste)
plus three body-position and internal channels:
[interoception](interoception.md), [proprioception](proprioception.md),
and [the vestibular sense](vestibular-sense.md). Map **triggers** (what
causes overload) and **preferences** (what soothes or calms) within each
channel. Also note which response pattern dominates per channel:
[over-responsivity](sensory-over-responsivity.md),
[under-responsivity](sensory-under-responsivity.md), or
[sensory craving](sensory-craving.md). Mapping is
complicated by paradoxical or conflicting sensory experiences, and by body-
awareness difficulties that make it hard to even decode what the sensory
experience is in the first place — see [interoception](interoception.md).

**Identifying triggers** is described as detective work, using several
methods together:

- Keep a sensory-trigger journal, logging feelings alongside associated
  experiences through the day.
- Do a mindful environment scan the moment discomfort arises: is it a
  bright light, a strong smell, background noise?
- Notice muscle tension, clenching, or bracing, and what is happening
  around you at that moment — the body often flags a trigger before it is
  consciously identified.
- Seek professional support (e.g. an occupational therapist) to help
  identify triggers that are hard to pin down alone.

Undetected sensory overload is described as being "like odorless poison...
slowly creeping in" — it may register only as vaguely "feeling bad" or as a
headache, with no obvious cause, unless the mapping work has already been
done to make the connection visible.

**Individual variance.** "Sensory differences" is a broad label for nervous-
system variance from the bell curve — two autistic people may share the
diagnostic root but have opposite aversions (hair-brushing vs. soft-food
texture; blue jeans vs. perfume; electrical buzzing vs. visual overwhelm).
Discovery is often slow: childhood memories resurface to explain old
aversions. One person may find store music unbearably intrusive while most
people filter it to background; another may manage a loud household without
registering background chaos at all — an impervious filter, not absence of
stimulus. The world is tuned to neurotypical filtering; without the
expected volume knob, the environment does not lower itself.

**Identifying preferences** is often harder than identifying triggers:
reflect on how sounds, textures, smells, tastes, and visuals affect mood and
energy; keep the same kind of journal; get feedback from trusted people on
how they observe your responses to environments; use sensory-preference
checklists. There is no universal "right" preference — one visually
sensitive person may want dim, uncluttered lighting, while another
sensory-seeking person may want bright lights and movement.

For diagnostic and self-monitoring purposes, what matters is that the
individual perceives, experiences, or responds to sensory input atypically —
whether the root cause is physiological sensitivity or observable behavioral
reaction is often unclear, but the lived signal pattern is what the map
must capture.

Systems integrate constantly; crowd overwhelm, for example, often combines
visual, auditory, and olfactory triggers. A single "bad mood" reading may
reflect several channels loading at once — see
[distinguishing anxiety from sensory overload](distinguishing-anxiety-from-sensory-overload.md).
