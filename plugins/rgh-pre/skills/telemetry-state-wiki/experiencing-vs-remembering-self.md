title: Experiencing Self vs Remembering Self
description: >
  Two evaluators inside one person — the self that lives each moment and
  the self that stores and judges the story — which often disagree and
  make confidence in a state reading depend on which one is answering.
sources:

  - title: "Thinking, Fast and Slow"

Kahneman distinguishes two selves:

- The **experiencing self** answers "Does it hurt now?" or "How is this
  moment?" — the channel [micro interoceptive check-ins](micro-interoceptive-check-ins.md)
  and moment-to-moment practice try to read.
- The **remembering self** answers "How was it, on the whole?" — keeps the
  score, governs learning from the past, and drives many decisions because
  memories are all we keep.

These selves can diverge sharply. A bad ending can "ruin the memory" of an
experience while the experiencing self already had forty minutes of genuine
enjoyment the ending cannot undo — confusing experience with memory of it
is a compelling cognitive illusion. Global life-satisfaction questions
("How satisfied are you with your life?") address the remembering self and
can be a poor witness to experienced well-being in any given period.

For calibration: notice which question you (or someone else) is actually
answering. A retrospective "that was awful" may reflect [peak-end
weighting](peak-end-duration-bias.md) rather than the sum of moments lived.
A real-time "I'm fine" may be accurate for the experiencing self while load
is building internally — see [sensory over-responsivity](sensory-over-responsivity.md).

The remembering self has no direct access to duration-weighted totals; it
stores prototypical peak and end moments. Decisions often maximize future
memories rather than future experience — "the tyranny of the remembering
self."
