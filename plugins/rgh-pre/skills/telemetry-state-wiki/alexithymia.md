Alexithymia ("without words for emotion") is a fairly stable pattern in how a
person takes in and works with emotional information, existing on a spectrum
from mild to pronounced rather than as a binary diagnosis. It is a major
source of low confidence in a state reading: the underlying signal is
present, but — in the author's framing — it shows up "more like static than
a clear radio station," with a foggier translation layer between sensation
and word.

**Core difficulty.** A person with alexithymia often cannot cleanly
distinguish an emotion from a co-occurring body sensation — for example,
whether a feeling is anxiety or low blood sugar, sadness or fatigue, fear or
just too much coffee. The **same bodily sensations** can receive different
labels depending on context — stomach clenching and a pounding heart read as
"excitement" at the front of a roller-coaster line but "anxiety" during a
coworker confrontation. Accurate labeling needs both internal-state
awareness and situational context; without both, the person may localize
feeling to a body region ("I feel it in this part of my body") without
being able to name or describe the emotion, even with otherwise fluent
speech. When [flat or restricted affect](facial-expression-mismatch.md)
co-occurs, both verbal and nonverbal channels for conveying pain may be
lost, inviting clinical misread of intensity. When asked directly what they're feeling, the honest
answer is frequently "I don't know" or a broad catch-all word like
"stressed," even though something is clearly sensed. Finding the specific
word is a separate, harder step than sensing that something is present —
which means a low or absent verbal reading does not imply a low or absent
underlying signal.

**Prevalence.** Roughly 1 in 10 people in the general population meet
criteria; rates are far higher among Autistic people (~50%, vs ~5% of
non-autistic people in one meta-analysis) and reported at 20-40% in adult
ADHD studies. For an agent trying to read state, this means alexithymia
should be treated as a common, not rare, source of degraded signal quality —
especially for neurodivergent people.

**Core features** (researcher consensus cluster), useful as a checklist when
suspecting alexithymia is degrading a state reading:

1. **Difficulty identifying feelings** — trouble working out what you're
   feeling, or telling emotions apart from body sensations.
2. **Difficulty describing feelings** — even when something is sensed
   internally, finding words for it is a separate hurdle; the default
   answer is often a broad term like "stressed" or "off," or "I don't know."
3. **Externally oriented thinking** — attention runs outward toward facts,
   tasks, and surroundings rather than inward toward the emotional world.
   This is a thinking-style difference, not evidence of not caring about
   feelings.
4. **Restricted imaginative processes** — a more concrete, practical
   thinking style and limited fantasy life, in many (not all) people with
   alexithymia; debated whether this is core to the construct or a
   related-but-separate trait.

Not every instance of alexithymia is the same kind of thing: see
[primary vs. secondary alexithymia](primary-vs-secondary-alexithymia.md) for
the trait/state distinction, and
[interoception](interoception.md) for the mechanism thought to underlie it.
Self-report tools for gauging where you fall on the spectrum are covered in
[alexithymia self-report measures](alexithymia-self-report-measures.md).

**Decoupling model.** In one proposed mechanism, physiological arousal from
an emotional state is not integrated with conscious awareness of that
arousal — a disruption between subjective emotional experience and
physiological emotional arousal. Neuroimaging and skin-conductance studies
are probing why many autistic people struggle to read their own internal
states; the lived difficulty often outruns what those measures explain.

**Distinct from, but often mistaken for, autism itself.** Alexithymia and
autism are separate constructs that frequently co-occur. Not all autistic
people have alexithymia, but many do. Recent work suggests alexithymia may
be the key factor regulating autistic people's difficulties knowing what to
do with face and voice (Oakley et al. 2022). Difficulties historically
attributed to autism itself — including apparent lack of empathy or
difficulty connecting with one's own emotional states — may actually be
better explained by co-occurring alexithymia. When reading an autistic
person's apparent emotional state (or apparent lack of one), it is worth
checking whether alexithymia, not autism per se, is the better
explanation for a flat or hard-to-read signal.

**Calibration failures from alexithymia.** Because the translation layer
is degraded, state readings can be systematically wrong in predictable ways:

- **Flat intensity reporting** — pain or distress rated the same regardless
  of severity (e.g. always "5" at a doctor visit, unable to distinguish a
  stubbed toe from a broken bone).
- **Late or absent sadness detection** — some emotions stay unnamed until
  an extreme low point, while others (e.g. happiness, elation) remain
  identifiable.
- **Outsider–insider mismatch** — others describe the person as anxious
  while the person would not self-identify that way, even when anxiety has
  driven burnout, meltdown, or shutdown episodes.

**Cognitive substitution.** A common coping pattern among alexithymic
autistic people: when emotions cannot be felt directly, reason through them
instead — analysing environmental risk factors to substitute for feeling
fear, or "logic-ing" whether distress is warranted by a situation's
severity. This is time-consuming, exhausting, and frequently wrong (ER visits
for minor injuries, ignoring serious ones, running into danger while
avoiding safety). It can also fail in domains where feeling is irreducible —
relationships, where love cannot be fully reverse-engineered from rules.
When you notice heavy top-down reasoning about what you "should" be
feeling, treat that as a low-confidence reading and reach for
[interoceptive](interoception.md) methods instead.
