Where [alexithymia](alexithymia.md) makes describing feelings harder than
sensing them, the gap can be narrowed by deliberately building a richer
vocabulary for emotion, separately from working on the sensing itself:

- Use visual aids — an emotion matrix, feeling wheel, or feeling list — as
  a menu of specific words to check against a vague sensation.
- Read fiction, which models characters' emotional landscapes and offers
  language for states that might otherwise stay unnamed.
- Use music or photo prompts to evoke a feeling deliberately, then practice
  naming the response it produces.
- Journal, to notice patterns and triggers over time rather than relying on
  in-the-moment recall alone.

**Structured recall exercise.** For each target emotion (e.g. happiness,
sadness, anger, anxiety, excitement): recall the life-peak memory and a
recent instance; describe the situation and the bodily felt sense; then
notice cross-emotion patterns. This builds a personal catalogue of
[physical signatures](emotional-physical-signature.md) tied to named
categories. Follow with reflection on childhood suppression training — many
autistic people were taught to eat anxiety-provoking foods and enter
sensory-overload environments, training them to ignore their own emotional
signals. Ask when and how emotions were taught to be ignored, and how that
shapes the present relationship to feeling.

Progress on this is nonlinear — plateaus are as normal as breakthroughs.
Building vocabulary pairs naturally with
[affect labeling](affect-labeling.md), which is the real-time practice this
vocabulary gets used in, and with [body scanning](body-scan.md), which
supplies the raw sensations the vocabulary needs to attach to.
