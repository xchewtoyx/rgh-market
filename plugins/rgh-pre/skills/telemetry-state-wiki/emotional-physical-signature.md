The brain compresses a large amount of background analysis into a single
labeled emotional output, which obscures the root cause of a feeling —
described as "emotional camouflage." Because tracing an emotion back to its
origin is hard, a more reliable diagnostic route is the emotion's physical
signature: the pattern of bodily sensation it reliably produces, read the
way a symptom is used to diagnose a medical condition. Example signatures:

- **Panic** — a surge of energy with an urge to flee.
- **Anger** — a sense of boiling blood, muscular tension, an urge to
  attack.
- **Fear** — racing heart and thoughts, an urge to hide.
- **Disgust** — a stomach recoil.
- **Sadness** — a sense of emptiness or weakness, an urge to withdraw.
- **Excitement** — feeling energised, optimistic, open.

These signatures are chemically mediated (e.g. adrenaline is associated
with panic and anger, dopamine with excitement), which is part of why they
recur reliably enough to be diagnostic.

**Calibration caveat.** Butterflies, racing blood, lightheadedness, sweaty
palms, and pounding heart overlap across many positive and negative states
(excitement, anger, anxiety, love) and across healthy versus unhealthy
versions of the same negative emotion (e.g. butterflies in both unhealthy
anxiety and healthy concern). Physical symptoms alone cannot reliably judge
whether a negative feeling is healthy or unhealthy. The main variation
between categories is **intensity**: sensations tend to be more intense,
uncomfortable, and debilitating with unhealthy emotions, and uncomfortable
sensations tend to last longer. Signatures are useful heuristics, not
standalone diagnoses — pair with context, theme, and behaviour.

Building a personal catalogue of signatures — through [body scanning](body-scan.md) or a dedicated exercise
of recalling emotional memories and noting the bodily changes against a
[calm baseline](calm-baseline-state.md) — makes it possible to identify an
emotion from its felt pattern even when a clean verbal label doesn't come
easily, which is especially useful where
[alexithymia](alexithymia.md) makes naming hard. In practice, emotions
rarely occur in isolation — see [the emotional storm](emotional-storm.md)
for how multiple signatures typically overlap at once.
