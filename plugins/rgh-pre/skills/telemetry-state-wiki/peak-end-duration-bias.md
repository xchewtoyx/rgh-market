---
type: concept
title: Peak-End and Duration Bias
description: >
  Retrospective judgments of an episode weight its worst moment and its
  ending heavily and often ignore how long it lasted, distorting confidence
  in how bad or good it actually was.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking, Fast and Slow"
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 36"
---

When people rate "total pain" or "how was it overall" after an episode, two
biases dominate:

- **Peak-end rule** — the global rating approximates the average of pain or
  pleasure at the worst moment and at the end.
- **Duration neglect** — how long the episode lasted has little or no
  effect on the retrospective rating.

A shorter procedure with a bad ending can be remembered as worse than a
longer one that ended gently, even when the longer episode included more
total discomfort. In the cold-hand experiment, most participants chose to
repeat a longer trial with a milder ending over a shorter trial — governed
by which memory they disliked less, not by total experienced pain.

For state reading and confidence calibration:

- After an event, a harsh retrospective summary may overweight the final
  minutes or the peak spike — check against moment-by-moment notes or
  [day reconstruction](day-reconstruction-method.md) before treating the
  memory as the full truth.
- When designing experiences (medical procedures, difficult conversations),
  a gentler ending may improve remembered state without changing total
  experienced load — relevant when the remembering self will drive future
  choices.

This bias connects to the [experiencing self vs remembering self](experiencing-vs-remembering-self.md)
split: the experiencing self's duration-weighted total and the remembering
self's peak-end prototype are different answers to "how bad was it?"
