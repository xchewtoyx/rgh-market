For people who find a direct [body scan](body-scan.md) too intrusive (for
example with a trauma history) or too effortful to add as a separate task,
interoceptive practice can instead be folded into what's already happening.
The pattern is: create a sensation, then notice it — for example by tensing
a muscle or pressing hands together, or by touching something soft, cool,
warm, or textured, then attending to it without judgment.

Interoception-difference types named in practice include
[under-responsivity](sensory-under-responsivity.md) (not registering input
until very strong), over-responsivity (feeling sensations more intensely or
for longer), and **discrimination difficulties** (trouble telling how you
feel or what you need). Scheduled check-in reminders — three daily prompts
guiding through thirst, hunger, bathroom, and rest — support proactive
self-care when felt signals are weak or confusing.

Two concrete forms:

1. Attach a brief moment of noticing to an existing ritual — e.g. feeling
   the warmth of a morning tea or coffee cup as it's held.
2. Set two daily alarms for a 30-second check-in — e.g. noticing feet on
   the ground, or the current rhythm of breath.

Scheduling **regular self-check-in time** — alone, via journalling, voice
notes, or whatever format suits — helps bring self-monitoring back into
consciousness. The more it is practised, the more naturally noticing
happens across the day without a formal prompt.

The value is frequency and low friction rather than depth: many brief,
easy check-ins across a day build the same underlying
[interoception](interoception.md) that a longer, rarer scan would, without
requiring the dedicated time or tolerance a full scan needs. This also
supports catching [delayed emotional processing](delayed-emotional-processing.md)
when reactions surface after the triggering event.
