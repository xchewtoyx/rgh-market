Delayed processing means an event occurs, but the person does not process
or display their emotional reaction at the time — happy or sad — and does so
sometime after. Carly Jones describes it as a common autistic pattern: the
feeling arrives on a delay relative to the trigger.

For state reading, this produces two calibration problems:

- A **real-time reading during or right after an event** may report "fine"
  or "nothing much" while the reaction is still in transit.
- **Tracing feelings back to their source** becomes harder because the lag
  separates the sensation from its cause — a bad mood tonight may belong to
  something that happened hours or days ago.

When a state reading feels disconnected or unexplained, check whether
delayed processing might be involved before treating the reading as noise.
Example: after a car accident, a person may tell themselves they won't
worry until tomorrow and dismiss residual tension as resolved; a later
innocuous trigger (e.g. a bank letter) then provokes disproportionate
anger — only afterward does the spillover from the earlier event become
clear. A partner may read tension in posture and face while the person
themselves has no idea they are still feeling bad.

Pair real-time [micro interoceptive check-ins](micro-interoceptive-check-ins.md)
with later journaling or voice notes to catch delayed reactions. See also
[emotion tracking journal](emotion-tracking-journal.md) for logging that
can bridge the gap.

Asking for time to process before responding — to a request, a comment, or
a big communication — is a legitimate way to avoid acting on a premature
reading. See [unhelpful self-monitoring](unhelpful-self-monitoring.md) for
how social-performance monitoring can compete with this internal work.
