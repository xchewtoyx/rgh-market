Panic overrides systems: procedures, plans, and deliberate response get
replaced by survival hormones reacting to the wrong targets. In acute
incidents — medical crises, aviation emergencies, high-stakes negotiations
under intimidation — emotional regulation is not a soft extra; it is a
prerequisite for any other skill to deploy.

**Core checks before escalating arousal:**

- "Does getting upset provide me with more options?" — sometimes yes, usually
  no.
- "Do I need to freak out about this?" — the professional answer, after
  [crisis rehearsal](crisis-rehearsal.md), is often no: either you practised
  for this class of situation or you caught the urge and chose not to feed it.
- If an emotion cannot change the condition, it is likely unhelpful or
  destructive. There is no problem so bad you cannot also make it worse.

**Stance: nerve, not numbness.** Nerve combines defiance ("I refuse to be
intimidated; I will not declare this a failure prematurely") with acceptance
("It is on me; I do not have the luxury of replaying close calls; people are
counting on me"). Grant's unflappability under literal explosions was
trained — deliberate exposure to loud stressors as a child — not innate.
Holiday frames equanimity (apatheia, ataraxia) as domestication of emotion,
not suppression: feel it, take a moment if needed, then return to the task.
Self-talk pattern: "I am in control, not my emotions. I see what is really
going on. I will not get excited or upset."

**Logic as counterweight.** When anxiety spikes, run structured questions:
Is this catastrophic? Is it unprecedented or merely rare? Have I handled
worse? What would justice, generosity, self-control, or prudence require
next? Mantra for physiological anxiety: "I am not going to die from this."

**Sharp-end evidence.** Sullenberger reported not calm but **very focused**
under dual engine failure; the ATC controller described the same hyper-focus.
Highly trained operators often become focused rather than falling apart —
attributed to training and experience, not temperament alone. Initial
disbelief ("fundamental surprise") may arrive first; regulation is what
prevents denial from freezing the response. See [fundamental surprise at
onset](fundamental-surprise-at-onset.md).

Epictetus's pre-confrontation drill — rehearsing that exile, chains, and
death were called matters of indifference in training, so worldly power
looks smaller when encountered — is the philosophical version of the same
pattern: decide responses while calm so acute threat does not rewrite your
priorities.

Acute regulation supports [sacrificing decisions](sacrificing-decisions.md)
and [acute stabilisation procedures](acute-stabilisation-procedures.md);
it does not replace [incident escalation path](incident-escalation-path.md)
when outside help is the correct step.
