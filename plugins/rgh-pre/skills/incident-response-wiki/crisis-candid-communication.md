Acute incidents often require decisions with no precedent and serious
consequences. [Acute emotional regulation](acute-emotional-regulation.md)
and [acute stabilisation procedures](acute-stabilisation-procedures.md)
depend on communication that is candid without becoming bogged down —
"psychologically safe meetings do not have to take longer."

**Hudson River pattern (Flight 1549):** dual engine failure was a non-normal
situation outside automated warnings. Captain declared "My aircraft"; first
officer accepted without hesitancy, fear, apology, or disagreement — handoff
in one exchange. Controller asked only essential questions, kept lines open
so parallel conversations were audible without repetition, and did not dictate
a plan — "his words let me know that he understood that these hard choices
were mine to make." Much coordination was silent, each watching the other for
visual cues. CRM/TEM training institutionalises speak-up and listen-up, but
psychological safety is a **precondition** for effective use of emergency
protocols — without it, interpersonal risk crowds out proper response.

**Fukushima Daini pattern:** plant superintendent armed workers with data on
a whiteboard — earthquake magnitude, aftershock frequency, decreasing danger
over time — rather than megaphone commands, letting workers decide whether
to take reconnaissance missions. Before dispatch, each group repeated
instructions back because fear impairs memory. When the cable plan proved
untenable, he **immediately admitted the mistake** to the team — unwelcome
news that increased psychological safety and bonded the group. When engineers
noticed Unit 1 pressure climbing faster than Unit 2's, he listened, shifted
focus, and told an exhausted team to redo work — upsetting some, but carried
by shared understanding of stakes. After sister-plant explosion, direct address:
"Please, trust me. I definitely won't do anything to harm you, but Fukushima
Daini is still in trouble, and I need you to do your best."

**Runbook takeaways:**

- Share information and calculations so people can act when conditions change.
- Admit errors and worst news early to solve together.
- Listen to front-line observations that contradict the current plan.
- Clear handoffs and explicit ownership ("My aircraft" / "Your aircraft").
- Candor reduces interpersonal fear within the group even under physical
  danger.

These patterns apply to personal acute response when coordinating with a
partner, clinician, or support person during [meltdown and shutdown
runbook](meltdown-shutdown-runbook.md) planning or [incident escalation
path](incident-escalation-path.md) execution.
