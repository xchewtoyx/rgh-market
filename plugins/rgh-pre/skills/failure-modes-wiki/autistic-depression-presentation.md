## Recognizing it

- **Trigger**: same broad factors as non-autistic depression — unsupportive
  social connections, poor achievement, lack of agency, bullying — with
  rates much higher in autistic than non-autistic people, especially those
  with average-to-above-average intellectual functioning.
- **Autistic-specific markers** (beyond standard sadness, hopelessness,
  sleep/appetite/energy changes):
  - less energy to [mask](masking.md) — previously masked skills appear to
    regress
  - increased repetitive behaviors and negative thought loops
    ([rumination spiral](rumination-spiral.md))
  - new or increased inflexibility
  - heightened anxiety, aggression, or self-injury
  - shifts in intense interests — intensified focus, loss of passion, or
    shift to a new (sometimes darker or morbid) topic
- **Progression if unaddressed**: can compound with
  [autistic burnout](autistic-burnout.md) — burnout can lead to depression,
  and both raise suicide risk. Depression rates are elevated for autistic
  adults regardless of gender; autistic females show a sharper rise in
  adolescence and adulthood compared to childhood.

## Distinguishing it from autistic burnout

Both can produce fatigue, withdrawal, and functional decline. Reliable
separating markers for depression specifically:

- feelings of worthlessness and hopelessness typical of depressive mood
  (burnout sufferers can still feel pleasure and typically lack this)
- depression may respond to medication or therapeutic reframing that
  burnout does not
- burnout is tied to a specific demand/support mismatch with autistic-specific
  features (masking breakdown, spiking sensory sensitivity, increased need
  for sameness) as the driver

Treating autistic depression presentation as if it were only burnout — or
only generic depression without recognizing autistic-specific markers —
misses part of what is actually happening.

## What it is commonly confused with

A decline in adaptive or academic functioning, withdrawal, increased
irritability, or intensified repetitive behavior in an autistic person
should prompt consideration of an emotional challenge — these are general
"pink flag" signals that an underlying depression (or related emotional
pattern) may be present even when standard screening tools, normed on
non-autistic populations, fail to detect it.
