## The distinction

Both autism and OCD can involve repetitive behaviors, intense persistent
thoughts, sensory hyper-responsivity, and a "not right" feeling — and the
two conditions can reinforce each other. The critical diagnostic split is
what drives the repetitive behavior:

- **Stim**: does not distress the person; may be pleasurable or
  self-soothing. It may distress observers, but the person is not trying to
  suppress it to relieve internal anxiety.
- **Compulsion**: distresses or interferes with the person's life; driven
  by intrusive thoughts, urges, or images the person tries to suppress or
  neutralize through the repetitive act.

Example: repeatedly opening and closing a door — for OCD without autism,
this is typically a threat-warding ritual providing temporary anxiety relief;
for autism without OCD, it may be pleasurable or self-soothing. An autistic
person with both conditions may struggle to distinguish sensory-craving
behavior from compulsion-driven ritual, complicating intervention design.

## Why the distinction matters

Treating an intense interest or stim as an "obsession" is unhelpful and
potentially harmful. Conversely, missing a compulsion because it looks like
a stim leaves the distress-driving intrusive thought unaddressed. A
compulsive behavior can even originate from a sensory reaction rather than
an obsessive thought — e.g. repeated handwashing triggered by an aversive
texture, which then develops its own compulsive momentum.

## Diagnostic test

Ask whether the person experiences the repetitive behavior as relieving
internal distress they wish they could stop, versus engaging in it because
it feels good, soothing, or interesting. Observer distress alone is not
informative — the person's own relationship to the behavior is the marker.
