## Recognizing it

- **Trigger**: actual rejection or criticism, or merely the anticipation of
  it — the pain response can fire from a predicted future rejection with
  no external event having occurred yet. In autistics, arranging life to
  avoid the feeling of rejection at all costs — perceiving rejection where
  it hasn't occurred (not feeling invited unless explicitly asked; assuming
  a colleague's generic comment is about you; assuming a boss wanting to see
  you means firing; assuming partner silence means anger).
- **Marker**: emotional pain amplified well past what the triggering event
  would ordinarily warrant — described as turning the volume on the
  feeling all the way up — accompanied by real physical symptoms: fast
  heartbeat, sweating, upset stomach, tension. The response is rapid and
  dramatic, often immediate and visceral. Non-autistic people with RSD may
  misread small signals (a sideways glance, a tone shift) as devastating
  rebuke; autistic people with RSD may misread the same cues or be confused
  about underlying intent, compounding dysregulation.
- **Progression if unaddressed**: because anticipated rejection alone is
  enough to trigger the pain, the reliable way to avoid the pain becomes
  avoiding the rejection-risking situation altogether — social withdrawal,
  avoiding new or unfamiliar things, and procrastinating specifically on
  tasks that carry a risk of judgment, failure, or negative feedback. The
  procrastination here is not about the task's difficulty or the person's
  discipline; it is a downstream consequence of avoiding the anticipated
  rejection-pain the task's evaluation might trigger. This is a specific
  instance of the same shape as [emotional avoidance](emotional-avoidance.md)
  and can combine with [escalating escapism](escalating-escapism.md) when
  the avoided task is displaced by more immediately rewarding activity.
- **What it is commonly confused with**: read as being "overly sensitive,"
  thin-skinned, or dramatic — but the amplification is not a character
  trait or a choice; it reflects a real difference in how the emotional
  response to rejection is processed, not an exaggeration of an ordinary
  feeling. Because the response is immediate and visceral, it often does
  not respond well to behavioral or cognitive interventions alone.

## A specific bind it creates

The same underlying temperament that produces RSD is often paired with
impulsivity that pushes toward risk-taking and going all in — so the
person can experience simultaneous pressure toward and away from the same
rejection-risking action, which is a harder bind to resolve than either
tendency alone would be.

## Distinguishing marker from raw spots

RSD is a specific, named case of the more general [raw
spots](raw-spots.md) mechanism (a present stressor compounding a
vulnerability), narrowed specifically to the rejection domain and paired
with a defined physical symptom signature — useful to recognize as its own
pattern because its trigger (rejection, actual or anticipated) and its
downstream behavior (avoidance, procrastination) are specific and
recurring enough to name directly rather than needing to be reconstructed
from the general mechanism each time.

## Root cause in masking histories

A life spent pretending to be someone else can lead to concluding the real
self isn't good enough for anyone, undermining felt worthiness to pursue
what one wants. Early trait-based rejections generate social anxiety, which
elicits further rejection — a self-fulfilling prophecy; rejection sensitivity
also suppresses how many social attempts are made, making true rejection
rates hard to establish. Links to [masking](masking.md) as upstream driver.
