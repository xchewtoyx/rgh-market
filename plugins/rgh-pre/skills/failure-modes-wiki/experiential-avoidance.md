## Recognizing it

- **Trigger**: past painful moods or feelings that led the person to treat
  their own inner experience as a threat — activating the brain's avoidance
  system toward emotions, thoughts, and body sensations, not only toward
  external dangers.
- **Marker**: habitual efforts to wall off, suppress, numb, or pretend
  internal states are not there — analogous to turning up the car radio to
  drown out a strange engine noise: effective at blanking the signal,
  ineffective at preventing the engine seizing. The pattern often
  disconnects the person from negative feelings *and* mutes positive ones,
  producing a sense of being out of touch with the full experience of being
  alive.
- **Interoceptive consequence**: like John after a car accident — visibly
  tense (red face, raised blood pressure, tight grimace-smile) yet reporting
  only "passing annoyances," brushing off body signals until a minor later
  trigger (an innocuous bank letter) provokes disproportionate anger. The
  person does not notice emotional reactions until they have already pulled
  mood downward — too late for timely recognition. This is a direct result
  of avoidance, not merely [alexithymia](alexithymia.md) or [delayed
  interoception](delayed-interoception.md) as standalone traits.
- **Progression if unaddressed**: unpleasant feelings remain and still
  trigger habitual reactions that turn passing unpleasantness into persistent
  suffering; attitudes and judgments are influenced by unregistered states
  (frowning alone biases experience more negatively); the psychological
  "engine" becomes highly likely to seize up. Research across 100+ studies
  links many forms of emotional disturbance to unhealthy escape-and-avoid
  efforts. Over time, [aversion reaction](aversion-reaction.md) and
  [rumination spiral](rumination-spiral.md) engage from states the person
  cannot yet name.

## What it is commonly confused with

[Emotional avoidance](emotional-avoidance.md) — experiential avoidance is
the broader clinical pattern (ACT/MBCT terminology) covering thoughts and
body sensations as well as emotions. Ordinary distraction or temporary
grounding used to regain processing capacity is not experiential avoidance;
the distinguishing marker is habitual, ongoing disconnection from internal
experience as a default strategy, not a short bridge back to engagement.
