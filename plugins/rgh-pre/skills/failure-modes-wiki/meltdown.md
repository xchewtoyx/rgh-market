## Recognizing it

- **Trigger**: buildup of unreleased sensory or emotional energy —
  overstimulation or unprocessed emotion with no outlet — crossing the
  upper threshold of what the person can currently hold. High-masking
  autistics often route emotion into the neocortex, rationalizing and
  talking through disempowerment; when emotion exceeds what that route can
  hold, it overflows — the whole body fills with panic and chaos. Common
  feedback-loop triggers: feeling the reaction is irrational, chronic
  misunderstanding after failed explanations, frustration with technology or
  bureaucracy, powerlessness spinning cause/effect rapidly, isolation and a
  sense that no one can help. Often preceded by ignored periods of
  [autistic burnout](autistic-burnout.md).
- **Marker**: an outward, escalating eruption — the opposite direction from
  shutdown's collapse and withdrawal. Where shutdown is a sudden drop in
  capacity to respond, meltdown is energy discharging outward, involuntarily,
  once it can no longer be contained. Presentation can include increased
  stimming, crying, increased echolalia, irrational thoughts, and erratic
  behavior — not a single uniform "tantrum" shape. Physical presentation can
  include waving limbs, hitting one's head, screaming, inability to talk,
  falling to the floor, crying — intensely physical for many.
- **Operational fact**: once triggered, meltdowns cannot be stopped — they
  must run their course until the person is out of energy. Best managed
  somewhere safe and away from too many eyes. A meltdown is not a medical
  emergency in the heart-attack sense — it is an explosion of pent-up
  emotion. Aftermath may include relief and clarity but also exhaustion and
  risk of [post-meltdown fawning rebound](post-meltdown-fawning-rebound.md).
  Many adults report wanting to die during meltdown not because death is
  appealing but because meltdown feelings are so aversive they would do
  anything to make them stop. Once underway, the person is irrational and
  unreachable by reasoning — coping must assume rational self-direction is
  offline.
- **What it is commonly confused with**: because it is outward and visible,
  meltdown is easily misread as a deliberate outburst, temper tantrum, or
  loss of control chosen by the person. A temper tantrum implies willful,
  goal-directed behavior; a meltdown is an uncontrollable physiological and
  instinctual response to overwhelm — treating it as a tantrum is ineffective
  and can be traumatizing. The involuntary, energy-discharge nature is the
  same regardless of which direction it goes.

## Distinguishing marker from shutdown

Both arise from exceeded capacity; the reliable difference is direction —
meltdown discharges outward and escalates, shutdown withdraws and collapses
inward. The same underlying trigger (overload) can produce either,
depending on the person or the moment, so the presence of one rather than
the other is not itself informative about how overloaded the person is —
only that some threshold was crossed. Together, meltdown and shutdown are
sometimes grouped under **neuro-crash** — short-term overwhelm of a system
pushed past its limit, producing a fight/flight/freeze response.

Common neuro-crash triggers include: being around agitated people; being
rushed or pushed beyond current capability; invasions of personal space;
sensory overload; lack of predictability; unclear expectations; and
physiological reactions. Some autistic people strongly reject the "anxiety"
label clinicians apply to these events — the presentation is overwhelm
response, not necessarily an anxiety disorder in the ordinary sense.
