## Recognizing it

- **Trigger**: being ordered to act against one's own ethics (e.g. told to
  discriminate, or to make a legitimate request process deliberately
  exhausting for the person making it), or witnessing a leadership betrayal
  or harm to others in a high-stakes situation where one lacks the power to
  stop it.
- **Precursor state**: **moral distress** — recognizing an act or situation
  as wrong while still participating in or tolerating it. Moral distress
  that is sustained or repeated, without resolution or acknowledgment,
  escalates into moral injury.
- **Progression if unaddressed**: deepening injury can lead to resignation
  or to physical/psychological illness — the person's continued presence in
  the situation itself becomes the harm.
- **Who is especially vulnerable**: people with heightened justice
  sensitivity combined with a prior trauma history are more susceptible;
  this combination is common among neurodivergent people, and can
  functionally exclude them from jobs or industries that normalize the kind
  of ethical compromise involved.

## Distinguishing marker

The tell that separates moral injury from generic overload-driven stress or
burnout is *content*, not intensity: the precipitating event is specifically
an ethical transgression (one's own or witnessed), not workload volume or
duration. A low-workload role can still produce moral injury if it requires
participating in something the person finds morally wrong; a demanding but
ethically clean role does not.

## Recovery requires more than rest

Because the injury is caused by a breach of trust/ethics rather than
depletion, recovery is reported to require the breach itself being
addressed — transparent acknowledgment and amends from whoever caused or
allowed the transgression — rather than recovery techniques aimed at
restoring energy or capacity alone.
