title: Universal Design Default
description: >
  Make an accommodation a standard environmental default for everyone
  rather than an individually-triggered exception, when doing so doesn't
  disadvantage anyone else, removing the need for whoever needs it to
  disclose or request it at all.

Many accommodations designed for a specific need (a quiet workspace,
flexible hours, organizational software, remote-work options) turn out to
also benefit people who never would have requested them individually — the
need was real but the disclosure/request step was the actual barrier to
access. Making the accommodation a standard feature of the environment by
default, rather than something unlocked only after an individual
identifies themselves and asks, sidesteps that barrier entirely: nobody has
to disclose a private need just to get a generally useful improvement.
[Multi-format information provision](multi-format-information-provision.md)
is one specific application of this principle. It doesn't replace more
tailored, individually-negotiated accommodations for needs that genuinely
vary — it just shrinks the set of things that require going through that
process at all.

A design can be checked against seven working criteria for whether it
actually achieves this: **equitable use** (the same design works for
everyone, not a separate "special" version); **flexibility in use**
(accommodates a range of preferences and abilities); **simple and
intuitive use** (understandable regardless of prior experience);
**perceptible information** (necessary information is clear and
noticeable, not easy to miss); **tolerance for error** (mistakes and
accidental misuse don't produce disproportionate harm); **low physical
effort**; and **size and space for approach and use** (everyone can
actually reach and use it). A design that fails several of these is
usually still solving for one group at the expense of another, rather than
being genuinely universal. Involving the people the design is meant to
serve directly in its creation — see
[co-design with affected users](co-design-with-affected-users.md) — is how
gaps against these criteria tend to get caught early, rather than
discovered after the fact.

**Autism-friendly public hours** — reduced visual and auditory distraction
during normal business with no diagnosis gatekeeping — are a worked example:
everyone may use them, the problem is located in the environment, and no
one must disclose to access relief. The same principle covers dimmable
lighting, fragrance bans, quiet applause alternatives, and relaxed dress
codes that reject "professionalism" defined by uncomfortable formalwear.
Best versions are directed by people who know the need intimately and run
during regular hours, not segregated special sessions.
