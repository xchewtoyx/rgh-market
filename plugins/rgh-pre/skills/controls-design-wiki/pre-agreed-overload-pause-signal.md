---
type: concept
title: Pre-Agreed Overload Pause Signal
description: >
  Agree in advance, while calm, on a specific word or signal that either
  party in a recurring interaction can use to call a pause once overload
  sets in, so stopping doesn't require negotiating permission to stop in
  the moment.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Megan Anna Neff), Chapter 5: Social Self-Care"
  - title: "How to Be You: Simple Ways to Love Who You Are"
    resource: "How to Be You (Ellie Middleton), ch. 2"
---

Effective communication degrades past a certain level of physiological
arousal — continuing a difficult conversation once either party is
overloaded tends to make things worse, not better. Deciding a stop signal
ahead of time, while both people are calm, removes the need to negotiate
whether-and-when to pause in real time, which is exactly when negotiating
anything is hardest. The signal can be a plain word or phrase, or something
more structured — couples therapist John Gottman's technique of wearing
heartbeat monitors and pausing once heart rate crosses an agreed threshold
is one worked version of the same pattern, substituting a physiological
readout for self-report as the trigger.

Two design choices make the signal actually usable: both people commit in
advance to honoring it without argument when invoked (a signal that can be
overridden by the other party in the moment isn't a real pre-agreement),
and the pause itself has a home — a quiet, sensory-comfortable space to
retreat to — rather than just stopping mid-conflict with nowhere to go.

The signal need not be literal ("stop" or "I need a break"). Pre-agreed
coded phrases that sound ordinary to bystanders work when explaining overload
in public is hard — e.g. telling a partner "We need to get back to the car
before the parking runs out" to mean "leave this social situation now," or
telling a manager "I'm just going to collect something from the printer" to
mean "leave this meeting to decompress." For people who lose verbal capacity
entirely, substitute gestures (tapping an arm three times), communication
cards, or AAC devices — the format matters less than prior agreement with a
trusted person who will honor it without debate.

This is the interpersonal counterpart to a [sensory safety
plan](sensory-safety-plan.md): both are agreements made in advance, while
capacity is available, so a stop-and-recover move doesn't have to be
invented under the load it's meant to interrupt. Where the underlying issue
is that overload isn't visible to the other party (someone can look calm
while internally overwhelmed), that's a failure mode to diagnose, not a
control — the pre-agreed signal's job is only to give overload a
recognized channel once it's noticed.
