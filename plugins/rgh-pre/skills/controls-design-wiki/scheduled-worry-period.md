---
type: concept
title: Scheduled Worry Period
description: >
  Contain an otherwise-unbounded recurring activity (worrying) to a fixed
  daily time slot, and defer intrusions outside that slot to the next
  scheduled occurrence, instead of engaging with it wherever it arises.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Megan Anna Neff), Chapter 3: Emotional Self-Care"
  - title: "That Little Voice in Your Head"
    resource: "That Little Voice in Your Head (Mo Gawdat), Chapter 8: Alchemy"
---

A consistent daily slot (e.g. 10-20 minutes) is set aside for the activity
— in the source case, writing down worries and sorting them into
controllable (act on them) versus uncontrollable (release them). When the
impulse shows up outside the slot, the response is a fixed deferral cue
("this will be addressed at the worry period") rather than a fresh decision
about whether to engage right now. The container does two things at once:
it bounds an activity that has no natural stopping point, and it removes
the in-the-moment choice of whether to indulge it, replacing that choice
with a standing rule.

This is a different use of time-boxing than [time-boxed goal setting](time-boxed-goal-setting.md)
— that pattern lowers the barrier to *starting* a wanted activity by
capping its size; this one *contains* an unwanted, otherwise-unbounded
activity by capping when it's allowed to happen at all.

The deferral cue can be addressed to the intrusive thought directly rather
than framed as a personal rule: telling it, in the moment, that it has a
specific future appointment and can be set aside until then. Naming a
concrete time (not just "later") is what makes the deferral credible
enough to actually work, in the same way a vague intention fails where a
[concrete implementation plan](concrete-implementation-plan.md) succeeds.
