---
type: concept
title: Cue Exposure Reduction
description: >
  Eliminate bad habits by removing triggering cues from the environment
  rather than relying on willpower to resist them once noticed.
sources:
  - title: "Atomic Habits: Tiny Changes, Remarkable Results"
    resource: "Atomic Habits (James Clear), ch. 7"
  - title: "Tiny Habits: The Small Changes That Change Everything"
    resource: "Tiny Habits (BJ Fogg), ch. 8"
---

Vietnam heroin addicts: 20% addicted in theater, but only 5% relapsed within
a year back home — radical environment change dissolved addiction when war
cues vanished. Typical rehab fails when people return to the same cue-rich
neighborhood. Once a habit is encoded, cues can spark craving decades later
(Patty Olwell craved cigarettes the moment she got back on a horse).

**Cue-induced wanting:** external triggers spark compulsive craving below
conscious awareness — cocaine images shown for 33 milliseconds still activated
reward pathways in addicts. You can break a habit but unlikely forget it;
resisting temptation exhausts in a cue-saturated environment.

People with "high self-control" aren't morally superior — they structure life
to need less willpower and spend less time in tempting situations. Avoiding
temptation beats resisting it. Cut bad habits at the source: phone in another
room; unfollow envy triggers; TV out of bedroom; stop tech reviews if they
drive overspending; unplug and closet game console after each use.

**Cue removal options** (Fogg, stopping habits): *remove* the prompt from the
environment (strongest); *avoid* places/people/media that trigger it; *ignore*
the cue by willpower (weakest — depletes with repetition). See [behavior
change masterplan](behavior-change-masterplan.md) Phase 2 ordering.

This is the inversion of the 1st Law in [four laws of behavior change](four-laws-of-behavior-change.md)
— make cues invisible. Pair with [friction shaping](friction-shaping.md) when
removal isn't possible and full elimination needs added effort to act.
