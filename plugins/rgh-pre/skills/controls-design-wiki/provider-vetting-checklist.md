type: concept
title: Green/Red Flag Vetting Checklist
description: >
  Prepare a standing list of green flags and red flags for evaluating a
  category of recurring people-decision (a service provider, a new
  friendship) in advance, so each new candidate is screened against known
  criteria instead of being freshly judged from scratch.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Megan Anna Neff), Chapter 4: Mental Self-Care; Chapter 5: Social Self-Care"
  - title: "The Unmasking Workbook for Autistic Adults"
    resource: "The Unmasking Workbook for Autistic Adults (Jessica Penot), ch. 8"
---

Worked example (service provider): vetting a prospective therapist for
being genuinely neurodivergent-affirming. Green flags: identity-first
language, explicit affirmation of neurodiversity, culturally
competent/intersectional care, emphasis on client autonomy, proactive
accessibility (flexible scheduling, sensory accommodations, alternative
communication methods). Red flags: heavy promotion of compliance-focused
therapies, pathologizing language, a "fix-it"/cure-oriented framing.

Worked example (friendship): reflection prompts to define personal
green/red flags for a new friend, decided before evaluating anyone against
them — what makes you feel respected, what communication/listening styles
work, expected reliability/responsiveness, what support you value, how you
want your traits/differences respected, preferred conflict-handling style,
privacy/confidentiality expectations. This use is worth deliberately
preparing for people who are more vulnerable to being taken advantage of
because reading deceptive intent from social cues is harder for them —
having the criteria fixed in advance compensates for that gap at
evaluation time.

The general pattern extends to any recurring people-evaluation decision —
providers, friends, partners: compile the criteria once, ahead of any
specific search, and reuse the same checklist against each candidate. This
turns an otherwise open-ended, effortful judgment call ("do I trust this
person") into screening against a fixed list, and makes it easier to
notice and walk away from a bad fit rather than talking yourself into it
in the moment.

Penot's trauma-prevention lists (general interpersonal safety): **red
flags** — love-bombing before knowing you; condescending language; regularly
crossing boundaries; isolating you from friends/family; statements that make
you feel bad; consistently putting their needs above yours; history of
abusing others; explosive/erratic moods; unwillingness to compromise;
active substance abuse. **Green flags** — respects boundaries and listens;
communicates in ways you understand; kind and values your needs; you feel
safe; non-judgmental enough to unmask; willing to compromise; sets their
own healthy boundaries; honest; disagrees respectfully; never pressures you
into discomfort. Prioritize safety over being liked — if current people
cannot provide loving kindness, structural change (new people, [commitment
pruning protocol](commitment-pruning-protocol.md)) may be required.
