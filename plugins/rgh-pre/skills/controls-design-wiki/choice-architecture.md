People are not coherent rational agents who weigh every option from scratch;
automatic, low-effort processing strongly shapes what gets chosen. Choice
architecture uses that fact for benefit: arrange environments so the
path of least resistance points where the person would choose on reflection,
while keeping an explicit opt-out.

Examples: pension enrollment as opt-out rather than opt-in (checking a box
to leave is an act of commission, feels like deviating from the normal
choice, and carries more anticipated regret than joining); contracts written
simply enough to actually read instead of relying on disclosure in unread
fine print; labels that display "10% fat" as prominently as "90% fat-free"
so framing cannot hide the same fact.

**Opt-out versus opt-in forms** show how strongly defaults matter when
decision effort is high: organ-donation rates near 100% where silence counts
as consent versus single-digit rates where active opt-in is required — same
population, different form design. The effect reflects decision laziness
under unprepared questions more than deep preference; policy and personal
design should treat default designation as a first-class lever, not a
neutral presentation detail.

This is libertarian paternalism — nudging without forbidding. Pair with
[default option preselection](default-option-preselection.md) for personal
routines and [friction shaping](friction-shaping.md) when the goal is to
make unwanted options harder rather than making wanted ones default.

**Environment as invisible hand:** at Massachusetts General Hospital, adding
water alongside soda in every refrigerator and placing water baskets at food
stations — without talking to anyone — cut soda sales 11.4% and raised water
sales 25.8%. People often choose products because of where they are, not what
they are. Cookies on the counter get eaten even when you weren't thinking
about them. Lewin's B = f(P,E): behavior is a function of the person in their
environment; much daily behavior follows the most obvious option, not a
purposeful drive. Visual cues are the greatest catalyst of human behavior —
of roughly eleven million sensory receptors, about ten million are for
sight — so small visibility changes produce large shifts. Dutch homeowners
with electrical meters in the main hallway used up to 30% less energy than
neighbors with basement meters: making use visible changed behavior without
motivation appeals. You can be the architect of your environment, not only
its victim — pair with [context as cue](context-as-cue.md) for how locations
train whole habit contexts.
