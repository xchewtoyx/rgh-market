title: Graded Task Assignment
description: >
  A design principle for any task-based intervention: start at the person's
  current level of functioning and decompose the goal into progressively
  smaller steps, rather than attempting the full-sized task in one go.
sources:

When a task feels too large to start, the usual failure is aiming at the
target size of the task rather than the person's present capability — the
equivalent of attempting a marathon without training. Graded task assignment
instead decomposes the task into a chain of progressively smaller steps until
one is found that is achievable right now, and treats reaching that small
step as the actual goal.

Worked pattern: don't clean the whole kitchen — just do the dishes; if that's
still too much, just stack the dirty dishes into a pile; if even that's too
much, aim for one clean bench top, or wash five plates. The step size is
chosen so it is achievable at the current level, not at the eventual target
level; the task can always be re-decomposed further if the current step is
still too big.

This reframes what "doing the task" means: early on, the measure of success
is not how much gets done but that something gets done at all — the size of
the step is deliberately made small enough to remove the need for
willpower or motivation to clear it. [Time-boxed goal setting](time-boxed-goal-setting.md)
is a specific way to size the step (by duration rather than amount) when the
task doesn't have a natural small unit to peel off. Once a task is being
scheduled at all, [balanced fun-and-achievement activity scheduling](balanced-activity-scheduling.md)
governs what mix of tasks to put in the schedule. Writing the decomposed
steps down as a [visual checklist for multi-step routines](visual-checklist-for-multistep-routines.md)
keeps the decomposition from having to be redone from memory each time the
task recurs.

Caveat: forced small increments aren't universally helpful. Some people
work better in large, uninterrupted chunks and find imposed breakpoints
disruptive to building a coherent understanding or maintaining flow —
treating small-step decomposition as a universal default risks making
things worse for them. Whether comprehension or progress is actually
happening should be checked directly (e.g. via assessment), not assumed
from the presence or absence of small steps.
