---
type: concept
title: Law of Least Effort
description: >
  People gravitate toward the option that delivers value with the least work,
  so durable controls reduce friction for wanted behaviors rather than
  boosting motivation to overcome unnecessary effort.
sources:
  - title: "Atomic Habits: Tiny Changes, Remarkable Results"
    resource: "Atomic Habits (James Clear), ch. 12"
---

Conventional advice treats motivation as the key to habit change, but the
brain is wired to conserve energy — choosing the path of least effort is a
smart default, not a character flaw. Out of all possible actions, the one
that happens is the one with the best value-to-effort ratio. A hundred-push-up
goal drains after motivation fades; a one-push-up habit is nearly costless
to start and more likely to recur.

Habits that dominate daily life (phone scrolling, email, TV) win because they
require almost no effort. Every practice is an obstacle between you and the
outcome it delivers — fitness, calm, clarity — so extra friction on the habit
stands between you and what you actually want. "Make it easy" is not about
only doing easy things; it is about making long-payoff actions as easy as
possible in the moment so they still happen on hard days.

Two strategies to increase behavior flow: crank motivation to force effort
through a bend (raises tension), or remove the bend — [friction
shaping](friction-shaping.md) on the environment so behavior flows naturally.
Pick habit locations already on your daily path; a gym on the commute route
beats one that requires a separate trip. Tidying feels good partly because
it lightens environmental cognitive load.

Create an environment where doing the right thing is as easy as possible —
the same design move meal delivery, dating apps, and simplified tax forms
use to raise uptake by removing steps.
