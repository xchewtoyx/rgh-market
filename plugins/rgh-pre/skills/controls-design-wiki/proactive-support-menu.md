title: Proactive Support Menu
description: >
  Present a menu of available accommodations or supports upfront by
  default, rather than requiring a person to identify and request what
  they need, since self-advocacy capacity is unevenly distributed and often
  lowest exactly where need is highest.

A request-driven support system only helps people who already know what to
ask for, have the language to ask for it, and feel safe enough to ask —
capacities that correlate with exactly the kinds of disadvantage the
support is meant to offset, so the people who'd benefit most are the least
likely to trigger it. Offering a menu of options proactively (transport
help, loaner equipment, quiet space, adjusted hours) inverts the default:
uptake no longer depends on the recipient's own self-advocacy. This is the
provider-side counterpart to a [curated activity menu](curated-activity-menu.md)
— someone else builds and offers the menu instead of the person having to
know to ask.

The same logic scales down to a single offer of help: a generic "let me
know if you need anything" still leaves the burden of identifying a need
and phrasing a request on the person under load — it's a request-driven
system in miniature, and gets rebuffed for the same reason. A specific,
concrete offer ("I can watch the kids Tuesday afternoon," "I'll bring
dinner Thursday") removes that burden the same way a full menu does, just
sized to a single instance of support rather than a standing set of
options.

Published workplace "menus of accommodations" — flexible hours, hybrid
work, quiet desks, permanent seating, sensory decompression rooms, written
agendas — apply the same inversion at organizational scale: employees
select from known options instead of cold-starting self-advocacy. Many
high-impact entries cost little or nothing; the barrier is uncertainty about
what exists, not budget.
