title: Environmental Sensory Accommodation

  When a specific sensory trigger is identified, substitute a
  product/environmental variant that avoids it rather than trying to endure
  or will through the standard-issue version.

The pattern: *identify the specific sensory trigger* (a texture, scent,
temperature-transition, or sound), then *substitute a variant that removes
it*, rather than repeatedly spending willpower tolerating the standard
option. This turns a recurring point of friction into a one-time
environment/product change. Examples across senses: fragrance-free
soap/shampoo/deodorant for scent sensitivity; a soft-bristle or vibrating
toothbrush, or a toothpaste pump instead of a squeeze tube, for oral-care
texture/motor issues; a portable heater for uncomfortable bathing
temperature transitions; sensory-friendly (constrictive or loose, per
preference) clothing as a buffer against touch overload; reduced visual
clutter and dimmed lighting for sight overload; a stocked set of comforting
sounds plus noise-cancelling headphones for sound overload.

The same substitution logic also applies where the missing signal is a
*detection* signal rather than a comfort one — e.g. a hyposensitive sense of
smell makes it hard to detect spoiled food or air contaminants by nose alone.
There, the substitute is an external detector or visual system: a
color-coded fridge/expiration system, or an air-quality/smoke detector,
standing in for the sense that can't be relied on for safety-relevant
detection. This is the same "replace an unreliable sense with an external
signal" move used in [fixed schedule for unreliable body cues](fixed-schedule-for-unreliable-body-cues.md),
applied to external hazards rather than internal state.

Asking others to accommodate a known trigger (e.g. removing a scented
product from a shared space) can be made easier with a
[pre-scripted accommodation request](pre-scripted-accommodation-request.md).

When the environment cannot be changed, **route and timing substitutes**
reduce exposure: shop earlier in the day or away from busy centres; visit
during events that draw crowds elsewhere; pre-map quiet "retreat corners" in
familiar venues (e.g. technical-book sections in large shops). At venue
design scale, the same substitution logic covers dimmable lights, diffused
rather than overhead fluorescent sources, echo-damping materials, fragrance
bans, and spaced seating with corner options — see [universal design
default](universal-design-default.md) when these become standard rather
than individually requested.
