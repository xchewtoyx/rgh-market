---
type: concept
title: Voluntary Hardship Drill
description: >
  Periodically and deliberately practice a scaled-down version of a feared
  future deprivation (going without comfort, food, or money) while
  circumstances are still stable, so the real thing — if it arrives — is
  familiar rather than untested.
sources:
  - title: "Letters on Ethics: To Lucilius"
    resource: "Seneca, Letters on Ethics (trans. Graver and Long), Letter 18"
---

Dread of a possible future loss (poverty, scarcity, loss of comfort) is
worst when the loss has never actually been experienced — the mind has
nothing but imagination to work with, and imagination tends to run worse
than reality. Scheduling a real, if scaled-down and time-boxed, rehearsal
of the feared condition — a set number of days eating plain food, sleeping
on a hard bed, wearing rough clothes — converts an abstract fear into
concrete, already-survived experience. The point isn't performative
austerity for its own sake (staging a fake "pauper's day" as an aesthetic
pose accomplishes nothing); it's building an actual, tested answer to "can
I handle this" before circumstances force the test on you at a worse
time, with less choice about when it happens and how much of it you face
at once.

The same logic as a fire drill: rehearsing the response in a controlled,
low-stakes window is cheaper than discovering the response is inadequate
during the real emergency, and the rehearsal itself reduces the standing
anticipatory dread of the emergency ever happening.
