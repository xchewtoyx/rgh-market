type: concept
title: Sensory-Friendly Activity Selection
description: >
  When planning a shared activity, choose the venue and format for its
  sensory characteristics (lighting, noise, structure) up front, rather
  than defaulting to a conventional venue and hoping sensory overload
  doesn't undermine the activity's actual purpose.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Megan Anna Neff), Chapter 5: Social Self-Care"
---

Sensory overload takes the "social system" offline regardless of how much
someone actually wants to connect — a loud, bright, crowded venue can
undermine a shared activity's purpose (connection, enjoyment) no matter how
promising the company or occasion. Because venue and format are chosen in
advance, this is a design decision available before the activity happens,
not something to manage reactively once overload has already started.

Traits worth selecting for: quiet or naturally sound-absorbing settings
(nature outings, walks — which add rhythmic sensory regulation and reduce
sustained-eye-contact pressure); dim or naturally lit spaces over harsh
fluorescent ones; activities with a built-in shared focus rather than
face-to-face conversation as the whole point (a creative workshop, a
picnic with full control over the physical setup) — this doubles as a form
of [lower-load modality choice](lower-load-modality-choice.md), since a
side-by-side or activity-anchored format lowers direct social demand the
same way parallel play does; settings that are sensory-rich but
predictable and self-paced (a botanical garden, an arboretum) rather than
crowded and unpredictable.

This is the activity-planning application of the same instinct behind
[curated social exposure](curated-social-exposure.md) (choosing who and
where based on load, not just availability) and complements a written
[sensory safety plan](sensory-safety-plan.md) for situations that can't be
avoided or redesigned in advance.
