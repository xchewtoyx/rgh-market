---
type: concept
title: Environmental Mismatch as the Source of Disabling Struggle
description: >
  A struggle that looks like a fixed personal deficit can instead be a
  demand/capacity mismatch created by the environment; changing the
  environment to match actual capacity can resolve the struggle entirely
  without any change in underlying skill.
sources:
  - title: "The Canary Code: A Guide to Neurodiversity, Dignity, and Intersectional Belonging at Work"
    resource: "The Canary Code (Praslova), Chapter 6 (Work Environment — Flexible Spaces)"
  - title: "Knowing Why: Adult-Diagnosed Autistic People on Life and Autism"
    resource: "Knowing Why (ASAN), Chapter 5 (Kelly Bron Johnson — Working While Autistic)"
---

The source's illustrative case: a person spent roughly 25 years in office
environments experiencing chronic depression, anxiety, illness, and weight
gain from constant unmanaged sensory filtering (lights, noise, movement,
temperature, scents, interruptions), having assumed everyone experienced
work this way. When the environment changed (forced remote work), all of
those symptoms reversed — not because any underlying skill or trait
changed, but because the ambient demand the environment was imposing
dropped to something the same capacity could actually sustain.

This is the clearest form of the capability-versus-capacity distinction:
what looked for decades like a stable personal limitation (chronic poor
health, low resilience) was actually a fixed environment producing a
demand that permanently outstripped available capacity — the "disability"
was a property of the mismatch, not of the person, and it disappeared once
the mismatch did. Practically, this means a recurring struggle should
prompt checking whether the *environment's* demand is fixed and
excessive before concluding the *person's* capacity is the limiting
factor — see [designing around capacity
limits](designing-around-capacity-limits.md) for what to do once a limit
is identified, and [net capacity effect depends on activity
fit](net-capacity-effect-depends-on-activity-fit.md) for the general
principle that the same nominal task can cost very different amounts
depending on the setting it's done in.

Johnson left an otherwise accommodating office job because cumulative
exhaustion from fluorescent lighting and fluctuating temperature over
eight-hour days drained her despite good coworkers; her most productive
in-office hours were after colleagues left and lights and noise dropped.
Remote work let her control sensory input directly (balance-ball chair,
fidgets without scrutiny, no perfume or pen-tapping) — same skills, far
more end-of-day energy. Disclosure in hiring then filters for employers
willing to accommodate written directions, scent-free norms, and
communication style before the mismatch is locked in.
