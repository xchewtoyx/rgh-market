  A model of saturation as a bounded operating zone with two failure
  thresholds — hyperarousal above it, hypoarousal below it — whose width
  is itself a capacity parameter that varies by person and can be trained.

The window of tolerance is the range of stress load within which a person
stays regulated: balanced, clear-thinking, and able to engage socially.
It has two edges, not one. Exceeding the upper threshold produces
hyperarousal — anxiety, agitation, panic. Dropping below the lower
threshold produces hypoarousal — dissociation, fatigue, numbness,
detachment. Both are saturation states; the window models capacity not as
a single ceiling to stay under but as a band to stay inside, with distinct
symptoms depending on which edge is crossed.

Two people can have very different window widths for the same nominal
capacity: a narrower window means a smaller swing in load pushes past
either threshold, so the same demand fluctuation that a wider-window
person absorbs comfortably can saturate a narrower-window person in either
direction. Window width is described as trainable rather than fixed — it
can be widened over time through general regulation practice, boundary-
setting, and by raising the baseline recovery-rate parameter itself (see
[vagal tone as a determinant of recovery
capacity](vagal-tone-and-recovery-capacity.md)) — which distinguishes
widening the window (a longer-horizon capacity investment) from any single
episode of staying inside it.

Crossing either threshold doesn't just degrade a functional domain, it can
take that domain fully offline: past a certain arousal level, capacity for
clear communication and social engagement can drop out entirely rather
than merely getting harder, regardless of how motivated or willing the
person is in that moment. This matters for demand planning because it
means the relationship between load and output isn't smoothly diminishing
right up to the threshold and then gradual after it — a domain can still
look available right up until it saturates, then stop functioning
altogether.

The lower threshold has a task-level analogue worth distinguishing: the
[optimal stimulation range](optimal-stimulation-range.md) covers cases
where too little ambient input, not just too much, degrades output — a
floor on stimulation, not only a ceiling.

Window width also isn't purely a fixed trait — it can narrow temporarily
as a state effect of accumulated, unresolved load on top of whatever the
person's baseline width is. One first-person description of this
narrowing under cumulative strain: "my sensory threshold has become
incredibly thin and delicate," describing triggers that would ordinarily
be tolerable becoming overwhelming after prolonged unaddressed demand.
This means the same trigger can be well inside the window on one occasion
and outside it on another for the same person, depending on how much
unresolved load is already carried — not because the trigger itself
changed.
