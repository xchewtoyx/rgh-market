Masking is the ongoing suppression of natural self-soothing and
self-regulating behaviour — avoiding stims, forcing eye contact,
restricting movement — in order to appear less conspicuous. That
suppression is itself work: it runs concurrently with whatever the visible
task is, and it blocks the very behaviours that would otherwise offload
sensory and emotional load in real time. The result is twofold: capacity
drains faster than the task alone would predict, and the load that masking
prevents from being released in the moment accumulates instead.

Masking also adds a specific cognitive cost on top of the suppression
itself: translating a more direct, efficient natural communication style
into indirect, socially-expected phrasing in real time is described as
heavily taxing to executive function, running as continuous overhead
alongside whatever the conversation is actually about. A closely related
workplace-specific cost is consciously, analytically decoding unwritten
social and hierarchy cues that others absorb automatically — again running
as continuous background overhead on top of the actual work.

This matters for demand estimation: two people doing the identical visible
task can face very different true demand if one of them is also masking
throughout it. A capacity budget built only from the visible task list will
under-count load for anyone who is masking, and will look like it doesn't
add up — the person seems to be doing "less" than a schedule implies yet
still ending up saturated. See the
[nervous-system load model](nervous-system-load-model.md) for the broader
account of why baseline depletion rate can run ahead of what a shared plan
assumes.

Masking's cost is also often shifted in time rather than avoided:
sustaining it can produce a short-term gain (avoiding an explanation,
appearing more capable in the moment) at the price of a larger long-term
capacity cost paid later, once the accumulated drain outpaces what ongoing
recovery can offset. A demand model that only checks the immediate
cost/benefit of masking in a given interaction will miss this — the
ledger only balances if the deferred cost is counted too.

The Henderson authors' **Flurb** thought experiment makes the hidden load
concrete: imagine navigating every interaction under unfamiliar, effortful
rules (forbidden eye contact, mandatory shoulder-touching, blaring music,
extreme heat) while trying to connect, advocate, and work. Autistic people
often face analogous mismatches without the rules being stated — and bear
the full burden of adaptation. The **Mitch** wheelchair analogy reframes
"you got here, didn't you?": arriving exhausted after dragging oneself up
stairs is success only if the cost is ignored. Someone can appear to
"blend in" while eye contact, small talk, and sensory tolerance remain
actively effortful behind the scenes.

Compensatory techniques are learned strategies, not intuitive social
skill — they work in rehearsed situations and fail in novel ones, and they
consume cognitive, emotional, and sensory resources even when they succeed.
First-person accounts describe masking as "playing Tetris non-stop" on top
of the actual job, or as a second language never spoken as fluently as the
first. **Being able to compensate is not the same as not having the
challenge** — the vulnerability (burnout, headaches, dissociation, pain,
exhaustion) sits behind the camouflage observers rarely see. For the
capability-versus-capacity distinction, visible social performance can
look like capability while the true limit is headroom consumed by
compensation; see [designing around capacity
limits](designing-around-capacity-limits.md).

Suppressing stims and holding the body still through **constant force**
rather than relaxed calm is a major channel of hidden drain — see
[movement suppression as hidden capacity
drain](movement-suppression-as-hidden-capacity-drain.md) and [stimming as
real-time capacity regulation](stimming-as-real-time-capacity-regulation.md).
