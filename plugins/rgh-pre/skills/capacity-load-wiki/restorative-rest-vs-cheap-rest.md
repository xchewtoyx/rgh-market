---
type: concept
title: Restorative Rest versus Cheap Rest
description: >
  Not all activities that look or feel like rest actually restore
  capacity; some give fleeting relief while leaving net capacity lower,
  so recovery value must be measured by actual rejuvenation, not by
  social convention or immediate gratification.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Neff), Chapter 3 (Emotional Self-Care)"
---

"Rest" is not a uniform capacity-restoring input. The source distinguishes
**restorative rest** — activities (reading, a favorite podcast, hiking,
engaging a special interest) that genuinely rejuvenate, even when they
don't resemble socially-expected self-care (a spa day, a nap) — from
**cheap rest** — activities (e.g. passive TV bingeing) that provide quick,
fleeting gratification but leave the person more drained afterward, not
less.

The distinguishing test is outcome, not category or social legibility: does
this activity actually leave more headroom afterward, or does it just feel
like rest in the moment while net capacity keeps falling? This matters for
pacing because a schedule that allocates "rest time" without checking
whether that time is spent on restorative or cheap rest will show a
capacity deficit persisting despite apparently-adequate downtime on paper.
It also means the same activity can be restorative for one person, one
dimension, or one moment, and cheap for another — see [net capacity effect
depends on activity fit](net-capacity-effect-depends-on-activity-fit.md).
