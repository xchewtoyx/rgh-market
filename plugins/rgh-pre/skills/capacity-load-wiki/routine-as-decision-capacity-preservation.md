---
type: concept
title: Routine as Decision-Capacity Preservation
description: >
  Standardizing low-stakes choices (meals, commute, morning sequence)
  frees executive and decision capacity for demands that actually vary,
  especially when the baseline depletion rate is already high.
sources:
  - title: "Unmasked: The Ultimate Guide to ADHD, Autism and Neurodivergence for Women"
    resource: "Unmasked (Middleton), Chapter 3 (Literal Thinking and Core Trait Translations)"
  - title: "Is This Autism? A Guide for Clinicians and Everyone Else"
    resource: "Is This Autism? (Henderson, Wayland, White), Chapter 6 (Inflexibility — Lived Experience)"
---

Need for sameness is often misread as controlling rigidity; for many
autistic people it is a rational response to limited executive resources
and a draining environment. Peter B. eats the same lunch almost daily not
from boredom but to **conserve executive function for work that matters**.
Middleton describes a busy brain juggling stimuli and open decisions —
what to wear, eat, when to break — becoming overwhelmed and defaulting to
making none (e.g., skipping a meal when choosing what to cook feels too
hard). Repeating parts of a routine (same commute, breakfast, lunch order)
reduces the decision count without requiring every hour to be identical.

The benefit is capacity accounting: each eliminated trivial decision is
attention not spent on the [shared pool of voluntary
effort](shared-pool-of-voluntary-effort.md). This differs from
[scheduled batching of recurring demand](scheduled-batching-of-recurring-demand.md),
which bounds exposure to an external recurring task; here the lever is
removing variability from choices the person controls. It also differs from
inflexibility-as-failure-mode framing in other bundles — within
capacity-load, routine is a **pacing strategy** when depletion rate is high,
not a pathology to overcome for its own sake.

Unpredictability without preparation time raises demand separately: entering
a situation without being able to think through expectations produces panic
even when the event is welcome (spontaneous dinner with a friend can be
fine; a boss's last-minute schedule change is not) — see [scheduling
isolated blocks underestimates cumulative
load](scheduling-isolated-blocks-underestimates-cumulative-load.md) for
sequence effects and [camouflaging extends demand beyond the
interaction](camouflaging-extends-demand-beyond-interaction.md) for
pre-interaction preparation load.

Difficulty with **unprepared change** is a separate demand spike: panic
when a boss moves a schedule at the last minute is not necessarily
rigidity for its own sake but entering a situation without time to think
through expectations — even welcome spontaneity can be fine when the
person can prepare (known restaurant, predictable order). Drew R. prefers
chains with scripted ordering over open-ended choices because unexpected
questions fluster him; given all options in advance, he can prepare answers.
**Interruption** during a task raises load through the same executive
switching cost as external [task-switching](task-switching-cost.md) —
Leen V. dislikes being interrupted at all. Masking can hide inflexibility
cost: pretending not to mind when things change because asking for one's
way reads as "difficult" spends capacity without removing the underlying
strain (see [masking as a hidden capacity
drain](masking-as-hidden-capacity-drain.md)).
