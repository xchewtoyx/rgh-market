title: Limited Attention Budget
description: >
  Attention is a finite resource allocated across activities, and
  effortful tasks interfere with each other when their combined demand
  exceeds that budget — even when each task alone seems manageable.
sources:

"You dispose of a limited budget of attention that you can allocate to
activities, and if you try to go beyond your budget, you will fail."
Effortful activities interfere with each other, which is why several
cannot run at full intensity simultaneously — computing 17 × 24 while
making a left turn into dense traffic is the canonical failure mode.
Several easy, undemanding activities can run together (conversation on an
empty highway); passengers intuitively stop talking when the driver needs
full attention on a narrow overtaking maneuver.

System 2 normally runs in a comfortable low-effort mode with only a
fraction of capacity engaged; System 1 continuously generates suggestions
that System 2 usually endorses with little modification. System 2
mobilizes when surprise occurs, when a problem exceeds System 1's
repertoire, or for continuous self-monitoring (staying polite while angry,
staying alert while driving at night). Effortful work has bodily
correlates — tensed muscles, raised blood pressure, dilated pupils —
marking real expenditure, not mere subjective difficulty. Pupil diameter
tracks this load second by second: in paced digit-transformation tasks
(Add-1, Add-3), dilation rises with each added digit, peaks at the
hardest moment of transformation, then falls as working memory unloads —
an inverted-V curve that mirrors subjective strain. At the limit (Add-3
with a metronome), pupils can dilate by roughly half their resting area
and heart rate climbs several beats per minute; when demand exceeds what
someone can handle, dilation stops or reverses — a physiological sign of
giving up. Pupils therefore act like an electricity meter for the
_current rate_ of mental energy use, not a cumulative fuel gauge.

The invisible-gorilla demonstration shows that even automatic perception
still requires some attention allocation: an instruction to count passes
can render a nine-second gorilla walk invisible to half of viewers —
people are blind to obvious stimuli and blind to that blindness. The
same selective blindness appears under peak cognitive load: subjects doing
Add-1 while watching for a flashing letter miss it almost half the time
at peak effort but rarely at the start or end — detection failures follow
the same inverted-V as pupil dilation. When a priority instruction says
"do the digit task first," spare capacity is not shared fairly; secondary
targets simply go unseen. Mental overload therefore behaves unlike a
household circuit breaker that cuts everything at once: System 2 protects
the highest-priority activity and allocates whatever remains, second by
second, to lower-priority inputs.

Ordinary mental life mostly runs at a comfortable walk — casual
conversation demands little more than holding two or three digits — with
occasional jogs and rare sprints. Tasks like Add-3 are sprints; most
daily work stays well below that ceiling by design (see [law of least
effort](law-of-least-effort.md)).

For capacity planning, this budget is the cognitive dimension's shared
pipe: scheduling two attention-heavy demands as if they were independent
undercounts load. See [task-switching cost](task-switching-cost.md) for
the refocusing tax when moving between demands, and [shared pool of
voluntary effort](shared-pool-of-voluntary-effort.md) for the overlapping
self-control budget that further couples unrelated effortful tasks.
