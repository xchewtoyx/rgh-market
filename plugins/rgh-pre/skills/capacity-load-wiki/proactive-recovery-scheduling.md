type: concept
title: Proactive Recovery Scheduling
description: >
  Scheduling recovery periods before saturation is reached is more
  effective than taking recovery only reactively after a breakdown,
  because by the time reactive recovery is triggered the deficit is
  already much larger.
sources:
  - title: "The Canary Code: A Guide to Neurodiversity, Dignity, and Intersectional Belonging at Work"
    resource: "The Canary Code (Praslova), Chapter 7 (Work Organization — Productivity and Purpose)"
  - title: "Discipline Is Destiny: The Power of Self-Control"
    resource: "Discipline Is Destiny (Holiday), Chapter 6 (Manage the Load)"
---

Recovery mechanisms (leave, sabbaticals, planned time off) can be used two
ways: reactively, once a person has already broken down and needs to
recover from acute or chronic overload, or proactively, scheduled in
advance as a routine part of the pacing plan regardless of whether
distress is currently visible. The source frames proactive use as
substantially more effective, specifically for preventing burnout rather
than treating it once established — by the time symptoms are visible
enough to trigger a reactive response, the accumulated deficit is far
larger than what a proactive, regularly-scheduled recovery period would
ever have let build up.

This generalizes beyond formal leave: any standing commitment that
recurs on a schedule works better as a capacity plan when its recovery
periods are placed on the calendar ahead of time as a default, not
triggered only once a problem is already legible. Waiting for visible
signs before scheduling recovery means the recovery window is being sized
to match a deficit that has already grown large, rather than to match the
load actually being carried.

**Load management** names the same principle from the other direction:
sometimes the disciplined choice is not to push through but to rest
deliberately before saturation — the sports origin of the term (resting star
players during a grueling schedule to preserve capacity for what matters
later) generalizes to any domain where skipping recovery is a short-term
bargain paid with interest later. Skipping workouts and overtraining both
end at the same place; so do overwork and chronic under-recovery. The
"off-season" — a standing period where demand is intentionally lowered —
is a model other professions rarely adopt but that serves the same
proactive function as scheduled leave or reduced load during predictable
high-demand stretches.
