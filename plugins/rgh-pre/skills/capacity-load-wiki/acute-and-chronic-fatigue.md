title: Acute and Chronic Fatigue
description: >
  Fatigue from inadequate recovery between demands (acute) and from
  accumulated shortfalls over time (chronic) both reduce performance
  headroom, but chronic fatigue is easier to underestimate because
  impairment creeps in gradually and often goes unnoticed.
sources:
  - title: "Resilience Engineering in Practice: A Guidebook"
    resource: "Resilience Engineering in Practice (Hollnagel et al., Eds.), Chapter 6 (FRMS Background and Fatigue Science)"
---

Fatigue is a state of reduced mental or physical performance capability —
not merely feeling tired — arising from sleep loss, extended wakefulness,
physical exertion, or workload. Two timescales matter for capacity
planning:

**Acute fatigue** follows a single work or wake period without adequate
rest before the next one. Recovery is possible if the gap is long and
restorative enough; the deficit is bounded to that cycle.

**Chronic fatigue** accumulates when acute shortfalls repeat without full
recovery between them. Research on repeated small sleep restrictions shows
the effect can approach that of total sleep deprivation even when each
night's loss looks minor — and people are often poor judges of their own
impairment under chronic restriction, behaving less cautiously while
performing worse. Chronic fatigue is less studied than acute but may be
more dangerous precisely because it is invisible to the person carrying
it.

Sleep and circadian rhythm are one major input; **workload** — the nature
and intensity of waking activity, including non-work hours — is the other.
Fatigue management therefore spans employer scheduling and personal life
outside duty, not only "hours on the clock." See [sleep debt compounds
across dimensions](sleep-debt-compounds-across-dimensions.md) for how
overnight shortfalls propagate into next-day cognitive and emotional
headroom, and [proactive recovery
scheduling](proactive-recovery-scheduling.md) for building recovery gaps
before chronic accumulation hardens into a baseline.

The fatigue-to-performance link is not linear: very high alertness can
produce overconfidence and reduced caution, while very low alertness
impairs controlled processing — with medium alertness often supporting the
best self-monitoring. Fatigue awareness may be part of what explains this
curve; as alertness falls, the ability to notice and compensate for
impairment falls with it.
