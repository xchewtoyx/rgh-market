type: concept
title: Designing Around Capacity Limits
description: >
  Treating recurring sensory, energy, and social limits as real
  constraints to design commitments around — rather than as failures to
  overcome — prevents the repeated overshoot-and-crash pattern that comes
  from ignoring them.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Neff), Chapter 4 (Mental Self-Care); Chapter 5 (Social Self-Care)"
  - title: "The Canary Code: A Guide to Neurodiversity, Dignity, and Intersectional Belonging at Work"
    resource: "The Canary Code (Praslova), Chapter 6 (Work Environment — Flexible Spaces)"
  - title: "Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder: A Handbook for Diagnosis and Treatment, 4th Edition"

A capacity limit that keeps reasserting itself — the same kind of task or
environment reliably produces saturation — is information about sustainable
load, not evidence of a skill deficit to push through. The source's
example is career-level: recognizing that a given work environment would
reliably cause burnout, and restructuring life and work around that limit
(changing role or environment entirely) rather than repeatedly trying to
override it and recovering from the fallout each time. Treated this way,
naming a limit stops being a one-time inventory and becomes an ongoing
input to planning — new limits surface as circumstances change, and each
one gets folded into future commitments rather than treated as a problem
already solved.

This is a practical form of the capability-versus-capacity distinction:
the recurring struggle isn't that the underlying task is unlearnable, it's
that the *commitment level* built around it exceeds
sustainable capacity. The fix accordingly targets the commitment — how
much of this kind of demand gets scheduled, and in what environment —
rather than trying to raise skill at tolerating the overshoot. Ignoring a
known limit doesn't remove the cost; it just defers it to a later,
often larger, recovery debt.

The same logic applies at smaller scale to a single dimension: the "social
battery" framing treats social capacity as having a real, individual-
specific size — some people's is simply smaller — rather than treating a
lower social appetite as something to overcome. The corresponding design
move is choosing engagement formats that fit inside the size actually
available (see [net capacity effect depends on activity
fit](net-capacity-effect-depends-on-activity-fit.md)) and declining
invitations that don't, rather than forcing attendance and paying for it
afterward.

Once a limit is accepted rather than fought, it can be built into the
calendar ahead of time as a standing feature of the plan rather than
addressed only when it's already been breached — see [proactive recovery
scheduling](proactive-recovery-scheduling.md). Before concluding a limit
is a stable personal trait to design around at all, it's worth checking
whether the limit is actually being manufactured by a fixed environment —
see [environmental mismatch as the source of disabling
struggle](environmental-mismatch-as-source-of-struggle.md).

When the limiting factor is weak internal representation rather than raw
capacity — time blindness, poor working memory, low internally generated
motivation — the corresponding design move is to **externalize** at the
point of performance: make cues, rules, deadlines, and motivators physical
and salient in the immediate context rather than asking the person to hold
them internally. Temporal myopia is addressed by shrinking gaps between
action and consequence (daily steps with immediate feedback instead of one
distant deadline). Motivation prostheses (token rewards, arranged
incentives at the task) are not crutches to remove once "fixed" — for
chronic executive-function deficits they are permanent scaffolding, like
hearing aids, not temporary training wheels.

**Reducing self-imposed demand** is another design move when the
environment is not the only source of overload: a lifelong habit of
overcompensation can stack more commitments than neurology can sustain
even when external demands are negotiable. Cutting intensity and count of
self-imposed "shoulds" — accepting that disabled is real, not a failure of
effort — frees capacity that perfectionism and internalized ableism were
consuming. Recovery plans that add more outings, socializing, or
conventional self-care rituals without checking fit can worsen deficit
when those activities are net-depleting for the person (see [social energy
as spendable capacity](social-energy-as-spendable-capacity.md)).
