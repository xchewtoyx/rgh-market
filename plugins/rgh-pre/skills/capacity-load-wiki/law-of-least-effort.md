---
type: concept
title: Law of Least Effort
description: >
  Given several ways to reach the same goal, people gravitate toward the
  least attention-demanding path — effort is a cost in the economy of
  action, and this bias shapes both habit formation and capacity planning.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking, Fast and Slow"
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), Chapter 3 (The Lazy Controller)"
---

System 2 is defined by effortful operations and a built-in reluctance to
spend more attention than strictly necessary — "laziness" in Kahneman's
terms, not moral weakness but a default cost-minimization strategy. If
multiple courses of action achieve the same outcome, people eventually
drift toward the one that taxes the [limited attention
budget](limited-attention-budget.md) least. Skill acquisition itself
follows this calculus: practice is undertaken when expected benefits
outweigh the effort cost, and as [skill reduces cognitive
demand](skill-reduces-cognitive-demand.md) the same goal becomes cheaper
to reach.

For capacity planning, the law explains why overloaded people do not
merely "try harder" indefinitely — they reroute, simplify, defer, or
default to habitual responses unless structure forces the harder path.
Breaking work into easy steps, writing intermediate results down, and
avoiding simultaneous high-load operations are everyday applications of
the same principle: conduct mental life at a walk when possible rather
than scheduling sprints by default. It also cautions against interpreting
every shortcut as a capability gap; sometimes the person is correctly
optimizing under a real attention cost.
