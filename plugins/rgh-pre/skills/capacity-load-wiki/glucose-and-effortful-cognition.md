type: concept
title: Glucose and Effortful Cognition
description: >
  Effortful mental work including self-control measurably draws down
  blood glucose, and restoring it can prevent the performance drop that
  otherwise follows a depleting task.
sources:
  - title: "Thinking, Fast and Slow"
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman), Chapter 4 (Effort and Self-Control)"
  - title: "Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder: A Handbook for Diagnosis and Treatment, 4th Edition"
    resource: "Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (Barkley, Ed.), Chapter 16 (Treatment Implications of the Extended Phenotype Model of EF)"
---

The nervous system consumes a disproportionate share of glucose relative to
its size, and effortful mental activity — reasoning, self-control,
inhibition — draws it down in a way that is more than metaphorical. In
Baumeister's lemonade experiment, participants who performed a
self-control task and then drank glucose-sweetened lemonade did not show
the usual post-depletion performance drop on a subsequent override task;
those who drank artificially sweetened lemonade did.

This matters for capacity planning in two ways. First, depletion from the
[shared pool of voluntary effort](shared-pool-of-voluntary-effort.md) has
a physiological correlate — low blood glucose worsens subsequent
self-regulation, alongside stress, illness, alcohol, and sleep loss as
known amplifiers. Second, replenishment is not only "rest later": small
glucose intake during or after a self-regulation-heavy period is one of
the factors shown to help the pool recover faster (see [factors that
replenish the self-regulatory
pool](self-regulatory-resource-replenishment.md)).

The lever is situational, not a license to sugar-load through every hard
day: it identifies one concrete input that can unblock recovery when a
demand stack has already drawn down the pool, especially when meals or
sleep have been skipped and hunger is compounding the deficit.
