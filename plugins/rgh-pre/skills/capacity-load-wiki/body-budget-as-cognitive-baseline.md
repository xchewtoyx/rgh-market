Lisa Feldman Barrett's **body budget** frames the brain as constantly
regulating the body's resources; when the body is physically overdrawn,
the brain's capacity for controlled thinking, resilience, and emotional
regulation falls with it. Physical intemperance or neglect does not stay
in the body — it shows up as bad decisions, distraction, fear, and
extreme emotion. Holiday extends bodily discipline beyond asceticism: the
point is not pleasurelessness but keeping the physical substrate funded
enough that the mind can run.

The HALT rubric (Hungry, Angry, Lonely, Tired) is a quick solvency check
before judging a struggle as purely cognitive or moral: any of those
states raises demand and lowers headroom across [dimensions of personal
capacity](dimensions-of-personal-capacity.md). See [sleep debt compounds
across dimensions](sleep-debt-compounds-across-dimensions.md) and
[glucose and effortful cognition](glucose-and-effortful-cognition.md)
for specific funding channels; see [nervous-system-on-the-outside load
model](nervous-system-load-model.md) when baseline depletion rate is already
high before HALT factors stack.

Temperance here is instrumental: restrain the body so it does not overrun
the mind — "we choose the hard way" on sleep, load, and discomfort partly
because an unfunded body makes every other capacity problem harder to solve.

Low-bar movement maintenance fits here: walking (~20 minutes) can put the
brain in a better state for executive work; running adds circulation and
post-exercise clarity for some people. Balance practice (yoga, single-leg
stands) is another physical input that may improve perceived self-control
when executive function is already thin. The bar should stay low enough to
sustain — see [self-maintenance as preventive capacity
work](self-maintenance-as-preventive-capacity-work.md) — not high enough
that the maintenance plan itself becomes a new overload source.
