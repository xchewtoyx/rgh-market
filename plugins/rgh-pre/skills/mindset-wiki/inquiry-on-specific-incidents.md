---
type: concept
title: Inquiry on Specific Incidents
description: >
  Deep inquiry works best on one concrete moment and the exact thoughts
  believed then — not on global labels — including when the person is no
  longer physically present because the mental image persists.
sources:
  - title: "Loving What Is, Revised Edition: Four Questions That Can Change Your Life"
    resource: "Loving What Is (Byron Katie), ch. 4"
---

General statements ("he was abusive, violent, hurtful, damaging, and a
horrible father") can start inquiry but are not the deep work. The method
targets one specific incident and the thoughts believed in that exact
moment — what you were thinking and believing when it happened, or when
the memory replays now.

This applies even when the other person is dead: "Fathers don't die; they
live in here" — in the thoughts and images that persist in the mind now.
The Work addresses the living mental representation, not the literal absent
person. The suffering is happening now, in present belief and replay.

In extreme cases, the pain often comes not from acknowledging that someone
did horrible things, but from believing they were *only* horrible — a
one-sided story that [argues with a fuller
reality](arguing-with-reality.md). Turnarounds such as "He was a wonderful
father" are explicitly *trying on*, not asserting fact — "like putting
lipstick on a pig." The point is to balance a story rigid enough to
sustain ongoing suffering, not to excuse harm. "No one is only horrible."

A guided re-entry into a childhood scene can test the thoughts the child
believed ("they're never coming back for me") while separately noticing:
aside from what she's thinking and believing, is the little girl okay?
"Technically she's okay." The illusion is a contest between imagined and
real, but the real doesn't compete. A turnaround ("They are coming back for
me") can be as true in that moment as the original fear — one is not more
valid than the other until belief selects one.

Turnarounds to the self often surface actions taken toward the mental
image of the other — confrontational letters, destroying gifts, screaming
— named as violence or abuse *you* did "in his name," which can be where
self-hatred comes from. Apply [the four questions of
inquiry](the-four-questions-of-inquiry.md) and [inquiry
turnarounds](inquiry-turnarounds.md) to each item. When a story has fused
with [identity](belief-identity-fusion.md) ("I am only a victim of a
horrible father"), balancing it threatens that identity — which is often
why the ego resists turnarounds even when they offer relief.
