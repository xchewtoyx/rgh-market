  Children can run the same four questions on scary or angry thoughts —
  with simpler vocabulary, monster-dialogue play, and parents modeling
  honest inquiry first.

Inquiry addresses thoughts, not age. Children hold the same stressful
concepts as adults — "I want my mother to love me," "People shouldn't be
mean" — and can investigate them with [the four questions](the-four-questions-of-inquiry.md).
Use simpler words when needed, but not baby talk: "Children know when they
are being talked down to."

**Monster under the bed (age five):** Standard questions on "There's a
monster under my bed" — Is it true? Can you absolutely know? What's your
proof? How do you react when you believe it? Who would you be without the
thought? The child moved from fear to laughter. Technique for monster/ghost
fears: have the child close her eyes, talk to the monster face-to-face, ask
what it wants, report back — in Katie's experience children always report
something kind ("He's lonely," "He wants to play"). Lesson: "It's not the
monster you're afraid of, it's the thought." Reported outcome: nightmares
stop; children return willingly because inquiry built shared understanding.

**Sibling rivalry (age four):** David tried to hurt his infant sister
because she "should play with me" — a belief learned from parents who
promised the baby would be his playmate. Parents did inquiry on each other
first in another room, then read their worksheets aloud in front of David
so he saw honest anger was safe to voice. His worksheet: "She should play
with me." Key question: "How did you learn that babies should play ball
with you?" — "My mommy and daddy." Once parents explained babies must grow
first and apologized, behavior stopped.

Children "come to inquiry so easily... when we really want to be free."
Pair with [children are not responsible for your
peace](children-are-not-responsible-for-your-peace.md) for parent-side
inquiry.
