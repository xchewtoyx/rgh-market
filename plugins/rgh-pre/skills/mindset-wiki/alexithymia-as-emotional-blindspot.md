Alexithymia — inability to recognize or describe one's own emotions — is
common among autistic people and overlaps with [delayed
interoception](delayed-interoception.md). Emotional regulation differences
can present as an apparent absence of reaction where one would be expected:
not crying at sad events (may take longer to process), not smiling when
happy, not responding to others' emotions in expected ways.

This is not lack of feeling but difficulty mapping internal states to words
and outward display. A common coping pattern when feelings cannot be felt
directly is to **reason through** them instead — analyzing environmental
risk to substitute for fear, or "logic-ing" whether distress is warranted by
situation severity. This is time-consuming, exhausting, and frequently wrong
(ER visits for minor injuries while ignoring serious ones; running into danger
while avoiding safety). In relationships it can mean trying to deduce an
"algorithm for love" from books rather than accessing felt connection.

In "decoupling" models, physiological arousal from an emotional state may
not integrate with conscious awareness of that arousal — a disruption between
subjective experience and bodily signal. Pain and other interoceptive cues can
be similarly flattened (e.g. rating all pain the same regardless of severity).

Tools like emotion-sensation wheels, [body scan
technique](body-scan-technique.md) check-ins, and charting feelings in your
**own vocabulary** (colors, non-standard words) rather than forcing standard
labels can bridge the gap. Difficulty completing a body-based check-in, or
shutting down at the naming step, is itself diagnostic information.

Many autistic people were trained in childhood to ignore emotional signals —
eating anxiety-provoking foods, going to overload-inducing places — which
deepens the blindspot. Recovery often starts with deliberately tracking and
acknowledging feelings daily, then factoring emotion (not logic alone) into
decisions — see [emotion as decision input](emotion-as-decision-input.md).
Explicitly identifying bodily discomfort and its associated feeling, then
communicating it, supports boundary-setting in relationships.

Pair with [emotional regulation as
skill](emotional-regulation-as-skill.md) and [naming emotions
specifically](naming-emotions-specifically.md).
