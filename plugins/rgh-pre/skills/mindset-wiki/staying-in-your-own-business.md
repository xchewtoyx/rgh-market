There are exactly three kinds of business: mine, yours, and God's — where
"God's business" means anything outside anyone's control (earthquakes,
when you will die, other people's choices). Much stress comes from living
mentally out of your own business. Thoughts like "You need to get a job,"
"I want you to be happy," or "You should be on time" put you in someone
else's business. Worry about uncontrollable events puts you in God's
business.

Being mentally in someone else's business produces separation and hurt.
When the thought was "My mother should understand me," the felt result was
loneliness — and every episode of hurt traced back to being in someone
else's business. "If you are living your life and I am mentally living
your life, who is here living mine? We're both over there." Believing you
know what's best for someone else — even in the name of love — produces
tension, anxiety, and fear.

A practical test when stressed: ask whose business you are mentally in.
The answer can be clarifying to the point of laughter. Repeated practice
can reveal how little of your mental life actually belongs in your own
business — and that life often runs well on its own when you return to it.
This pairs with [arguing with reality](arguing-with-reality.md): many
"should" thoughts are attempts to control what is not yours to control.

**Financial example:** After losing money on a relative's stock tips, anger
at "He should bail me out" collapses when you see whose money was invested
(yours) and whose business his money is (his) — even if you signed
inheritance over to him. Turnaround: "I should bail myself out." Believing
he owes you feels "sore"; without the thought, "free." Katie: "People lie
on their deathbed at ninety saying 'It's all my uncle's fault.' We don't
have to do that anymore." See [being right versus being
free](being-right-vs-being-free.md) and [inquiry
turnarounds](inquiry-turnarounds.md).
