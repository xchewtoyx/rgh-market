Forgiving someone who hurt you uses the same sequence as any other upset:
[judge your neighbor worksheet](judge-your-neighbor-worksheet.md), write it
down, apply [the four questions of
inquiry](the-four-questions-of-inquiry.md), then [inquiry
turnarounds](inquiry-turnarounds.md).

Real forgiveness means discovering "that what you thought happened,
didn't" — until you see there's nothing to forgive, you haven't really
forgiven. A woman who believed she had forgiven her brother's murderer
found bitterness flood back at his parole hearing — the Work is "about 100
percent forgiveness," and partial release doesn't count. The usual block:
identity as "the one who was wronged" — the unspoken logic that letting go
"lets the perpetrator off the hook." Inquiry answers must be genuine, not
summoned spiritual compassion: "you can't fake it. Only the real deal will
set you free."

**Compassion practice vs. inquiry:** widening compassion outward (self →
friends → enemies) can soften tone without reaching the root delusion. A man
whose identity was "I am the man whose mother ruined my life" could send
loving-kindness to his story of his mother while still sitting "in a puddle
of his own imagination." Only after questioning the thought — and finding
turnaround examples like a sixth-birthday party he'd cherry-picked away —
did love "appear by itself" once no story stood between them.

**Stubbed-toe analogy:** you don't blame the bed you stub your toe on in
the dark or conclude the universe is unfair — it's just an object doing its
job. "In the end it all comes down to taking responsibility for what you're
believing." Katie's literal claim: the worst that can happen is an
uninvestigated belief system — suffering tracks the story told about events,
not the events themselves.

The framing is innocence through confusion: people harm others because they
believe unquestioned thoughts; no one would hurt another while clear. "No one
can hurt me — that's my job" — it's not what they said or did that causes
suffering, but what is believed about what they said or did.

Whenever you suffer, inquire — identify the thoughts, write them down,
question them, let answers from within set you free. "Be a child. Start
from the mind that knows nothing. Take your ignorance all the way to
freedom." This pairs with [inquiry on self-judgments](inquiry-on-self-judgments.md)
when the unforgiven enemy is partly a story about yourself, and with
[innocence through past-action inquiry](innocence-through-past-action-inquiry.md)
for self-blame over actions already taken.
