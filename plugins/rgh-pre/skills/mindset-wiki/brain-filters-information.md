---
type: concept
title: Filtering (Perception Is Always Incomplete)
description: >
  The brain discards most incoming sensory information to conserve limited
  attention, focusing only on what seems relevant to the current task —
  so any felt sense of "the full picture" is already a partial one.
sources:
  - title: Solve for Happy
    resource: "Solve for Happy (Mo Gawdat), Part 3, Chapter 9: Is It True?"
---

Attention is a limited resource, so the brain filters aggressively:
crossing a busy street absorbs enough real-time calculation that smells,
background sounds, and even passersby go unregistered. This is
demonstrated starkly by selective-attention experiments where people
asked to count basketball passes in a video routinely fail to notice
someone in a gorilla suit walking directly through the scene. Filtering
also serves protective functions under acute stress (numbing severe pain
to focus on escape, an initial period of denial after a shock), but the
same mechanism can trap someone in a negative pattern when it excludes
disconfirming or positive information along with the irrelevant noise —
missing signs that a situation is actually fine because attention has
locked onto threat-relevant details only.

The practical implication is that "what I noticed" and "what actually
happened" are never quite the same thing — the story any single moment of
attention delivers is reliably incomplete, not because of any error but
because filtering is what attention *is*. This is the base layer that the
brain's other reconstructive habits — [filling gaps with
assumptions](brain-fills-gaps-with-assumptions.md), [treating predictions
as fact](predictions-treated-as-fact.md), [memory
distortion](memory-distortion-through-blending.md) — build on top of, each
compensating for filtered-out information in its own unreliable way.
