type: concept
title: Calibration and the Bet Test
description: >
  Stated confidence is only meaningful if it's calibrated — a genuine
  prediction of being right that fraction of the time — and imagining a
  real bet on the belief is a reliable way to reveal whether a stated
  confidence is an honest estimate or just a comfortable-sounding claim.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 6: How Sure Are You?"
---

Certainty feels good — simple, comfortable, and a marker of competence —
which makes overconfidence a chronic default rather than an occasional
lapse; even trained professionals routinely turn out less accurate than
their own stated confidence implied. Calibration is the corrective habit:
treating a stated confidence level as a genuine, checkable prediction
("70% sure" should mean actually being right about 70% of similarly
confident claims), not just a report of how much doubt is felt. It is a
fast-learnable skill — a couple of hours of deliberate practice against
checkable questions (predicting an answer, then scoring how often each
confidence bucket turned out right) produces a real improvement, at least
within the domain practiced.

The **press secretary vs. board of directors** distinction gives a quick
way to catch an uncalibrated claim in the moment: a press secretary makes
whatever confident public claim serves the message, regardless of the
underlying reality; a board of directors is incentivized to form an honest
best guess because the company's actual fate depends on getting it right.
Most casual claims of certainty — "I'm 100% sure," "there's no way" — are
press-secretary talk: fast, pat, and unexamined. The board-of-directors
question underneath is "how would I actually bet on this?"

The **bet test** operationalizes that question directly: take a belief
stated with apparent certainty and ask whether it would survive an actual
stake — "would I bet $1,000 that I'll still feel this way in a week?",
"would I bet a month's salary that a hired hacker couldn't get in?" A
belief that visibly hesitates under this framing (even though nothing
changed about the evidence) reveals that the original claim was
overconfident performance rather than an honest estimate. A more precise
version, the **equivalent bet**, compares betting on the belief against
betting on drawing a specific-colored ball from a box with a known ratio,
adjusting the ratio until the two bets feel equally appealing — the point
of indifference is an honest, quantified confidence estimate, arrived at
by feel rather than by trying to state a number directly.

This is a concrete instantiation of [is it true?](is-it-true-technique.md)
applied specifically to confidence rather than content — instead of
asking whether a claim is true, it asks how sure the claim actually
deserves to be, which is often the more useful question when the claim
can't be fully verified either way.
