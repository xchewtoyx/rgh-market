Distress tolerance sits between two failure modes: being overwhelmed by a
difficult feeling (losing the capacity to function or think clearly while
it is present) and avoiding the feeling altogether through numbing or
distraction (see [escape as avoidance](escape-as-avoidance.md)). Tolerating
distress means deliberately staying in contact with the feeling — noticing
it, naming it, letting it move through — without either suppressing it or
being consumed by it.

Building this capacity is what makes it possible to feel
[pain](pain-vs-suffering.md) fully in the moment it occurs rather than
either blocking it (escape) or reliving it indefinitely afterward
(rumination).

Distress tolerance is best built graduated, starting from low-stakes
discomfort before attempting it with a strong emotion — an old version of
the same instruction: practice equanimity on small pains first (a
headache, an earache) before trying to hold it under a larger one.
Sitting with an itch without scratching it, delaying a meal past the
first hunger signal, or going a period without speaking unless necessary
all exercise the same underlying skill — noticing an uncomfortable impulse and staying present
with it rather than immediately resolving it. Passive difficult states
(boredom, sadness) are typically easier starting points than active ones
with a strong action urge (anger, fear); a useful early exercise is
sitting in a quiet room with no distractions at all, purely to build
tolerance for boredom as a felt state. Once the basic capacity is built on
low-stakes discomfort, it transfers to the harder case of a strong emotion
in the moment — see [the buffer between feeling and
action](buffer-between-feeling-and-action.md) for applying the same skill
specifically to emotional impulses.

A practical aid for moments of acute distress is assembling a distress-
tolerance kit in advance — a small set of physical items (a textured
object, a soothing scent, a photo, a written reminder) kept ready so that
a healthy coping tool is immediately at hand rather than needing to be
improvised while already overwhelmed. Preparing this kind of resource
ahead of time is the same principle as [concrete implementation
plans](concrete-implementation-plans.md): a plan made calmly in advance is
far more reliably executed than one invented in the moment it's needed.

**Autistic distress-tolerance exercise** (once anxious, safety confirmed):
take five natural breaths counting them; then three breaths with exhale longer
than inhale (4 seconds in, 8 out); label what you see around you; observe the
uncomfortable emotion without acting or fleeing. Ask: "Am I having a meltdown?"
(if yes, follow a pre-written plan). Ask: "Am I reacting to a life event or
something I can control?" — change controllable factors or use the meltdown
plan. Allow the emotion; ask what it suggests might help; build a personal
self-soothing list (friend call, walk, sensory bag, pet an animal).

Many autistic people were trained to override discomfort (forced foods,
pushing through sensory overwhelm), teaching them not to listen to bodies —
adaptive for minor tolerance but toxic when tuning out danger. Learn to
listen to discomfort and set healthy boundaries instead.
