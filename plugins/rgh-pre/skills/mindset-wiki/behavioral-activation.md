Low mood and low energy tend to produce a self-reinforcing withdrawal
cycle: reduced activity brings less motivation and more lethargy, which
brings further reduced activity, while neglected everyday tasks pile up
in the background and generate their own compounding sense of being
overwhelmed. The instinct this produces — wait to feel motivated before
acting — has the causality backward for breaking the cycle: **"action is
the first step, not motivation."** Acting first, even before any
motivation is felt, is what reliably produces the shift in mood and
energy that motivation was being waited for.

Two distinct kinds of activity matter, and neither substitutes for the
other. Pleasurable activities supply positive feeling directly. Achievement
-oriented tasks — clearing a backlog, finishing a small chore, paying a
bill — supply something low mood specifically undermines: a sense of
being in control and moving forward, which pure pleasure doesn't provide
on its own. A useful week deliberately mixes both rather than leaning on
either alone; some activities (tidying a room, cooking a meal) supply
both at once.

The common failure mode is setting the bar too high — attempting a return
to full activity level in one step, which triggers exactly the automatic
thoughts ("I'll probably fail at this too") that block starting at all,
and produces disappointment that deepens the low mood the activity was
meant to counter. The corrective is **graded task assignment**: break any
task down until it's achievable at current capacity, and build up from
there rather than starting at the target level — get out of bed for ten
minutes rather than the whole day, wash five plates rather than clean the
kitchen. A useful variant when even a small amount-based goal still feels
too big is a **time-based goal** instead of an amount-based one — read for
five minutes rather than finish a chapter, since a fixed time is easier
to complete successfully than an open-ended amount, and completing it is
what matters most, not how much got done. This is the same underlying
move as [the five-minute rule for task
initiation](five-minute-rule-for-task-initiation.md), applied at the
level of a whole activity schedule rather than a single task.

Depressive fatigue differs from ordinary tiredness: rest relieves ordinary
tiredness, but depressive fatigue may call for increased activity rather
than rest, which can worsen it. Test personally what happens when resting
versus increasing activity — the invitation is empirical, not prescriptive.

Because [negativity bias](negativity-bias.md) predicts an activity will
feel less enjoyable or less worth doing than it turns out to be, treating
each attempt as a genuine experiment — rating mood before and after,
rather than assuming in advance whether it will help — keeps the process
honest and builds a personal record of what actually shifts mood, instead
of relying on how appealing an activity sounds beforehand.

## Withdrawal and inactivity as maintaining factors

Withdrawal and inactivity are among the most fundamental maintaining
factors in depression — a vicious cycle of isolation and low mood. More
time in bed to counter fatigue means more inactivity and less energy.
Isolating to avoid feeling flat or guilty about burdening friends seems
sensible but backfires: less pleasure, less support, more piled-up
problems. Believing yourself undeserving of friendship and acting on that
belief reinforces it. Social contact need not involve discussing depression
— often better not to; superficial talk and hearing others' lives gives a
break from your own thoughts, and others treating you normally helps you
feel more normal. **Rule of thumb:** if you want to feel normal again,
start acting as you would normally. Resist harmful self-comparisons when
depressed — the worst time to self-evaluate is when mood is low.

## Activity scheduling

Use a weekly activity schedule with realistic balance of activity and rest.
Build up gradually — if bed-bound for days, moving to a chair is already
progress. Start small; don't overload the schedule or you risk overwhelm,
sinking back into inactivity, and self-criticism for "ineffectiveness."
Plan gradual increase from where you are now, not where you think you
should be. Include self-care: bathing, laundry, tidying, cleaning — neglect
of self and environment deepens depression while environment strongly
affects mood.
