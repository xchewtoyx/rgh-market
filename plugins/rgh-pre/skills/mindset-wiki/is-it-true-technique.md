title: "\"Is It True?\" Technique"
description: >
  Repeatedly asking "is it true?" of a thought or story, at each
  successive layer, until it collapses from a sweeping judgment into a
  bare, minimal, factually defensible statement.
sources:

Starting from a strong, sweeping claim about a person or situation (e.g.
"my daughter is a pain in the neck"), the technique is to ask "is it
true?" — and when the honest answer requires walking the claim back
("well, she's impossible to deal with" → "is *that* true?" → "she's
always rude" → "is *that* true?"), keep asking at each successive,
weaker version until arriving at a residue that is actually fully
defensible against the question ("her recent behavior indicates she might
be a bit irritated"). Each round strips away one layer of [exaggeration
and peak shift](exaggeration-and-peak-shift.md), [labeling](labels-strip-context.md),
or [emotional bias](emotional-bias-in-judgment.md) that had been folded
into the original claim, leaving only what genuinely holds up.

The gap between the original claim and the final residue is itself
informative — it's a direct measure of how much distortion had
accumulated in the original, confidently-felt statement. This is a more
persistent, repeatable version of [cognitive
reframing](cognitive-reframing.md): rather than a single revision, it's an
iterative interrogation that keeps applying the same question until the
statement stops shrinking further, which is a reasonably reliable signal
that what's left is close to the actual, verifiable core.

Four rules give the interrogation something concrete to check a thought
against, rather than relying on a vague sense of whether it "feels" true:
(1) a thought only counts as confirmed if it matches direct sensory
perception — sight, sound, touch, taste, smell — everything else is a
story the mind has added on top of what was actually observed (see
[assumptions fill perceptual gaps](brain-fills-gaps-with-assumptions.md));
(2) anything set in the past or future is inherently unprovable in the
present moment — a claim about why someone acted a certain way, or about
what will happen tomorrow, should be assumed false until it can actually
be verified, not treated as settled fact; (3) a strong emotional charge on
a thought is itself a signal to check it more closely, not evidence that
it's accurate — drama is not the same as truth; (4) a thought contaminated
by an unrelated past experience (a parent's behavior, a prior
disappointment, general conditioning) should be evaluated on its own
merits, isolated from the earlier event that's actually driving the
emotional weight behind it. A practical way to apply the rules under
pressure is to convert an open, ramble-inviting question ("why do I feel
like this?") into a closed one the mind must answer directly ("is this
literally true, yes or no?") — if no evidence is produced, the default is
to treat the claim as false and set it down, rather than needing to
affirmatively disprove it first.
