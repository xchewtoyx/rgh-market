---
type: concept
title: Single-Tasking
description: >
  The brain can only fully perform one demanding task at a time and pays
  a real switching cost every time it changes tasks, so deliberately doing
  one thing at a time — rather than "multitasking" — both performs better
  and starves background rumination of the resources it needs to run.
sources:
  - title: That Little Voice in Your Head
    resource: "That Little Voice in Your Head (Mo Gawdat), Part Three, Chapter 10: Welcome to the Real World"
---

What's experienced as "multitasking" is actually rapid switching between
tasks, and that switching itself consumes processing capacity on top of
whatever the tasks require individually — a controlled study comparing a
memory task done alone versus done alongside simple concurrent
verification found worse accuracy and speed in the combined condition,
with extra brain activity specific to the switching itself, not just the
sum of the two tasks. Because attention functions with sharply limited
total capacity, giving a task genuinely undivided attention — rather than
splitting it against a second task or a wandering mind — improves
performance on the task and, as a direct side effect, leaves no spare
capacity for [rumination](rumination.md) or background thought-chatter to
run at the same time (see [attention displacement to interrupt a
thought](attention-displacement-to-interrupt-thought.md) for that
mechanism applied directly to an unwanted thought).

The practical discipline is committing fully to one task at a time rather
than treating partial attention as good enough: finishing a conversation
before checking a phone, finishing one bite of food before starting the
next, listening to one thing rather than leaving media on as unattended
background, working through one number or data point at a time rather
than skimming. Deliberately isolating a single sense channel (closing the
eyes to listen, using earplugs while watching) can intensify this further
by removing the option of half-attending to something else. The habit
compounds: a mind practiced at giving one thing full attention finds it
easier to do so again next time, while a mind practiced at partial,
scattered attention finds sustained focus harder to summon when it's
actually needed.
