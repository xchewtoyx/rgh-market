The body scan guides systematic, direct attention to each part of the body in
turn, using the breath to "carry" awareness into each region. **Not a relaxation
technique** — the point is to be aware of the body as it is, not to achieve calm
(though relaxation often emerges anyway). Set the intention to **fall awake**
rather than fall asleep: be aware of experience as it unfolds, without changing
how you feel.

Move the "spotlight" through the body — left leg and foot, right leg, pelvis,
back, chest, shoulders, hands and arms, neck, face, whole head — breathing into
regions of tension on the in-breath, releasing on the out-breath. Numbness, no
sensation, and unpleasant sensations are all okay. When the mind wanders (what
minds do), gently acknowledge where it went and return — the subtle first step
is often shifting from **direct sensation** to **thinking about** the body, then
associations cascade.

## Why it steadies the mind

Thoughts are ephemeral and trigger associations that carry attention away;
detailed body sensation gives a vivid anchor moment by moment. The
[non-striving](non-striving-in-meditation.md) quality fosters calm because there
is nowhere to get to — sensations are allowed as they are, resting in being mode
rather than closing the gap the doing mind wants closed. Treating the scan as
relaxation training revives doing-mode habits — "This calm means I'm getting
somewhere" — and backfires: wanting pleasant feelings tightly, like dry sand
grasped in a fist, makes them slip away. When you stop trying to force pleasant
feelings, they are freer to emerge; when you stop resisting unpleasant ones,
they may drift away by themselves.

## No bad meditation

There is no failing at meditation as long as you are mindful of experience,
whatever it is. Impatience, restlessness, pain, or sleepiness are not failures —
everything that arises becomes the teacher. The scan frees suffering from wanting
things different than they are right now.

## Why stick with it

Programs assign extended daily practice even when immediate benefit feels absent:
it cultivates experiential knowing, reconnects with the body's role in emotion,
uncouples body-sensation/thinking links that fuel [rumination](rumination.md),
and teaches openhearted attention to intensely unpleasant sensation — skill
generalizable beyond the mat. On waking, even a single in-breath scanning the
body, or a minute breathing with the body as a whole before rising, can change
the whole day — heaviness is greatly increased by negative thoughts, whereas
gentle awareness of sensations themselves, without trying to change them, can be
immensely energizing.

## Performance attitudes and instructor stance

Depressed participants often bring achievement and social-evaluation attitudes:
"Am I doing this right?" "Will people think this is OK?" The approach is not to
stop such thoughts but to recognize them skillfully. Framing: "It is important
not to try too hard," "We are not trying to achieve any special state," and
discomfort or boredom does not mean failure — discover what they feel like in
the body and your reactions.

Participants commonly invent unstated rules ("Don't move," "Don't sleep," "Relax!")
then judge themselves for breaking them. Instructor response: "Whatever happens,
whatever comes up, that is OK" — map the weather patterns of mind and reactions
like a cartographer, without fighting or pushing away. "There isn't a right or
wrong thing to happen... it's just your experience in that moment."

Home practice is central, not optional — "the real business is learning by
experiencing for ourselves." Willingness to practice between sessions is
prerequisite; those unable to commit are advised to wait. Common obstacles
(time, place, family) are anticipated in pairs; framing includes "you do not
have to enjoy it, you just have to do it" and "Whatever happens, stick with it."
