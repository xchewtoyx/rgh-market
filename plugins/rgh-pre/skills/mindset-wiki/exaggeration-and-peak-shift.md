---
type: concept
title: Exaggeration and Peak Shift
description: >
  The brain amplifies signals — especially threats — beyond their actual
  scale to make sure they capture attention, which is why vivid, dramatic,
  or extreme information reliably crowds out calmer signals of equal or
  greater real importance.
sources:
  - title: Solve for Happy
    resource: "Solve for Happy (Mo Gawdat), Part 3, Chapter 9: Is It True?"
---

Exaggeration is a deliberate amplification strategy: a stronger signal is
more reliably noticed, so the brain inflates the felt magnitude of
threats and other salient stimuli past their actual scale — "you can
never be too careful" as a built-in bias, not just a saying. This mirrors
"peak shift," a documented pattern where a trained preference for a
stimulus generalizes toward an even more exaggerated version of that
stimulus (an animal rewarded for preferring a rectangle over a square
comes to prefer increasingly elongated rectangles more than the original
rewarded shape) — visible in human preference for exaggerated status or
attractiveness markers as well.

The clearest consequence for judgment is the availability heuristic: a
vivid, dramatic, extensively covered risk (a plane crash, a viral news
story) gets weighted in the mind far beyond its actual statistical
likelihood or scale, while a larger but less vivid or less-covered risk
gets underweighted by comparison — probability estimates shift measurably
just from having recently seen a dramatic example. This directly compounds
[catastrophizing](catastrophizing.md): the exaggeration bias is part of
why an imagined worst case can feel so viscerally real and urgent, well
past what its actual likelihood would justify. See [the safety reality
check](safety-reality-check.md) for a technique that specifically corrects
for perceived odds being driven by vividness rather than by actual base
rates.
