---
type: concept
title: Arguing with Reality
description: >
  Suffering often comes from believing a thought that insists reality
  should be different than it is right now, rather than from the event
  itself — and accepting what happened is not the same as approving it.
sources:
  - title: "Loving What Is, Revised Edition: Four Questions That Can Change Your Life"
    resource: "Loving What Is (Byron Katie), ch. 1"
---

The only time we suffer is when we believe a thought that argues with what
is. Wanting reality to be different than it is right now is compared to
trying to teach a cat to bark — hopeless, no matter how long you try.
Everyday versions: "People should be kinder," "My neighbors should take
better care of their lawn," "The line at the grocery store should move
faster." When you argue with reality, you lose — every time.

Accepting what happened is not condoning or approving it. The formulation
is narrower: what you think shouldn't have happened *should* have
happened, because it did happen, and no thinking in the world can change
that fact. Opposing reality produces tension and frustration; not opposing
it makes action "simple, fluid, kind, and fearless." The contrast is
between "I wish I hadn't lost my job" and "I lost my job; what intelligent
solutions can I find right now?" — the second stays in contact with facts
while leaving room for response.

This is operational kin to [committed acceptance](committed-acceptance.md)
and [pain vs. suffering](pain-vs-suffering.md), but emphasizes the
specific mechanism: an untrue or reality-arguing *thought* is what
generates ongoing suffering, not the bare event. The countermove is not
passivity but inquiry — testing whether the thought that argues with what
is actually holds up (see [inquiry as thought-testing](inquiry-as-thought-testing.md)).
