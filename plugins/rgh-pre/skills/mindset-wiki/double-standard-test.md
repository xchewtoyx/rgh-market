---
type: concept
title: Double Standard Test
description: >
  Checking whether a judgment being applied to a person, group, or oneself
  would still be reached if it were applied to a different person, group,
  or oneself in an otherwise identical situation.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 5: Noticing Bias"
---

The test's defining question: "am I judging this behavior by a standard I
wouldn't apply to someone else — or to myself — in the same situation?"
[Soldier mindset](soldier-mindset.md) tends to apply a stricter standard
to unwelcome cases and a looser one to welcome cases without ever noticing
the asymmetry, because only one branch of the comparison is ever actually
experienced (e.g. excusing a political ally's misconduct while condemning
identical misconduct from an opponent). Making the comparison explicit —
actually picturing the reversed case, in detail, rather than just
asserting the "correct" answer — surfaces the asymmetry directly.

This test runs equally well pointed inward: it also catches occasions
where a harsher standard is applied to oneself than would ever be applied
to someone else for the identical situation — asking a question in a
meeting perceived as "stupid" when asked by oneself, but unremarkable when
imagining a colleague asking the same question. This makes the double
standard test one of the more direct tools for catching [negativity bias
in self-talk](negativity-bias-in-self-talk.md) in the moment it's
happening: if a specific lapse or mistake would be shrugged off in someone
else, treating it as damning evidence about oneself is the double standard
made visible.
