title: Heuristic Substitution
description: >
  When a hard question has no quick answer, the mind silently answers an
  easier related question instead — and you may not notice the swap.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 10"
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 9"
---

If a satisfactory answer to a hard question isn't found quickly, System 1
finds a related easier question and answers that instead. Automatic
thinking also runs continuous basic assessments — threat level, mood,
normality, approach or avoid — without being asked, and those fast reads
are easily swapped in for harder judgments (likability of a face for
political competence, current mood for life satisfaction). The **target
question** is what you intend to assess; the **heuristic question** is
what you actually answer. A heuristic is a simple procedure that finds
adequate though often imperfect answers to difficult questions.

Examples of substitution:

- How much would you contribute to save a species? → How much emotion do I
  feel about dying dolphins?
- How happy are you with life? → What is my mood right now?
- How popular will the president be in six months? → How popular is the
  president right now?
- Will this politician go far? → Does she look like a political winner?

**Intensity matching** maps answers across unrelated dimensions that share
an underlying scale of "more." Many everyday questions — happiness,
popularity, punishment severity, future prospects — permit the word *more*,
and System 1 can match values across completely different domains: crime
severity to loudness, reading precocity to height or income. In one study,
people adjusted sound volume to match crime severity and legal punishment;
mismatched loudness pairs felt unjust. Given "Julie read fluently at four,"
people readily match her precocity to a man's height, professional income,
crime severity, or Ivy League GPA — and converge closely with others in the
same culture. That same matching operation later drives direct predictions
(e.g., GPA from reading age) that ignore regression — a natural
[System 1](fast-and-slow-thinking-heuristic.md) move that even non-statisticians'
[System 2](engaged-vs-lazy-monitoring.md) may accept.

Intensity matching then maps the heuristic answer onto the target scale
(feelings → dollars). System 2 can reject the swap, but lazy System 2 often
endorses without noticing the target question was hard.

**Mood heuristic for happiness:** Students asked about dates before general
happiness showed huge correlation; happiness first showed near zero —
romantic mood substituted for life satisfaction when primed.

**Affect heuristic** (Slovic): likes and dislikes determine beliefs about
the world — political preference shapes which arguments feel compelling;
disliking irradiated food or motorcycles predicts high perceived risk and
low benefit. System 2 often acts as apologist for System 1 emotions rather
than critic — searching mostly for consistent arguments.

The 3-D size illusion is substitution at perception: asked about printed
size, you involuntarily answer 3-D height. You knew the question but
couldn't help the easier answer.

When a judgment feels instant and confident, ask what question you actually
answered — especially for [emotional bias in
judgment](emotional-bias-in-judgment.md), [focusing
illusion](focusing-illusion.md), and [availability
heuristic](availability-heuristic.md) cases.
