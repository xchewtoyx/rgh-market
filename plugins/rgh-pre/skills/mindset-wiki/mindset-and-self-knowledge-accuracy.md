---
type: concept
title: Mindset and Self-Knowledge Accuracy
description: >
  A growth mindset produces markedly more accurate self-assessment of
  one's own ability than a fixed mindset does, because accurate feedback
  is only useful — rather than threatening — to someone who believes
  ability can change.
sources:
  - title: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success"
    resource: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success (Carol S. Dweck), Chapter 1: The Mindsets"
---

Research comparing self-estimated ability against actual performance
found that people with a [fixed mindset](fixed-mindset.md) accounted for
nearly all of the observed inaccuracy — in either direction, over- or
under-estimating — while people with a [growth mindset](growth-mindset.md)
were remarkably accurate. The explanation traces to what each mindset does
with feedback: under a fixed mindset, every piece of information about
performance is either "good news" or "bad news" about a fixed, precious
trait, creating pressure to distort it toward whichever reading feels
safer to the ego. Under a growth mindset, ability is expected to change
with effort, so accurate information — including unflattering information
— is useful data for improving rather than a threat to defend against,
removing the motive to distort it.

This directly bears on both impostor-style self-underestimation and fear
of appearing overconfident: both are, in effect, defensive distortions
away from an accurate read of one's actual ability, produced by treating
a performance assessment as a verdict on fixed worth rather than as
information to act on. The fix implied is not to try to talk oneself into
a specific self-rating, but to weaken the stakes attached to any single
rating — the growth-mindset move of expecting ability to be a moving
target automatically removes the incentive to distort self-assessment in
either direction.
