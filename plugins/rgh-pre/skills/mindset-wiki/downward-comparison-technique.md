---
type: concept
title: Downward Comparison Technique
description: >
  Deliberately comparing a situation against those who have less, rather
  than against those who have more, counteracts the default habit of
  "looking up" that guarantees perceived unfairness no matter how well
  life is actually going.
sources:
  - title: Solve for Happy
    resource: "Solve for Happy (Mo Gawdat), Part 4, Chapter 11: The Pendulum Swing"
---

Because outcomes vary across many independent dimensions (wealth, health,
looks, luck, relationships), there is always someone ahead of any person
on whichever axis attention happens to land on — comparison upward is
structurally unwinnable, since even the most fortunate person is behind
someone else on some dimension, and the habit of noticing what's lacking
relative to whoever has more inflates expectations and manufactures a
sense of unfairness regardless of actual circumstances. This "looking up"
habit compounds across any hierarchy (employees compare to managers,
managers to executives) with no natural stopping point.

The corrective is a deliberate, symmetric habit in the other direction:
comparing a hard situation against a realistically worse version of
itself, or against people who genuinely have less. This isn't
minimization of real difficulty — it's supplying the missing half of an
otherwise one-sided comparison habit, since the same reasoning that makes
upward comparison feel natural applies equally well downward, but rarely
gets applied there by default. It also extends to comparing against
imagined worse outcomes of the same event, not just against other
people's overall circumstances: a loss considered against a plausible
worse version of the same loss can shift from feeling purely unfair to
feeling, in part, fortunate — for instance, being grateful for the time
that was actually had, rather than resentful only about what wasn't.

This is a close relative of [gratitude
practice](gratitude-practice.md) — where gratitude practice directs
attention to what's already present, downward comparison specifically
recalibrates a comparison-driven judgment ("this isn't fair," "I have it
worse than I should") by supplying the comparison that was being left
out. Both work against the same underlying tendency: attention, left to
its own devices, tends to fixate on the gap toward "more" rather than
noticing the equally real distance already covered from "less."
