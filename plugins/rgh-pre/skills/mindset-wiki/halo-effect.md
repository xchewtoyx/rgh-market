---
type: concept
title: Halo Effect
description: >
  Liking or disliking one trait about a person spreads to unrelated
  traits you have not actually observed, because the mind builds a
  coherent story that fills gaps to match the overall feeling.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 8"
---

The halo effect is the tendency to like (or dislike) everything about a
person — including qualities you never actually observed — because a
global positive or negative feeling colors how every trait is read. Real
evidence is often missing; the gap is filled by a guess that fits the
emotional response. Meeting someone personable and likable, then being
asked whether she would be a generous charity donor, there is little
actual basis to infer generosity from social ease — but liking her (and
liking generosity) creates an associative predisposition to believe she
is generous, which in turn deepens how much you like her.

Solomon Asch's classic demonstration shows how strongly early
impressions shape the whole story. Participants rated identical trait
lists in different orders — "intelligent, industrious, impulsive,
critical, stubborn, envious" (Alan) versus the reverse order starting with
"envious" (Ben). Most people viewed Alan far more favorably, because
initial traits change the meaning of later ones: stubbornness in someone
already established as intelligent reads as possibly justified; the
same trait after "envious" reads as more dangerous. Ambiguous traits are
interpreted to cohere with context, much like [assumptions fill perceptual
gaps](brain-fills-gaps-with-assumptions.md). In a variant where
participants considered the first three traits and the last three as
describing two different people, most judged it implausible that all six
could describe one person — showing how sequence and first impressions
can waste later information entirely.

A practical instance: grading exam essays one student at a time, reading
all of one person's answers in sequence. Scores within a booklet become
strikingly homogeneous — the first essay sets an anchor, and ambiguous
statements in later essays get the benefit of the doubt if the first was
strong. Two equally weighted essays can yield different final grades
depending on which was read first. The felt consistency under sequential
grading is spurious; it produces cognitive ease while [System 1-style
coherence](emotional-bias-in-judgment.md) masks real inconsistency. The
countermeasure is to [decorrelate error](decorrelating-error.md) — for
example, grading every student's answer to question 1 before moving to
question 2, so each judgment is less contaminated by the halo cast by
earlier work from the same person.
