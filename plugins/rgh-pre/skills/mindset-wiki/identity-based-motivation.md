An obligation framing ("I shouldn't break promises to myself") makes
following through feel grudging — compliance with a rule, with no reward
beyond having avoided a violation. An identity framing of the same
behavior ("I'm the kind of person who follows through on their promises")
converts the same act into an affirmation of who you already are, which
generates its own immediate satisfaction rather than only a long-deferred
payoff. This matters because most beneficial behaviors — persistence
through a setback, resisting a comfortable rationalization, following
through on a hard commitment — have costs that land immediately and
benefits that only show up much later; an identity framing supplies an
immediate reward (pride, self-consistency) that offsets the immediate
cost, closing the gap that otherwise favors the easier short-term choice.

This works because the felt reward is available the moment the behavior
happens, not only once its eventual results materialize — resisting a
cheap shortcut becomes something to feel good about right away ("I don't
make excuses for myself") rather than only paying off if and when the
long-term benefit shows up. It generalizes past any one domain: the same
mechanism that makes "I'm not the kind of person who takes cheap shots"
easier to live up to than "I shouldn't take cheap shots" applies equally
to persistence, honesty, effort, or any other trait someone wants to
reliably act from rather than only aspire to.

The identity chosen this way should stay revisable rather than becoming
another fused, ego-protective identity — see [belief-identity
fusion](belief-identity-fusion.md) for what happens when an identity stops
being a source of motivation and starts being something that has to be
defended regardless of the evidence. An identity held usefully is a
standing intention ("the kind of person who follows through"), not a
verdict about fixed character that a single lapse would have to threaten.

## Outcome-based versus identity-based change

Behavior change can target three layers: **outcomes** (results you want),
**processes** (habits and systems), or **identity** (beliefs about who you
are). Outcome-based habits start with "I want X, so I'll do Y" without
examining beliefs driving actions. Identity-based habits start with who you
wish to become — "No thanks, I'm not a smoker" signals identity shift where
"No thanks, I'm trying to quit" still treats smoking as core self.

Behavior incongruent with self-image will not last: identifying as a consumer
while wanting wealth pulls toward spending; prioritizing comfort while wanting
health pulls toward relaxing. True behavior change is identity change —
motivation starts a habit, but only identity sustains it. The goal is not to
read a book but to become a reader; not to run a marathon but to become a
runner.

**Identity as double-edged sword:** once you believe an aspect of identity,
you act in alignment — people who identified as "being a voter" voted more
than those who treated voting as a mere action they wanted. But self-limiting
identity claims ("I'm not a morning person," "I'm bad at math") create
pressure to behave consistently with them even when wrong. The biggest barrier
to positive change is identity conflict; day-to-day failures look like being
too busy, but long-run cause is often self-image. Progress requires unlearning
— continuously editing beliefs and expanding identity rather than clinging to
one version.

## Votes for identity

Identity emerges from habits — every belief about yourself was learned through
experience. Habits embody identity: making the bed = organized person; writing
daily = creative person. Each habit act is a **vote** for the type of person
you wish to become; no single vote transforms belief, but accumulated evidence
builds conviction. Bad habits are votes too — perfection is not required, only
a majority for the desired identity.

**Two steps:** (1) decide the type of person you want to be; (2) prove it with
small wins. If you know outcomes but not identity, work backward: "Who could
get this outcome?" — e.g., a book author is probably consistent and reliable,
shifting focus from writing a book to being that kind of person.

Ask "What would a healthy person do?" when choosing between options — acting
like that person long enough can make you one. Pride in an identity aspect
(e.g., well-kept nails after stopping a nail-biting habit) often sustains
change more than the original willpower resolve.
