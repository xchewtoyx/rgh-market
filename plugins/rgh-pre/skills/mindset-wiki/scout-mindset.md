---
type: concept
title: Scout Mindset (Accuracy Motivated Reasoning)
description: >
  The motivation to see things as they actually are rather than as one
  wishes they were — evaluating a claim by asking "is this true?" instead
  of searching for permission to accept or reject it.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 1: Two Types of Thinking"
---

Where [soldier mindset](soldier-mindset.md) treats reasoning as defense of
a position, scout mindset treats it as mapmaking: the goal is an accurate
map of the terrain — what's actually true, what the real risks and
opportunities are — not a map drawn to be reassuring. A scout can still
hope the path ahead is clear; wanting a good outcome doesn't disqualify
someone from scout mindset. What matters is that the wanting doesn't get
to distort what actually goes on the map. Because all maps are
simplifications, being in scout mindset also means tracking which parts of
one's own understanding are shaky or likely to need revision, rather than
treating the current map as finished and settled.

The practical payoff of this stance is that "there is no such thing as a
threat to your beliefs" — discovering you were wrong about something isn't
a defeat, it's an improvement to the map, which removes the main reason
soldier mindset resists corrective information in the first place. This
reframe is directly useful for any self-talk that treats being wrong, or
being corrected, as something to fend off: under scout mindset, updating a
belief in light of new evidence is the mechanism working correctly, not a
loss. Concrete scout-mindset habits include actively asking what would
have to be true for an alternative explanation to be correct (a
clinician's version: "if this isn't X, what else would it be?"), and
treating a claim's plausibility as independent of whether it's convenient
to accept. See [mindset and self-knowledge
accuracy](mindset-and-self-knowledge-accuracy.md) for the related finding
that this same accuracy-seeking stance, applied to self-assessment
specifically, produces more accurate self-knowledge.

Feeling objective, or believing oneself to be generally fair-minded, is
not itself evidence of being in scout mindset — see [behavioral tests for
scout mindset](behavioral-tests-for-scout-mindset.md) for why only
concrete past behavior, not self-image, reliably distinguishes the two.
