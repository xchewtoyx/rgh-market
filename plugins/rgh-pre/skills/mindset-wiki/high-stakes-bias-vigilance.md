title: High-Stakes Bias Vigilance
description: >
  Intuitive errors cannot be prevented on demand, so the practical
  compromise is to recognize error-prone situations and apply extra
  scrutiny when the stakes are high — not constant vigilance everywhere.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 2"
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 8"
---

Automatic thinking cannot be switched off at will, and even when you know a
bias is possible, prevention takes [effortful
monitoring](engaged-vs-lazy-monitoring.md) that is tiring and slow —
impractical as a permanent stance and too slow to replace automatic
judgment in routine moments. The realistic standard is a compromise: learn
to recognize situations where intuitive mistakes are likely, and try
harder to avoid significant errors when the stakes are high.

Jumping to conclusions is efficient when the conclusion is likely correct,
the occasional mistake is cheap, and speed matters. It is risky when the
situation is unfamiliar, stakes are high, and more information could
change the answer — the profile where a deliberate pause, [testing a
limiting belief](testing-a-limiting-belief.md), or running a [selective
skeptic test](selective-skeptic-test.md) is worth the cost.

It is also easier to spot other people's intuitive errors than your own —
so building habits of external checking (a friend, a worksheet, a
structured inquiry) matters as much as personal resolve. This pairs with
[judgment limited by attitude more than
knowledge](attitude-over-knowledge-in-judgment.md): knowing bias names
helps mainly when you are already willing to look for them in yourself.
