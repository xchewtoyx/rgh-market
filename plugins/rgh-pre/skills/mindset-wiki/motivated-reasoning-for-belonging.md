---
type: concept
title: Motivated Reasoning for Belonging
description: >
  Deferring to a group's consensus is often a reasonable heuristic, but it
  becomes motivated reasoning specifically when a person wouldn't even
  want to find out the consensus was wrong, because dissent threatens
  their belonging.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 2: What the Soldier Is Protecting"
---

Groups carry expected beliefs, and disagreeing with them risks social
cost — anywhere from mild friction to, in extreme cases, losing
relationships or community entirely. Deferring to a group's collective
judgment is not inherently a bias; often it's a reasonable heuristic (if
many trusted people believe something, that's real evidence). The
distinguishing test for when this tips into motivated reasoning is
whether the person would even want to find out if the consensus were
wrong — genuine openness to updating differs from an aversion to looking
too closely because looking risks the belonging itself. A group's
"honor" also gets defended alongside its beliefs: criticism of something
central to group identity tends to be met with more skepticism than
equally rigorous criticism of something identity-neutral.

[Tall poppy self-downplaying](tall-poppy-self-downplaying.md) is this same
mechanism applied specifically to personal achievement rather than to a
factual belief: standing out threatens belonging, so understating one's
own accomplishment becomes the belonging-preserving move, sometimes
regardless of whether stating it plainly would actually cause the social
cost being anticipated. In both cases the corrective isn't to stop valuing
belonging — it's to notice when belonging-protection, rather than
accuracy, is quietly setting the answer in advance.
