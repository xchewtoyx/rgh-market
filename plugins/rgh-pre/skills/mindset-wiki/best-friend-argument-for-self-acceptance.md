title: Best-Friend Argument for Self-Acceptance
description: >
  Importing the more forgiving standard you would apply to a close friend's
  mistake onto yourself breaks the double standard that fuels global self-
  condemnation.
sources:
  - title: "CBT For Dummies, 4th Edition"
    resource: "CBT For Dummies (Rhena Branch, Rob Willson), ch. 16: Self-Acceptance"
---

People usually judge friends more acceptingly than themselves. The **best-
friend argument** asks you to import that standard inward: does the
punishment fit the crime? What would you say to your best friend for the
same behavior?

Three moves: **judge the behavior, not the person** — challenge a friend's
poor conduct without defining them as wholly bad; **accept failings as you
would a friend's** — a failed driving test four times does not make someone
"a total failure"; **view behavior in context with compassion** — circumstances
matter, regard for the person can persist.

An "exceptional code of conduct" applied only to yourself is an inverted
inferiority complex — as if you alone must transcend ordinary human
fallibility. In fact you fail and succeed no more extravagantly than peers.
Refraining from name-calling others also reduces your anger at their poor
behavior and reinforces [unconditional self-acceptance](unconditional-self-acceptance.md).

See also [friend to yourself](friend-to-yourself.md) and [self-compassion
statements](self-compassion-statements.md).
