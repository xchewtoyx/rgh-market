---
type: concept
title: Effort Praise vs. Ability Praise
description: >
  Praising someone for being smart or talented after a success makes them
  more fragile toward future difficulty than praising the effort or
  strategy that produced the success, even when the initial performance
  was identical.
sources:
  - title: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success"
    resource: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success (Carol S. Dweck), Chapter 3: The Truth About Ability and Accomplishment"
---

In a controlled study, students who did well on a set of problems were
praised either for ability ("you must be smart at this") or for effort
("you must have worked really hard"), despite performing identically at
the start. The two groups diverged sharply afterward: ability-praised
students avoided a harder task they could have learned from, out of fear
of exposing a flaw, while most effort-praised students chose it. Facing
subsequent harder problems, ability-praised students concluded they
"weren't smart after all" and their enjoyment and even their scores on
easier follow-up problems dropped below their own starting level; effort-
praised students read the difficulty as a cue to try harder or use a new
strategy, kept enjoying the problems, and kept improving. A striking side
effect: given the chance to report their scores to unseen peers, a large
fraction of ability-praised students lied and inflated their results,
something effort-praised students essentially didn't do — praising
ability, it turns out, can make people more willing to misrepresent
themselves to protect the image the praise created.

The mechanism is the same one behind [effort and
mindset](effort-and-mindset.md): ability-focused praise (and labels like
"gifted" or "talented" more generally) implicitly frames ability as the
thing being evaluated and as fixed, so every subsequent challenge becomes
a referendum on whether the label still holds. Praise aimed at effort,
strategy, or process instead reinforces that the *process* is what's being
recognized, which stays available regardless of outcome. This generalizes
directly to self-talk: praising oneself internally for being smart, gifted,
or talented after a win sets up the same fragility as being praised that
way by someone else — praising the specific effort, approach, or choice
that led to the result is the more resilient habit, for the same reason.
