---
type: concept
title: Impressions Versus Beliefs
description: >
  A corrected belief can coexist with a persistent felt impression — you
  can know something is false and still feel it, and self-regulation means
  refusing to act on the feeling rather than trying to switch it off.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 2"
---

Some distortions behave like visual illusions: even after measuring two
lines and forming the correct belief that they are equal length, the bottom
line still looks longer. The belief updated; the impression did not — and
you cannot simply will the impression away.

The same structure appears in [cognitive
illusions](naming-cognitive-distortions.md) that are not visual. A strong,
quick sympathy for someone with a repeated history of failed treatment can
feel compelling even when experience says it is a danger sign — the
sympathetic feeling arises involuntarily, like the unequal-lines illusion,
while the pattern itself is what should trigger caution. The workable move
is not to suppress the feeling but to recognize the pattern, hold the
correct belief, and refuse to act on the misleading impression.

This is why [catching automatic thoughts](catching-automatic-thoughts.md)
targets the voluntary endorsement of a first reaction, not the reaction
itself: the initial flinch or pull may be unavoidable; treating it as
settled truth is optional. Pair with [impressions versus
perception](observing-versus-perceiving.md) when separating raw event from
added judgment, and with [high-stakes bias
vigilance](high-stakes-bias-vigilance.md) when deciding how hard to
monitor intuitive impressions in a given moment.
