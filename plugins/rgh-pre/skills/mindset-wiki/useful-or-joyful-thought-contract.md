---
type: concept
title: The Useful-or-Joyful Thought Contract
description: >
  Deliberately setting and enforcing a standing rule that the mind is only
  "allowed" to offer joyful or genuinely useful thoughts, rejecting and
  re-requesting anything else, turns passive suffering through a thought
  loop into an active, ongoing negotiation.
sources:
  - title: That Little Voice in Your Head
    resource: "That Little Voice in Your Head (Mo Gawdat), Chapter 9: In Limbo"
---

Waiting passively for an unwanted thought loop to resolve itself on its
own is often an implicit holdover from childhood, when distress reliably
summoned outside help — a pattern adulthood doesn't honor. The
alternative, once [thought defusion](thought-defusion.md) makes it
possible to treat the mind as a separate party rather than as oneself, is
to negotiate a standing contract with it: any thought offered gets sorted
against two axes, useful-or-useless and joyful-or-painful, and only
thoughts landing in useful or joyful are accepted — anything
useless-and-painful is explicitly rejected and a replacement demanded, out
loud if needed ("I know, brain, but—"), rather than sat with passively.

This treats the recurring, unwanted thought the way an alarm is treated —
it exists to demand a response, not to be endured — and rests on the
claim that the mind, like any trained system, does largely what it is
consistently instructed to do; it snaps to full compliance when a real
external authority (a deadline, a boss) requires a different train of
thought, which is evidence the redirection is available on demand, not
only when forced from outside. Two concrete moves fill the "accepted"
side of the contract once an unhelpful thought is rejected: supplying [a
specific positive thought to replace it](positive-thought-replacement.md)
when the painful thought is simply unhelpful, or, when the thought points
at a genuinely unresolved problem, redirecting to [what can actually be
done about it](rumination.md) rather than continuing to replay the
problem without acting on it. Enforcing the contract is a repeated,
ongoing practice, not a one-time decision — every fresh appearance of the
rejected thought is met with the same redirection again, for as long as
it keeps recurring.
