---
type: concept
title: Leaning into Confusion
description: >
  A small detail that doesn't quite fit a current explanation is a clue
  worth pursuing, not friction to be smoothed over — explaining an anomaly
  away one small rationalization at a time is how a badly wrong picture of
  a situation survives long past when it should have been questioned.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 11: Lean in to Confusion"
---

When something doesn't fit an existing interpretation — a detail in a
situation, an unexpected reaction from someone, a result that comes out
backward from what was expected — there are two available responses:
get curious about what would actually make it make sense, or reach for a
strained explanation that lets the original interpretation survive
unchanged. The second option is the soldier-mindset default, and it's
often invisible from the inside precisely because each individual
rationalization feels minor: "nobody likes me" survives one declined
invitation by reclassifying it as pity, then survives the next by
reclassifying it too. Each single stretch feels reasonable; the pattern of
always stretching, never breaking, is the tell.

This failure mode has a name — **de minimus error**: minimizing each
single inconsistency between what's observed and what's believed, one at a
time, so that the overall pattern of accumulating misfit never gets
recognized as a pattern. The corrective isn't to abandon a working
explanation at the first sign of friction either — a good explanation
should be able to absorb some genuine noise and imperfect fit without
being thrown out. The useful image is a spring: each anomaly stretches the
current explanation a little; noticing *how much* it's had to stretch,
cumulatively, is what signals when it's time to admit "I'm no longer sure
this holds up" and go looking for a better account, rather than either
dismissing every stray data point or discarding a sound explanation over
one weak anomaly.

This requires tolerating being unresolved for a while — holding a working
explanation while consciously aware it might be wrong, rather than forcing
premature closure in either direction. In practice this looks like
consciously logging anomalies as they're noticed, even without knowing yet
what they add up to (see Darwin's own practice, in [is it
true?](is-it-true-technique.md)'s spirit, of immediately writing down any
observation that contradicted his working theory, specifically because
inconvenient facts are the ones most likely to be forgotten) — and staying
willing to say "I don't have all the answers yet" rather than forcing a
verdict before enough of the anomalies have actually been accounted for.
