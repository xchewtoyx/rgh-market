Core beliefs are ideas held strongly and deeply, usually formed in childhood or
early adulthood — global, absolute, held 100% true under all conditions. Formed
in childhood to make sense of limited experience, they may never be reevaluated
as an adult. Negative beliefs are often reinforced by later experiences that
seem to confirm them.

## Three camps (dartboard model)

Core beliefs (bull's-eye) give rise to rules/demands/assumptions, which produce
automatic thoughts. Three camps:

1. **About yourself** — worth, capability, lovability (e.g., "I'm a failure").
2. **About other people** — trust, predictability (e.g., "People are dangerous").
3. **About the world** — safety, fairness (e.g., "The world is cruel").

Beliefs across camps interact and can be taught explicitly by caregivers before
personal experience justifies them. CBT investigates the past to see how early
events still affect present thinking — not for its own sake.

## Detecting core beliefs

People are usually more aware of automatic thoughts than core beliefs. Four
techniques:

1. **Downward arrow** — from a situation causing unhealthy emotion, ask what it
   means about you/others/world; repeat until a global absolute statement.
2. **Dreaming and screaming** — worst-nightmare scenarios reveal beliefs; apply
   the same questioning to parallels in waking life.
3. **Tracking themes** — review ABC forms for recurring automatic-thought themes.
4. **Filling in the blanks** — complete "I am ___", "Other people are ___",
   "The world is ___"; refine against prior written work.

## Prejudice model

Core beliefs make you cognitively prejudiced: negative confirming information
is let in; positive contradicting information is rejected or twisted. Praise
can be reframed ("he's only happy because my other work is rubbish") while
minor negatives register as confirmation. The belief acts as a filter preventing
reevaluation — see [disqualifying the positive](disqualifying-the-positive.md).

Behavior follows belief in self-fulfilling loops: believing you're unlovable
drives jealousy and reassurance-seeking that drives partners away, which then
"proves" unlovability.

Once core beliefs are identified (downward arrow, theme tracking, fill-in-the-blanks),
a **five-part formulation** captures them at a glance:

1. **Relevant early/past experiences** — events that contributed to the beliefs.
2. **Core unconditional unhelpful beliefs** — held 100% true, 100% of the time
   (about self, others, world). Example: "I am bad" / "Others are unpredictable
   and dangerous" / "The world is full of bad things."
3. **Rules and assumptions** — demands on self, others, world because of the core
   beliefs. Example: "I must be good at all times"; "If criticized, I'm bad."
4. **Avoidance and compensatory behaviors** — perfectionism, timidity, over-apologizing,
   not asserting yourself, not trusting others.
5. **What I've got going for me** — positive facts contradicting the belief; raw
   material for alternatives.

## Core belief flashcard

When a belief activates, step back for a more unbiased view. Carry a flashcard:
what the belief is; how it skews interpretation; how you act when triggered; a
more unbiased interpretation; a more productive alternative behavior. Review
several times daily, especially before high-risk situations.

## Developing alternative core beliefs

New beliefs need not be extreme opposites — "I'm unlovable" → "I'm lovable" may
be too hard; "some people do like me" may suffice. This is accuracy, not platitudes.

**Revisit history** with compassion — child-logic assigned meanings with limited
information; adults can reassess. Three-column worksheet: Event | Old Meaning |
New Meaning. Example: father yelling "you're bad" → old "I must be bad" → new
"My father's anger was the problem, not me."

**Live the new belief** before it fully takes hold — [act as if for
beliefs](acting-as-if-for-beliefs.md). Strengthen alternatives so they can "do
battle" with old ones — new paths through a field are awkward at first but become
natural with regular use while old worn paths remain tempting.

**Perspective prompts:** What would you teach a child? What would you want a
friend to believe? Apply the same standard to yourself — see [best friend argument
for self-acceptance](best-friend-argument-for-self-acceptance.md).
