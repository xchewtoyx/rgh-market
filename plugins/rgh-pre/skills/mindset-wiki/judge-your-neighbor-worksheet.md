The first step of [inquiry](inquiry-as-thought-testing.md) is identifying
thoughts causing stress and writing them down — about a person or
situation that angers, frightens, saddens, or confuses you. Use short,
simple sentences on a blank sheet, the Judge-Your-Neighbor Worksheet, a
One-Belief-at-a-Time Worksheet, or an app.

Writing matters because doing the work "in your head" lets the mind outsmart
you — off and running into another story to prove it's right before you
notice. Once written, thoughts stay stable enough to inquire. Write without
censoring; let the ego "write like a maniac" — it has been waiting for you
to stop and really listen.

Start with someone you haven't fully forgiven. Even 99 percent forgiveness
isn't freedom until the last 1 percent is found — that 1 percent is where
you're stuck in all other relationships, including with yourself. If new to
inquiry, judge others first, not yourself — self-judgments "come with a
motive and with solutions that haven't worked." Pointing outward lets you be
uncensored; self-judgment can come once you trust the method. "We have
20/20 vision about other people but not about ourselves."

Six statements (example: Paul):

1. Who angers, confuses, saddens, or disappoints you, and why?
2. How do you want them to change?
3. What advice would you offer ("he/she should/shouldn't...")?
4. What do you need them to think, say, feel, or do for you to be happy?
5. What do you think of them? (petty judgments welcome)
6. What don't you ever want to experience again?

Every story is a variation on: "This shouldn't be happening," "I shouldn't
have to experience this," "Life isn't fair." Be as judgmental, childish,
and petty as you were in the moment. Then apply [the four questions of
inquiry](the-four-questions-of-inquiry.md) and [inquiry
turnarounds](inquiry-turnarounds.md) to each statement.

Eventually you may see everything outside you as a reflection of your own
thinking — like lint on a projector lens you tried to fix on the screen.
The work changes the projector (mind), not the projected image.
