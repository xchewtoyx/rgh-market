The breath is an excellent anchor for attention — an anchor line tethering
awareness to the present and guiding it back when the mind wanders.
Bringing awareness to breathing reminds us we are here now.

To use breathing for mindfulness: tune in to the feeling of breath coming
in and leaving the body — bare-bones awareness of breath moving in and out.
This does **not** mean deep breathing, forcing breath, trying to feel
something special, wondering if you're doing it right, or thinking about
breathing. It need not take long — using the breath to return to the
present "takes no time at all, only a shift in attention," but stringing
moments of awareness together breath by breath over time opens up great
adventures.

Kabat-Zinn describes the breath as the current connecting body and mind and
connecting the body with the outer world — "all of Walden Pond is within
your breath." The breath is a thread linking all events from birth to death,
always present, moving by itself like a river — short/shallow when tense,
faster when excited, slow when happy. Observe it like a friend with interest;
do not control it. All that is needed to see clearly is the lens of awareness.

This anchor supports [deliberate attention practice](deliberate-attention-practice.md),
[sitting beside the thought stream](sitting-beside-the-thought-stream.md),
and structured techniques like the [body scan technique](body-scan-technique.md)
and [three-minute breathing space](three-minute-breathing-space.md).

It is "just as valuable to become aware that the mind has wandered and to
bring it back, as to remain fixed on the chosen object" — congratulate yourself
on noticing, optionally label briefly ("Ah, there's thinking"), escort back
with kindliness. Repeated wandering is an opportunity to practice patience and
[curiosity as scientist not judge](curiosity-as-scientist-not-judge.md). Belly rising/falling is a common
focus; contact sensations where body meets chair/floor are a fallback anchor.

Five reasons the breath anchors sitting meditation in MBCT: (1) it happens in
the present; (2) it is always available as an emotional-state marker; (3)
attending to it temporarily occupies the attention channel rumination would
fill; (4) it is the opposite of goal orientation — "not our job to make the
breath work; it simply does"; (5) noticing wandering and returning builds
metacognitive awareness and [decentering](welcoming-decentering.md) — seeing
thoughts as thoughts and practicing shifting mental gears.

**Try:** Stay with one full inbreath and one full outbreath, mind open and
free for just this moment; abandon ideas of getting somewhere; when the mind
wanders, return to the breath and string moments together.
