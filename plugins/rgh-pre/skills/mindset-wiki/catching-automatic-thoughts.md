Thoughts arise automatically and are usually consumed as if they were
direct reports of reality rather than one interpretation among several.
Catching a thought means noticing it as an event in the mind — "I am
having the thought that..." — at the moment it appears, rather than only
recognizing it after acting on it or after it has spiraled into
[rumination](rumination.md).

This noticing is a precondition for every other self-talk technique in
this domain: [cognitive reframing](cognitive-reframing.md) cannot be
applied to a thought that was never noticed, and the choice described in
[pain vs. suffering](pain-vs-suffering.md) — whether to keep replaying a
painful event — is only available once the replay itself is visible.

An old and useful refinement of this: an involuntary first flinch — going
pale at sudden danger, a jolt of fear, a flash of irritation — is not
itself a failure of self-regulation and isn't the thing to catch. What
can be caught, and is the actual site of the skill, is the *second* move
— silently endorsing that first reaction as an accurate verdict ("this is
genuinely terrible," "I really am in danger") and building further
thought on top of it. The gap between the automatic first flinch and
the voluntary endorsement of it is exactly where catching an automatic
thought does its work — the goal isn't to stop having the initial
reaction, which nobody can fully control, but to catch the moment before
it's reflexively agreed with and elaborated into something larger.
