Inquiry puts one belief at a time through four questions, then turnarounds
(see [inquiry as thought-testing](inquiry-as-thought-testing.md)). Each
belief understood releases the next; eventually you may look for a problem
and notice you haven't had one in years.

**Question 1: Is it true?** Answer with one syllable — yes or no. If the
answer includes "because…" or "but…," that is not the one-syllable answer
and you are no longer doing the work. Be still and let the honest answer
rise as you recall the situation. If clearly no, skip to question 3. If yes,
two follow-ups go deeper:

- *What's the reality of it?* Example: "Paul shouldn't watch so much
  television." The reality is he does — "a dog barks, a cat meows, and Paul
  watches television." The "shouldn't" is mentally [arguing with
  reality](arguing-with-reality.md); it doesn't change Paul, it only causes
  stress. In reality there is no "should" or "shouldn't" — only what is.
- *Whose business is it?* See [staying in your own
  business](staying-in-your-own-business.md). When you think someone else
  needs to change, you're mentally living their life while no one is living
  yours.

**Question 2: Can you absolutely know that it's true?** Most statements
only appear true because they rest on uninvestigated beliefs. Even with
visible evidence ("he's shouting at me"), can you know it's about you, or
what's in his mind, or that his anger is causing your hurt rather than
your interpretation? For "he should stop smoking" — can you know it would
be best for his life path? "I'm not saying it wouldn't be. I'm just asking."

When yes won't budge on Q1 or Q2, prompts surface a testable statement:

- *"...and it means that ___"* — completes the interpretation behind the
  fact ("he hit me... and it means he doesn't love me").
- *"What do you think you would have?"* — turns a demand ("he should tell
  me he loves me") into the underlying want ("I would feel more secure if
  he told me").
- *"What's the worst that could happen?"* — write out the feared catastrophe
  fully, then inquire into each item.
- *Find the should/shouldn't version* — "My father hit me" is fact; "My
  father shouldn't have hit me" exposes the argument-with-reality.
- *"Where's your proof?"* — list concrete evidence items, inquire into each
  with all four questions, then return to the original statement.

**Question 3: How do you react when you believe that thought?** This
surfaces internal cause and effect: believing the thought produces unease
from mild discomfort to panic — "you're looking at the power of a lie."
Before the stressful thought there was peace; believing it triggers mental
replay, grievance, and a world that doesn't exist. "Stress never feels as
natural as peace does." Optional follow-ups: "Can you see a reason to drop
the thought? (And please don't try to drop it.)" — inquiry is noticing,
not forcing; "once you see the truth, the thought lets go of you." "Can you
find one stress-free reason to keep the thought?" — test whether any reason
is peaceful or stressful.

**Question 4: Who or what would you be without the thought?** Imagine the
person doing what you judge, eyes closed, unable to think the judgment.
Which do you prefer? Common answers: free, peaceful, more loving, clear
enough to act intelligently. Without stories, appreciation and gratitude
become natural. The answer can leave you "without an identity" — just the
reality of the moment — which may feel scary; fears about that ("I won't
know how to live") are themselves fair game for inquiry. Beginners often
use "Who would you be without the thought?" and sit with whatever arises;
if the answer is "Peace," the follow-up is "What would you be without even
that thought?"

After the four questions, apply [inquiry turnarounds](inquiry-turnarounds.md)
to the original statement.

**Flexible order for experienced practitioners.** In live dialogues Katie
does not always ask the four questions in the stated order — she may vary
the order, leave questions out and zero in on one or two, or skip straight
to turnarounds. The usual order works well, and eventually may not be
necessary, but beginners are strongly advised to stay with the full form:
the deepest shifts tend to come from asking all four questions in the
suggested order, then applying turnarounds. [Self-facilitation of
inquiry](self-facilitation-of-inquiry.md) still starts there; flexibility
earns itself through practice.

We cannot know anything for certain. Experience is perception — ever
changing; even "now" is the story of a past by the time we perceive it.
Attaching to a thought turns it into "our religion"; trying to prove what
can't be known produces depression and disappointment. Question 1 opens the
mind even slightly; if the answer is yes, question 2 often produces an
agitated "No, I can't absolutely know that!" Sitting gently with that
answer opens into "infinite possibilities, to freedom" — like stepping out
of a narrow, smoky room into open space.
