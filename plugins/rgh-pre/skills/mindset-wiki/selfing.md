title: Selfing
description: >
  The mind's habitual construction of "I," "me," and "mine" out of almost
  every moment — a tenuous, constantly reconstructed self-sense that fuels
  insecurity, righteousness, and suffering when mistaken for solid identity.
sources:
  - title: "Wherever You Go, There You Are: Mindfulness Meditation in Everyday Life"
    resource: "Wherever You Go, There You Are (Jon Kabat-Zinn), Part 7: Selfing"
---

"'I,' 'me,' and 'mine' are products of our thinking." Larry Rosenberg's term
**selfing** captures the inevitable tendency to construct out of almost
everything an "I," a "me," and a "mine," then operate from that limited
perspective — mostly fantasy and defense. It happens almost constantly but
goes unnoticed, "much as the proverbial fish has no knowledge of water."
Out of virtually any moment, thinking constructs "my" moment, "my"
experience, "my" child, "my" hunger, "my" opinion, "my" problem.

With sustained attention, the self turns out to be a construct of mind,
hardly permanent. Looking for a stable core "you" underlying experience,
you find only more thinking — name, age, gender, opinions are labels, not
fundamental identity. Following the thread deeply, "there is no solid place
to land"; asking "Who is the I who is asking who am I?" arrives at "I don't
know." The "I" is known only by attributes, none of which constitutes the
whole person; it continually dissolves and reconstructs, moment by moment,
and tends to feel diminished, small, insecure, and uncertain — worsening the
tyranny of being caught up in "I, me, mine."

The "I" feels good when outside circumstances support its sense of goodness
and bad when it meets criticism or obstacles — a major explanation for
diminished self-esteem. Unfamiliarity with this constructed nature makes us
seek interior stability through outside rewards, possessions, and others'
love, perpetuating the self-construct — yet there may still be no enduring
stability or calmness. Buddhist view: there is no absolute separate self,
just the ongoing process of selfing.

Recognizing selfing as habit and giving permission to "take the day off" from
trying to be somebody — just experience being — could make life happier and
more relaxed. No-self does not mean being a nobody; everything is
interdependent and there is no isolated core you — you are only you in
relationship to all other forces and events. Practice is not about becoming
a contemplative zombie; it is about seeing things as they are without
distortions of thought, including interconnectedness and the non-solidity of
conventional "having" a self.

**Practical:** Take things a little less personally — when something happens,
maybe it just happened; maybe it is not aimed at you. Watch for "I this" and
"me that"; ask "Who am I?" or "What is this 'I' claiming ownership?" Awareness
helps balance selfing. The self is impermanent — whatever you try to hold on
to about yourself eludes you, a chaos-theory strange attractor that never
repeats. You can stop taking yourself so seriously and get out from under the
pressure of being central to the universe. Letting go of selfing gives the
universe more room to make things happen.
