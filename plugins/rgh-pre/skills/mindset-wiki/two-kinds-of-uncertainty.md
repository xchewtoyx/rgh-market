---
type: concept
title: Two Kinds of Uncertainty
description: >
  Uncertainty caused by one's own ignorance or inexperience reads as
  incompetence, while uncertainty caused by the world genuinely being
  unpredictable reads as expertise when delivered with clear command of
  the relevant context — the same hedge can land either way depending on
  which kind it is.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 9: Influence Without Overconfidence"
---

Expressing uncertainty ("I'm not sure," "it might be X or Y") gets read
very differently depending on its apparent source. Uncertainty "in you" —
"I haven't come across this before," "I don't really know how to explain
this" — signals a personal gap in knowledge or experience and tends to
read as incompetence. Uncertainty "in the world" — "the real risk factor
here is probably somewhere between one in five and one in ten," delivered
with obvious command of why that range is the honest answer — signals
that no one could responsibly give a tighter number, and reads instead as
expertise. The words expressing the uncertainty can be nearly identical;
what differs is whether the surrounding delivery demonstrates that the
uncertainty is a property of the situation rather than a gap in the
speaker.

This directly resolves a common bind for impostor-style self-talk: the
instinct to hide any hedge for fear it reveals incompetence, or the
opposite instinct to avoid stating anything with confidence for fear of
overclaiming. Neither is necessary — an accurate, hedged statement can be
delivered in a way that clearly signals genuine expertise, as long as it
demonstrates the uncertainty is inherent to the subject rather than
personal. See [communicating world-uncertainty
well](communicating-world-uncertainty-well.md) for the concrete technique
that makes this distinction land in practice.
