---
type: concept
title: Illusion of Truth
description: >
  Familiar, fluent, rhyming, or easy-to-read statements feel truer —
  repetition can make falsehoods believable because ease is mistaken for
  evidence.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 6"
---

The brain runs a continuous **cognitive-ease dial** from Easy to Strained.
Causes of ease (clear font, repetition, priming, good mood) and strain (poor
font, complex wording, bad mood) have **interchangeable effects** — you cannot
tell which input produced the feeling.

Consequences of ease: good mood, liking, believing what you hear, trusting
intuitions, superficial thinking. Consequences of strain: vigilance, more
effort, fewer errors, less intuition.

**Illusions of remembering:** invented names shown once can later feel like
celebrities — perceptual fluency mistaken for "pastness." **Illusions of
truth:** fluent statements judged true quickly; false plausible statements
slow judgment ("A chicken has four legs" slower than three — associative
interference). With no real knowledge, System 2 falls back on familiarity:
if it feels familiar, probably true.

Repetition alone makes falsehoods believable — even a familiar fragment
("the body temperature of a chicken") boosts acceptance of a false number
built on it. Authoritarian institutions and marketers have long exploited
this.

**Persuasion cues that inflate perceived truth:** legibility and contrast;
simple language (pretentious vocabulary lowers credibility); memorable
rhymes; pronounceable source names. These won't rescue obvious nonsense or
overcome strong prior knowledge — but lazy System 2 usually adopts System 1's
signal.

Countermove: when something "feels true," ask whether ease, rhyme, or
repetition is doing the work. Pair with [mere exposure
effect](mere-exposure-effect.md), [wysiati](wysiati.md), and [cognitive ease
and strain](cognitive-ease-and-strain.md).
