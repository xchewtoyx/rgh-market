  The people you live with most closely tend to press every sensitive
  belief until it becomes visible — making partners, parents, and children
  unusually effective triggers for inquiry, not random obstacles.

Spouses, parents, and children often show you truths you did not want to
see — and keep pressing the same buttons until you do. That framing turns
recurring friction from proof that someone is the problem into a signal
about which uninvestigated belief is active: "He should stop shouting"
reliably collapses, on inquiry, into "I should stop shouting mentally
about him."

When feedback that would once have been punished can finally be spoken —
"You always favored Ross over me" — inquiry can meet it without defensiveness:
"Could this be true?" sometimes yields a clear yes and repair instead of
a counterattack. The relationship becomes a laboratory for [inquiry as
thought-testing](inquiry-as-thought-testing.md), not a courtroom for
winning [being right versus being
free](being-right-vs-being-free.md).

The chapter's closing voice makes the same point outwardly: others keep
confronting you with what angers or repulses you until you understand —
because change in your inner stance is what changes how the world appears.
This is not permission to stay in harmful situations; it is a lens for
using unavoidable proximity as practice material when safety allows.
