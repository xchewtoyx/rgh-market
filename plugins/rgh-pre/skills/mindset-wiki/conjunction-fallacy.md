---
type: concept
title: Conjunction Fallacy
description: >
  A detailed, stereotype-coherent story feels more probable than a simpler
  version of the same outcome — even when logic requires the extra detail
  can only lower probability.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 16"
---

In the Linda problem, "Linda is a bank teller and is active in the
feminist movement" feels more probable than "Linda is a bank teller" because
it coheres with her description — yet feminist bank tellers are a subset of
bank tellers, so the conjunction cannot be more likely. **89%** of
undergraduates and **85%** of trained decision-science doctoral students
still ranked the conjunction higher in the famous within-subject version.

This is the **conjunction fallacy**: judging a conjunction more probable
than a constituent event. Like the Müller-Lyer illusion, "the fallacy
remains attractive even when you recognize it for what it is."

Representativeness judgments track [representativeness
heuristic](representativeness-heuristic.md) — probability confused with
similarity, coherence, and plausibility. Forecasting trap: "an earthquake
in California causing a flood" feels more probable than "a massive flood
somewhere in North America," though the detailed scenario is strictly less
likely. "Adding detail to scenarios makes them more persuasive, but less
likely to come true."

When detail isn't more plausible (e.g., "Mark has hair" vs. "Mark has blond
hair"), logic prevails — "In the absence of a competing intuition, logic
prevails." Short two-item Linda versions and "how many of the 100
participants...?" framing (versus "what percentage?") engage comparison and
nested frequency representation, cutting error rates sharply.

Countermove: anchor on a plausible base rate and question the diagnosticity
of your evidence — see [heuristic substitution](heuristic-substitution.md)
and [engaged versus lazy monitoring](engaged-vs-lazy-monitoring.md). Even
with all information laid out, System 2 often fails to apply Venn-diagram
logic unless stakes or framing force explicit comparison.
