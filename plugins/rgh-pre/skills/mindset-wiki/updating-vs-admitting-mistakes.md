---
type: concept
title: "\"Updating\" vs. \"Admitting\" a Mistake"
description: >
  Framing a revised belief as an "update" rather than a "confession" drops
  the implicit premise that being wrong was a failure, and treats the
  error as a source of transferable lessons about how your own mind works
  rather than just something to feel bad about.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 10: How to Be Wrong"
---

The ordinary phrase "admitting you were wrong" carries a hidden premise:
that being wrong means you screwed up, and admitting it is a matter of
owning up and being forgiven. That premise is often false. Reaching a
reasonable conclusion from the information available at the time, then
revising it once better information arrived, isn't a failure of process —
it's the process working correctly. "Updating" — routine language for
revising a probability given new evidence — is the more accurate frame:
matter-of-fact rather than confessional, describing something that makes
a belief better or more current without implying the earlier version was
a mistake to be ashamed of. Genuine contrition is appropriate only when
the earlier belief really was reached negligently — a process known to be
flawed, willful blindness, or not bothering to check an easily checkable
fact — which is a real but distinct case from ordinary belief revision.

Treating an error as an update rather than a confession also changes what
there is to extract from it. A **domain-specific** lesson only fixes the
one mistake ("that particular source was unreliable"); a
**domain-general** lesson is a transferable insight about how your own
mind operates — noticing, for instance, that a wrong belief stuck around
specifically because contrary evidence had been forgotten while confirming
evidence was remembered (an instance of [confirmation
bias](confirmation-bias.md)), or because recent information was weighted
more heavily than older information regardless of relevance. Simply
knowing a bias exists in the abstract is different from having personally
watched it operate on you in a specific case — the domain-general lesson
only really lands once it's been traced through an actual instance of
being wrong, which is what makes deliberately reviewing a specific error
("what does this teach me about how I form beliefs in general, not just
about this one topic") worth the extra step beyond just correcting the
fact itself.
