**Allowing** and **letting be** mean registering what is already here before
choosing how to respond — a conscious commitment, not passive collapse.
Explicitly distinguished from [acceptance versus
resignation](acceptance-vs-resignation.md): resignation implies helplessness;
allowing is active — "welcome," "treat each guest honorably," "invite them in."
Even a tentative first step — meeting strong emotions as they are rather than
showing them the door — is invaluable.

## Why it matters for relapse

Unwillingness to allow negative feelings, sensations, or thoughts (aversion)
is the first link in the chain that can rapidly reinstate old automatic,
relapse-related patterns — e.g. "I'm stupid to think like this" or "I should
be strong enough to cope." An allowing stance: (1) offsets automatic hijacking
by passing thoughts and moods; (2) shifts from "not wanting" to "opening,"
breaking conditioned responses at the first link; (3) shows you can be with
feelings and still be OK — all feelings pass of their own accord if not forced.

## Two-step body approach

Purely conceptual or willful attempts to become "more loving and accepting"
do not work. The alternative route works through the body:

1. Become mindfully aware of whatever most predominates or repeatedly pulls
   attention — note the pull itself, again and again.
2. Bring awareness to how you are relating, in the body, to what arises.
   **Liking** → holding on, wanting it to stay. **Disliking** → contracting,
   pushing away, wanting it gone. Both are the opposite of allowing. Allowing
   offers a third option: register the experience is here, let it be, hold it
   in awareness — conveying willingness to experience difficult states rather
   than automatically reacting.

Physical sensations of contraction, bracing, ache, or tensing are signatures
of aversion — the bodily manifestations of the aversive attitude. Bringing
awareness there, then "softening" and "opening" on the out-breath, works on
letting go of aversion itself. The intent is not to change the sensations but
to soften how they are held in awareness — how you are relating to them.
Gentle curiosity is itself part of acceptance.

## Exploring difficulty in practice

When attention keeps being pulled to painful thoughts or emotions, instead of
escorting attention back to the breath, allow the thought or feeling to remain,
then shift into the body to find accompanying physical sensations and focus
where they are strongest — breathing into the region on the in-breath, out
from it on the out-breath, not to change sensations but to explore them clearly.
Framing phrases: "It is here now. It is OK to be open to it. Whatever it is,
it's already here. Let me open to it." Then "Softening, Opening" on each
out-breath. "It's OK" is not judging the original situation or claiming
everything is fine — it helps awareness stay open to bodily sensations.
"It's OK not to want these feelings; they're already here; let me be open to
them."

If nothing arises spontaneously, deliberately bring a moderate difficulty to
the workbench of the mind. The purpose is not to get rid of difficulties —
accepting means allowing space for whatever is going on rather than creating
some other state. The formal **inviting a difficulty in** practice: after
settling on breath and body, bring a current or past difficulty to mind, tune
into physical sensations with a welcoming embrace, breathe into the region,
use "It's okay for me to feel this; whatever it is, it's already here" —
not endorsing the original situation, only staying open to bodily sensations.
Deliberately bringing attention to get rid of difficulty may get you more stuck;
a marker of progress is spaciousness — difficulty present but not filling the
whole mind. See [inviting difficulty in practice](inviting-difficulty-in-practice.md).

## The fixing trap

The subtlest issue: bringing allowing with a hidden agenda of fixing. Because
allowing is often followed by positive change, people use it instrumentally in
[doing mode](doing-mode-being-mode.md) — breath as a "shuttle service" off
frustration, or "breathe in to relax" — which undermines it and tends not to
produce lasting change. MBCT's radical premise: the best way "to get somewhere"
is not to try to get anywhere at all, but to open to the way things actually
are in this moment. The natural pull is to secretly still be trying to fix while
pretending only to take a kindly interest — which risks measuring against an
ideal, judging both problem and coping as falling short, and sliding into
rumination about meditation "not working."

## Breathing space and the escape hatch

From Session 5 onward, the [three-minute breathing
space](three-minute-breathing-space.md) can expand after the three steps:
allow attention to include discomfort, tension, or resistance — breathing into
and out from it with "It's OK to feel this." Some participants use the breathing
space as an **escape hatch** — brief retreat before re-engaging — which gives
only short-term benefit. The more skillful use brings awareness and relates
differently to the coming difficulty (rain-shelter metaphor: accepting you will
get wet and examining the rain, not just waiting for it to stop). "Cursing the
rain" is not failing — it is the next opportunity to practice; you can put out
the red carpet for feelings of failure too.
