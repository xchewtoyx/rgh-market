---
type: concept
title: Defensive Reaction as Truth Signal
description: >
  Immediate defensiveness when someone criticizes you often marks that the
  comment landed on something true — meeting it honestly can be more
  freeing than winning the argument.
sources:
  - title: "Loving What Is, Revised Edition: Four Questions That Can Change Your Life"
    resource: "Loving What Is (Byron Katie), ch. 4"
---

When family says the most painful thing — "You're lost" — and it has been
true at times, one way to know the criticism is accurate is that you
immediately feel defensive. "They were only saying what was true. As a
lover of truth, don't you really want to know what that is? Often it's the
very thing that you've been looking for."

Practical reframe for the next attack: when they say "You're lost," respond
"I noticed that, too, one day." When someone calls criticism "verbal abuse,"
ask whether they are telling a truth you do not want to hear — "There's no
such thing as verbal abuse. There's only someone telling me a truth that I
don't want to hear." Katie's practice when accused: check inside where it
is true, even in another situation decades old, and agree where it fits —
"the beginning of self-love." Friends give sympathy and agree with your
stories; "find an enemy" who will tell you straight what you may think you
do not want to know.

This pairs with [depersonalizing
critique](depersonalizing-critique.md) and [underlying
beliefs](underlying-beliefs.md) — defensiveness in conversation often
signals an uninvestigated belief is active. Use [inquiry as thought-
testing](inquiry-as-thought-testing.md) on the painful label before
rebelling or distancing.
