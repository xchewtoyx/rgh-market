---
type: concept
title: Emotional Bias in Judgment
description: >
  Most decisions are made emotionally first, with a logical justification
  assembled afterward to explain a conclusion the reasoning didn't
  actually produce — so a judgment that feels rationally derived is often
  a rationalization of an emotional reaction.
sources:
  - title: Solve for Happy
    resource: "Solve for Happy (Mo Gawdat), Part 3, Chapter 9: Is It True?"
---

Despite a strong felt sense of reasoning things out logically, the
typical sequence runs the other way: an emotional reaction forms first
(an impulsive purchase, an instant dislike of an unheard argument), and
the justification that seems to explain the decision is constructed
afterward to make it look reasoned. This doesn't make emotion illegitimate
or something to be purged from decision-making — emotional response is a
necessary and unavoidable input — but it does mean a stated "logical"
reason for a conclusion often isn't the actual cause of that conclusion,
which limits how much weight the stated reason should be given as
evidence the conclusion is correct.

This is directly relevant to [thoughts trigger
emotions](thoughts-trigger-emotions.md) working in the other direction as
well: not just thought producing emotion, but emotion producing a
seemingly independent thought that actually only rationalizes it. The
practical check is to ask, before accepting a "logical" conclusion:
what was the felt reaction to this before any reasoning started, and does
the reasoning actually support the conclusion or just dress it up? This
is close kin to [motivated reasoning](soldier-mindset.md) — a
post-hoc justification serving an already-decided emotional verdict is a
specific case of soldier mindset in action.
