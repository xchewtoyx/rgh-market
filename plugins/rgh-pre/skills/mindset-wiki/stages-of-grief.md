  has attached extra distortion on top of it.

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's commonly cited grief stages — denial, bargaining,
anger, depression, and acceptance — describe recognizable patterns rather
than a fixed order everyone must pass through in sequence; a person can
skip a stage, revisit one, or experience more than one at once. Their
practical use is as a naming tool: recognizing "this is anger" or "this
is bargaining" while it's happening makes a difficult reaction easier to
work with than experiencing it as undifferentiated distress, in the same
way [naming emotions specifically](naming-emotions-specifically.md)
helps with difficult feelings generally.

Reaching acceptance of the bare fact of a loss doesn't by itself prevent
additional suffering from being generated on top of it — accepting that
someone has died is a different thing from the mind's subsequent thoughts
about the loss (self-blame, injustice, catastrophizing about the future)
that can still need to be caught and worked with individually even after
the core fact has been accepted. See [pain vs.
suffering](pain-vs-suffering.md) for that distinction applied generally,
and [distress tolerance](distress-tolerance.md) for staying present with
a difficult feeling like grief without either suppressing it or being
overwhelmed by it.
