---
type: concept
title: Motivated Reasoning for Comfort
description: >
  Adopting a belief specifically because it makes a painful situation
  easier to bear — denial, "sour grapes," and "sweet lemons" — trades
  accuracy for short-term emotional relief.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 2: What the Soldier Is Protecting"
---

One function of [soldier mindset](soldier-mindset.md) is straightforward
comfort-seeking: adopting whatever belief hurts least, independent of its
accuracy. This takes recognizable forms — outright denial of an unpleasant
reality; "sour grapes" (deciding an unattainable thing was never wanted
anyway, after Aesop's fox and the grapes it couldn't reach); and "sweet
lemons" (convincing oneself an unfixable problem is actually a blessing).
It can also run in the pessimistic direction: giving up on something hard
("this is pointless") or attributing an outcome to fate rather than
effort can supply a rush of relief through resignation, since no longer
trying removes the discomfort of continued uncertainty.

The distortion isn't in feeling better — it's in reaching for whichever
belief provides relief rather than asking what's actually true and dealing
with the resulting feeling directly (see [distress
tolerance](distress-tolerance.md)). A useful check: would this belief
survive if the situation that made it comforting changed? A belief adopted
purely for comfort tends not to — the historical example of arguments that
childbirth pain "builds character," which faded once epidurals removed
the pain's inevitability, illustrates a belief that existed to make an
unavoidable cost bearable rather than because it was true.
