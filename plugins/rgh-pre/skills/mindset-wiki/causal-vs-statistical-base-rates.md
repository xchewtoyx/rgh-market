---
type: concept
title: Causal Versus Statistical Base Rates
description: >
  System 1 weights population facts that fit a causal story about the
  individual; bare statistical ratios are often ignored even when
  mathematically equivalent.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 17"
---

**Cab problem:** 85% Green cabs, 15% Blue; witness 80% reliable identifying
Blue. Bayesian answer ~41% Blue.

Two mathematically identical framings diverge psychologically:

- **Statistical base rate** ("85% of cabs are Green"): largely ignored;
  witness reliability dominates.
- **Causal base rate** ("Green cabs involved in 85% of accidents"): weighted
  heavily — implies reckless Green drivers, a stereotype combining with
  witness testimony toward the Bayesian answer.

**Statistical base rates** are population facts not causally linked to the
individual case. **Causal base rates** change how you think the case arose
(pass rate implying exam difficulty; accident rate implying driver trait).

Ajzen's exam experiment: 75% pass rate vs. sample constructed with 75%
failures — statistically equivalent, but causal framing strongly shifted
pass predictions; purely statistical assembly instructions had much less impact.

System 1 handles causally linked stories; it is weak at statistical
reasoning. Stereotypes are neutral mechanism — categories represented as
norms and prototypes; when social, we call them stereotypes.

Irony: neglecting base rates is a flaw, but causal stereotyping can improve
judgment in the cab problem — yet social norms rightly resist using group
causal base rates about people in hiring and profiling. Resisting valid
stereotypes has real costs; denying costs is not scientifically defensible.

Countermove when a vivid causal story feels decisive: ask whether you are
using a causal or merely statistical base rate, and what the numbers say.
