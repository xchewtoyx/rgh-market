---
type: concept
title: Labeling Judgment in MBCT
description: >
  In MBCT practice review, naming self-critical and standards-based thoughts
  as "judgment" — then returning to breath without arguing — breaks rumination
  spirals more skillfully than cognitive dispute alone.
sources:
  - title: "Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for Depression, 2nd Edition"
    resource: "Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for Depression (Segal, Williams, Teasdale), ch. 10"
---

Practice difficulties — "I haven't got time," "I'm letting the instructor
down," "I failed this week" — often trigger a **judging mind** that meets
negative thoughts with more self-criticism ("I ought to be over this by now.
I must be weak"). The machinery of rumination clanks into action: distraction
→ frustration at "losing it" → thought about letting others down → shame about
having the thought.

In standard cognitive therapy, one might examine evidence for "I don't fit
in." In MBCT the move is different: label the thought as **judgment** and
return to breath focus without answering back or reassuring via denial:

"Oh, hello, Mr. Judgment. Here you are again. Have a nice day!" — then bring
the mind back where you intended it to be.

The aim is not to block thoughts but to **be with them differently** — not
engaging, answering, or denying. "We can be here, and so can our thoughts,
but this does not mean we must be tied up with them in our accustomed ways."
Danger pattern: "I wish I did not have these thoughts" easily becomes another
layer of self-attack.

It is most often the **reaction** to an experience, not the experience itself,
that creates extra problems — noise interrupting practice, shame about missing
practice, perfectionist standards about "how meditation should be." Instructor
stance explores internal geography rather than answering "what's wrong."

as mental events](thoughts-as-mental-events.md), and [beginning again in
meditation](beginning-again-in-meditation.md).
