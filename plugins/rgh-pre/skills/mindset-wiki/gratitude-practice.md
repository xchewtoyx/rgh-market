Gratitude is framed here as "the practice of loving what you have," in
direct contrast to the constant pursuit of "getting what you want" that
drives [all-pervasive dissatisfaction](all-pervasive-dissatisfaction.md).
It works on the perception side of the [happiness
equation](happiness-equation.md): rather than trying to lower expectations
directly, it redirects attention toward what already satisfies them,
which resolves the equation positively without requiring anything about
external circumstances to change first.

The practice is most effective structured and repeated rather than
attempted only when already feeling low: regularly naming and fully
feeling specific things to be grateful for — starting from survival
basics, moving through freedom from hardship, to personal and relational
blessings — builds the habit of noticing what's present rather than only
what's missing. It complements rather than replaces addressing genuine
unmet needs; it targets the specific failure mode where satisfaction
never registers regardless of how much is actually going well.

Gratitude practice is not manufactured positivity — it functions as a
direct counterbalance to [negativity bias](negativity-bias.md): since the
brain (especially under stress, anxiety, or depression) already filters
attention toward negative-confirming information by default, deliberately
directing attention toward genuinely present positives is corrective
rather than distorting. A simple structured version: name a small number
of specific good things each night, then briefly review the previous
day's list before adding new ones — the review step in particular seems
to train attention to notice similar moments proactively going forward,
rather than only in retrospect. See [the downward comparison
technique](downward-comparison-technique.md) for a closely related move
that recalibrates a comparison-driven judgment specifically, rather than
directing attention to present positives in general.
