---
type: concept
title: Motivated Reasoning for Morale
description: >
  Inflating the odds of success, or selectively attending to favorable
  facts, can supply the motivation needed to start a hard undertaking —
  but the same overconfidence can also undermine the preparation needed to
  actually succeed.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 2: What the Soldier Is Protecting; Chapter 8: Motivation Without Self-Deception"
---

Believing the odds are better than they really are can function as fuel
for attempting something difficult — entrepreneurs, for instance,
routinely and dramatically overestimate their own venture's chance of
success relative to real base rates, and that inflated confidence can be
part of what gets a venture off the ground at all. The mechanism is
usually selective attention rather than outright fabrication: emphasizing
genuinely true favorable facts about a situation ("we have strong
backers") while downplaying equally true unfavorable ones ("we're all
inexperienced"), rather than believing something false outright.

This benefit is real but has a documented cost: in a study of law
students arguing assigned sides of a moot-court case, the students who
became most confident their side was correct were significantly *less*
likely to win — plausibly because conviction crowded out preparing for
the other side's actual rebuttals. Morale bought via overconfidence can
therefore directly undermine the outcome it was meant to support. The
alternative is decoupling motivation from a belief about the odds: it's
possible to commit fully to a hard effort while holding an accurate,
possibly sobering estimate of the odds — see [mindset determines
confidence trajectory](mindset-confidence-trajectory.md) for the related
point that full commitment doesn't require confidence to begin with.

This gives rise to two distinct kinds of morale. Soldier morale runs on
manufactured certainty — "odds don't matter if you believe in yourself,"
"failure is not an option" — which works short-term but is brittle,
because it requires ignoring or explaining away any information that
threatens it, and collapses hard when reality intrudes. Scout morale
instead comes from [bets worth taking](bets-worth-taking.md): knowing the
attempt is a good bet, worth making on its own terms, and that it's
possible to feel fine about having made it regardless of the individual
outcome. Because it isn't built on a belief that has to be protected from
disconfirmation, scout morale requires no maintenance against incoming
evidence — it stays intact whether the venture succeeds or fails, which
is precisely what makes it more durable than morale borrowed from
inflated confidence.
