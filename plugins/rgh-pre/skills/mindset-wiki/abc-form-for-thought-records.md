**A × B = C:** people often assume activating events (A) directly cause feelings
and behavior (C). CBT inserts **beliefs** (B) — meanings assigned to the event.
A partner's inconsiderate act (A) plus "they're doing this deliberately and must
not" (B) produces anger (C), not the act alone. Ten people can attach ten
different meanings to the same event and feel anger, depression, jealousy, guilt,
or annoyance accordingly.

**MBCT walking-down-the-street exercise:** imagine waving at a friend who does
not notice. Same trigger, different interpretations, different feelings — "He
didn't acknowledge me" → upset; "What have I done?" → worried; "Nobody likes
me" → isolated; "Fine, do what you want" → angry; "She was probably
preoccupied" → concerned. The event itself is neutral; all the action is in
what you make of it. Present mood powerfully shapes which interpretation arises —
ask which reactions would likely come to mind when depressed. Negative thinking
is a warning sign of oncoming depression; recognizing the pattern lets you check
whether current thoughts are distorted by mood. The stance toward this material
is curiosity, not problem-solving — like the raisin exercise, the aim is
noticing something not previously seen.

**Disturbed** responses are unhelpful, very uncomfortable, and hinder coping.
Three checks on whether a meaning is self-disturbing: (1) unduly extreme
conclusions? (2) global conclusions from one event? (3) loaded against you —
spurring action or collapse? Largely yes → needless amplification of a genuinely
bad situation.

Thoughts, feelings, and behaviors interact in vicious circles — changing either
unhealthy thinking or behavior patterns can break the loop.

**Premise:** thinking affects feelings and actions powerfully; changing unhelpful
thoughts is key to feeling better.

**ABC form I:** Activating event (trigger), Beliefs (thoughts/meanings), Consequences
(emotions and behaviors). Start with emotion (what you feel), then behavior, then
trigger, then thoughts ("What's the meaning behind how you feel?"), then identify
[naming cognitive distortions](naming-cognitive-distortions.md) / thinking errors.

**ABCDE form II** adds: **D — Dispute** (can I prove this 100% true? what are the
effects? would respected others agree? what's the evidence against? how will I view
this in a year?) and **E — Effect** (rate feelings 0–100% after alternatives).

Practical capture: jot thoughts immediately when upset — delay risks forgetting or
censoring once emotions settle. Writing reframes thoughts as hunches and theories
rather than absolute facts.

Generate flexible, nonextreme, realistic alternatives — the critical step because
alternative thoughts produce feeling better. Stubborn NATs may need repeated passes;
training the mind to think more flexibly over time.

**Caveat:** for OCD intrusive thoughts, health anxiety, BDD, worry, or jealousy,
avoid ABC forms — instead develop capacity to live with doubt and let catastrophic
thoughts pass ([intrusive thoughts tolerance](intrusive-thoughts-tolerance.md)).

Regular practice embeds new thoughts; even as skills develop, the form remains
useful for "biggies." Old forms document progress and remind how far thinking has
shifted.

Core CBT companion to [catching automatic thoughts](catching-automatic-thoughts.md)
and [cognitive reframing](cognitive-reframing.md).
