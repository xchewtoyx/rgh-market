---
type: concept
title: Stereotype Threat Interacts With Mindset
description: >
  Being aware of a negative stereotype about a group one belongs to
  measurably impairs performance and undermines sense of belonging, but
  this effect is substantially weaker for people holding a growth mindset.
sources:
  - title: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success"
    resource: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success (Carol S. Dweck), Chapter 3: The Truth About Ability and Accomplishment"
---

Stereotype threat is the finding that merely being reminded of a negative
stereotype about a group one belongs to (e.g. by checking a demographic
box, or being outnumbered by a group the stereotype favors) measurably
lowers performance for the stereotyped person — driven by largely
unconscious background worry about confirming the stereotype, which
consumes attention that would otherwise go to the task. The effect hits
much harder under a [fixed mindset](fixed-mindset.md): both a positive
label (fear of losing it) and a negative one (fear of confirming it)
threaten a fixed sense of self, so a fixed mindset is vulnerable to labels
in either direction. A [growth mindset](growth-mindset.md) is comparatively
protected — since ability is believed changeable rather than fixed, a
negative stereotype doesn't read as a verdict on permanent inferiority,
and the typical response to feeling behind is to work harder and seek
help rather than to disengage.

This extends to how critical feedback is received when it comes from
someone perceived as potentially biased or hostile: fixed-mindset people
in one study rejected useful feedback outright by attributing it entirely
to the source's bias, learning nothing from it; growth-mindset people
could acknowledge a source's off-putting manner while still using the
substance of the feedback instrumentally toward their own goals. The same
split shows up in sustained sense of belonging in a challenging
environment — growth mindset tends to keep belonging comparatively stable
even amid perceived stereotyping, partly because it frames prejudice as
"someone else's view," not as evidence to internalize about oneself. This
is not a claim that unequal treatment doesn't matter — it is a claim about
which internal stance limits the damage a real external bias can do to a
person's own trajectory.
