---
type: concept
title: Negativity Bias
description: >
  The brain systematically devotes more attention, faster processing, and
  stronger, more durable memory to negative information than to positive
  or neutral information of equal weight.
sources:
  - title: Solve for Happy
    resource: "Solve for Happy (Mo Gawdat), Part 3, Chapter 9: Is It True?"
---

Multiple independent lines of evidence point to the same asymmetry:
people are slower to name the color of a negative word than a neutral one
(more attention gets pulled toward the negative content), remember
negative traits better regardless of how they were presented, and rate a
person with a negative outlook as seeming smarter than one with a positive
outlook. Negative outcomes are weighted more heavily than equivalent
positive ones in ordinary decision-making (a single bad review can
outweigh a good one out of proportion to its actual informational value).
Neurologically, the amygdala devotes a disproportionate share of its
processing to detecting negative experiences, and negative information
gets committed to long-term memory almost immediately, while positive
experiences need much longer sustained attention to be retained at all —
summarized as the brain being "Velcro for negative experiences but Teflon
for positive ones."

This is a general perceptual and attentional bias, distinct from but
compounding [negativity bias in self-talk](negativity-bias-in-self-talk.md)
specifically. Because negative signals get amplified and positive signals
get filtered out or forgotten by default, an accurate read of any
situation requires deliberately correcting for this asymmetry rather than
trusting that attention and memory are already giving a balanced picture —
see [gratitude practice](gratitude-practice.md) for one structured
countermeasure, and each of [the seven perceptual blind
spots](brain-filters-information.md) for the specific distortions this
bias compounds.
