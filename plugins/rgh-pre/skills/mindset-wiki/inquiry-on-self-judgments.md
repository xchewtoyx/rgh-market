  self-judgments — and discomfort with a turnaround often signals a
  belief about yourself not yet investigated.

Many people judge themselves as relentlessly as a repeating recording —
telling themselves over and over what they are and what they're not,
"taking orders from a plastic toy." Once investigated, these self-
judgments often melt away.

If you've done [inquiry](inquiry-as-thought-testing.md) on others, you've
noticed judgments of others turn back toward you. Discomfort with a
turnaround signals a belief about yourself not yet investigated — "He
should love me" turning to "I should love myself" with stress on the
latter is a cue to inquire further. The Work deals with concepts, not
people; the "you" you judge is no more personal than everyone else.

Worked example, "I am a failure": apply [the four questions of
inquiry](the-four-questions-of-inquiry.md) — can you absolutely know it?
Could it be you have lived the life you should have lived and done what
you should have done? List concretely how you react physically and toward
others when you believe it. Imagine life without the thought ever again.

[Inquiry turnarounds](inquiry-turnarounds.md) on self-judgments can be
radical: "I am a failure" → "I am not a failure" or "I am a success." Let
the turnaround reveal itself as true or truer; list specific ways you are
a success, however small ("I brushed my teeth," "I breathed"). Alternate
technique: substitute "My thinking" for "I" — "My thinking is a failure,
especially about myself" — clarifying that without the thought you're
fine; it's the thought that's painful, not your life.

If a turnaround doesn't work, don't force it — inquire sincerely and let
turnarounds find you. [Arguing with reality](arguing-with-reality.md) on
self-judgment works the same way: when you argue with what you've actually
done or been, you lose every time.
