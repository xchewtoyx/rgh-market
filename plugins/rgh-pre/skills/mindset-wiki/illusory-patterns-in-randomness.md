---
type: concept
title: Illusory Patterns in Randomness
description: >
  The mind seeks causes and regularity, so random sequences often look
  meaningful — streaks, clusters, and "hot hands" feel real even when
  statistics say they are noise.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 11"
---

The associative machinery looks for causes. Chance events require comparing
what happened to what could have happened instead — but intuition treats
randomness as if something specific caused each outcome. Every six-birth
sequence is equally likely if boys and girls are independent; only
BGBBGB "looks" random, so people wrongly rank it more probable.

Maps of WWII rocket hits showed gaps that invited spy-conspiracy stories;
statistical analysis showed a typical random pattern — one that still
*looks* clustered to an untrained eye. Basketball's "hot hand" — the belief
that players enter streaks of elevated shooting — persists among players,
coaches, and fans even when shot sequences pass tests of randomness; the
streak is largely in the eye of the beholder.

Small samples amplify the illusion: among top-ranked schools, small schools
overappear because variability is higher at small N — not because small
schools are better on average. The same bias makes a few observations of a
person feel like a stable character verdict ([halo
effect](halo-effect.md)).

Evolutionarily, false alarms about change can be cheaper than missing real
change — but in self-talk, treating noise as signal drives [catastrophizing](catastrophizing.md),
superstitious blame, and overconfidence after lucky streaks. When a pattern
feels obvious, ask whether it could be produced by chance and what base
rates or sample size actually support.
