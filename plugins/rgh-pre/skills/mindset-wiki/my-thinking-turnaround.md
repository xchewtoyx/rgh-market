---
type: concept
title: My-Thinking Turnaround
description: >
  When inquiry targets an issue rather than a person, turn the statement
  toward "my thinking" — the problem "out there" is often a misperception
  inside your own mind.
sources:
  - title: "Loving What Is, Revised Edition: Four Questions That Can Change Your Life"
    resource: "Loving What Is (Byron Katie), ch. 10"
---

There is no thought or situation that can't be put up against [inquiry](inquiry-as-thought-testing.md).
When the worksheet statement points at an issue rather than a person,
apply [the four questions of inquiry](the-four-questions-of-inquiry.md)
as usual, then at the turnaround step substitute "my thinking" for the
issue wherever appropriate, and test whether that is as true or truer.

Examples:

- "I don't like war because it frightens me" → "I don't like my thinking
  because it frightens me" (or "...especially about war...").
- "I'm angry at bureaucrats for making my life complicated" → "I'm angry at
  my thinking for making my life complicated."
- "I don't like my handicap because it makes people avoid me" → "I don't
  like my thinking because it makes me avoid people" / "...because it makes
  me avoid myself."
- "I want sex to be gentle and loving" → "I want my thinking to be gentle
  and loving."

Inquiring into issues this way reveals that perceived problems "out there"
are often misperceptions within your own thinking — the same move as
[inquiry on self-judgments](inquiry-on-self-judgments.md), applied to
world issues. "Nothing outside you can ever give you what you're looking
for." Until you can see everything in the world as a friend, the work is
not done — but anger, fear, and sadness are signals to sit down, identify
the story, and inquire.
