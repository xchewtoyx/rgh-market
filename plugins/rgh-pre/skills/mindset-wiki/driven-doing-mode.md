The mind shifts between modes like gears in a car — mostly only one at a time.
**Doing mode** gets things done: form an idea of how things should be,
compare to how they are, generate thoughts and actions to close the gap,
monitor progress, adjust. It works brilliantly for external-world problems.

**Driven-doing** is when doing mode volunteers for a job it cannot do. When
a discrepancy cannot be resolved by direct action — especially when the
*self* is entangled in the goal ("there is some basic failure in me") — the
mind keeps processing in doing mode, dwelling on the discrepancy, worsening
mood and confirming the negative self-view in a self-perpetuating loop.

Subjective markers: recurring **unsatisfactoriness**; continuous monitoring
("How well am I doing?"); "have to," "must," "should," "ought," "need to."
Thoughts about situations and goals are experienced as real rather than as
mental events; attention is preoccupied with past and future at the expense
of full present-moment awareness — the present is registered only narrowly
for monitoring success or failure.

Examples: meditating with driven focus on achieving relaxation, then anger
when interrupted — meditating in driven-doing mode. Washing dishes only to
finish quickly — interruption produces frustration. Washing dishes accepting
the chore as activity for its own sake — being mode; interruption becomes a
neutral choice-point.

MBCT's core skill: at times of potential relapse, recognize and disengage
from driven-doing and enter an incompatible mode — [being mode in
mindfulness](doing-mode-being-mode.md) — so depression-related information
is processed in ways less likely to provoke relapse. This shifts focus from
**content** of negative thoughts to **process** of how experience is held.
[mindfulness](mindfulness.md) provides both the means to shift gears and an
alternative mode to shift into.

Ultimate aim: balance being and doing — driven-doing (culturally exalted and
propelled by [mental autopilot](mental-autopilot.md)) can crowd out other
ways of being with experience. The seven recurring markers of this mode are
listed in [seven signs of driven-doing](seven-signs-of-driven-doing.md).

Living ruled by endless "to do" lists damages emotional health: not list-making
itself, but impending doom if the list isn't completed and the myopic narrowing
of life. Many people behave as if doing were the only gear available. Reserve
doing mode for domains where it is skillful; cultivate [being mode in
mindfulness](doing-mode-being-mode.md) far more than culture encourages.

At a tipping point — e.g. late-night work frustration after a save failure —
pausing, feeling feet on the floor, resting attention on breath can reveal that
"I must finish because it's on the list" is just a thought; kindly widening
awareness to the whole body can restore enough calm to choose bed over driven
completion.

**Wise doing** (mindful doing) flows out of being: first recognize experience
as it is; then, if you choose, engage appropriate action to care for yourself
or respond compassionately. Everyday interactions are where runaway doing mode
shows its cost and where dropping into being transforms experience — being
fully awake, fully alive, and fully oneself without preventing necessary action.

**Relapse model:** depressive relapse reactivates at lowering mood patterns of
negative thinking similar to prior episodes — automatic, not deliberate, running
along well-worn grooves. It reactivates a whole package of thoughts, feelings,
and physical sensations in feedback loops. At the heart of rumination sits the
**discrepancy monitor**: continually comparing self and situation against
desired, required, expected, or feared standards. Finding mismatches motivates
further attempts to reduce discrepancy — but dwelling on mismatch deepens
negative mood. Attempts to solve "feeling sad" by thinking about sadness keep
you locked in the state you are trying to escape. People who have experienced
major depression persist in trying to get rid of depression even when repeated
efforts fail; a more skillful response is abandoning those attempts and
disengaging from the state of mind that creates relapse risk.
