---
type: concept
title: Blame vs. Ownership After Failure
description: >
  Blaming external factors for a failure protects self-esteem in the
  moment but blocks learning from it, while owning a mistake keeps it
  available as something that can still be worked on.
sources:
  - title: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success"
    resource: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success (Carol S. Dweck), Chapter 2: Inside the Mindsets"
---

Attributing a failure to external factors (bad luck, unfair conditions,
someone else's error) rather than to anything within one's own control is
a common [fixed mindset](fixed-mindset.md) self-esteem repair strategy —
if the cause was external, the fixed trait being protected was never
really tested. The cost is that blaming forecloses learning: "you aren't
a failure until you start to blame" — someone who owns a mistake is still
in the process of learning from it, while someone who explains it away has
stopped.

This is distinct from harsh self-judgment: owning a mistake is not the
same as globally condemning oneself over it (see [mindset shapes response
to setbacks](mindset-shapes-response-to-setbacks.md) and [self-criticism
is not a healthy motivator](self-criticism-is-not-a-healthy-motivator.md))
— it means accurately attributing the controllable parts of what went
wrong to oneself specifically so they can be addressed, without either
denying responsibility (blame) or converting the specific failure into a
verdict on identity (self-condemnation). The useful middle position is
"what part of this was actually mine to change, and what will I do
differently" — neither excusing it away nor treating it as proof of
permanent inadequacy.
