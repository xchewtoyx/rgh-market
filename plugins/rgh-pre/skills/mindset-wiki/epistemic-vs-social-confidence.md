---
type: concept
title: Epistemic Confidence vs. Social Confidence
description: >
  How sure you are that a claim is true (epistemic confidence) and how at
  ease and self-assured you appear while saying it (social confidence) are
  independent traits, not a package deal — one can be high while the
  other stays low.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 9: Influence Without Overconfidence"
---

Epistemic confidence is calibration: how sure a claim actually is, given
the evidence — a well-calibrated person says "I'm 99% sure" only when
that's genuinely warranted, and hedges ("I think," "it seems to me") when
it isn't. Social confidence is a different thing entirely: ease in a
social situation, a relaxed and authoritative manner, acting as though
one's presence and input matter. Benjamin Franklin deliberately combined
very high social confidence with deliberately hedged, low epistemic
confidence in speech — dropping firm language like "certainly" once he
noticed it made people push back, replacing it with "I think..." and "it
appears to me at present..." — and became, by his own account, more
persuasive for it, not less.

Research measuring how observers rate other people's "confidence" and
"competence" backs this up: those ratings track social-confidence cues
(participation, tone, posture, eye contact) far more than stated
epistemic confidence — how certain someone actually claims to be barely
registers. This is directly useful for anyone who worries that hedged,
calibrated language ("I believe," "probably," "I'm not certain but...")
will read as weak or incompetent: the hedging is not what most people are
actually responding to, and can be paired with confident delivery without
any contradiction. See [depersonalizing critique](depersonalizing-critique.md)
and [two kinds of uncertainty](two-kinds-of-uncertainty.md) for related
techniques that keep honest calibration from reading as incompetence.
