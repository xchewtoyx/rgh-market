---
type: concept
title: Soldier Mindset (Directionally Motivated Reasoning)
description: >
  Reasoning aimed at defending a preferred conclusion — asking "can I
  believe this?" of welcome evidence and "must I believe this?" of
  unwelcome evidence — rather than at finding out what's actually true.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 1: Two Types of Thinking"
---

Soldier mindset treats reasoning as defensive combat: beliefs are
"well-grounded" or get "attacked," positions are "defended" or
"fortified," changing your mind is "surrender," conceding a point is
"ceding ground." This isn't just colorful language — it reflects how
directionally motivated reasoning actually operates. When a conclusion is
wanted, the implicit question becomes "can I believe this?" — a search for
enough permission to accept it. When a conclusion is unwanted, the
question flips to "must I believe this?" — a search for any available
excuse to reject it. Either way, the amount of scrutiny applied to a claim
is set by whether the conclusion is wanted, not by the claim's actual
evidential weight. Other familiar names for the same underlying pattern:
denial, wishful thinking, confirmation bias, rationalization,
self-justification, overconfidence.

Under soldier mindset, finding out you're wrong is experienced as
suffering a defeat — which is precisely why it gets resisted so strongly,
and why evidence tends to be sought that fortifies an existing position
rather than evidence that would genuinely test it. Nobody operates in pure
soldier mode across every domain — the same person can be scoutlike about
one area of life (say, work strategy) and soldier-like about another
(say, a relationship or a political identity); see [scout
mindset](scout-mindset.md) for the contrasting stance, and [testing a
limiting belief](testing-a-limiting-belief.md) for a concrete technique
that substitutes an accuracy question for a defensive one.
