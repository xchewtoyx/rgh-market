---
type: concept
title: The Power of "Yet"
description: >
  Appending "yet" to a statement of present inadequacy — "I don't know how
  to do this yet" — converts a fixed verdict about current ability into an
  open statement about a trajectory still in progress.
sources:
  - title: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success"
    resource: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success (Carol S. Dweck), Chapter 2: Inside the Mindsets"
---

A [fixed mindset](fixed-mindset.md) expects competence to appear fully
formed, without any visible learning curve — so encountering people who
are further along feels like discovering a permanent gap rather than a
temporary one. This is the exact mechanism behind impostor-style thinking:
accomplished people who are excellent at demonstrating existing knowledge
(taking tests, presenting polished work) but who haven't yet built a
newer skill can feel like "complete impostors" within a day of encountering
others who have — "They know how to take tests and get A's but they don't
know how to do this — yet. They forget the yet."

The word "yet" is doing real cognitive work, not just softening language:
it reclassifies "I can't do this" (a fact about a fixed trait, and
therefore threatening) into "I can't do this yet" (a fact about a point in
time on a trajectory, and therefore just information). This is a direct,
minimal-effort application of [cognitive reframing](cognitive-reframing.md)
to the specific distortion of treating current inability as a permanent
verdict rather than as a snapshot mid-development — see also [test scores
are not permanent verdicts](test-scores-are-not-permanent-verdicts.md) for
the same reframe applied to a single evaluation rather than a single
statement about ability.
