Once you know your needs and values, expressing them means risking being
disliked. Penot's claim: everyone is disliked — neurotypicals, autistics,
and other neurodivergent people alike — no matter how hard you work to be
perfect, mask, or say yes to everything. You will also be liked, even loved,
by people who value your differences or who need a safe neurodivergent space.

Being disliked does not mean being bad or wrong. Dislike arises for many
reasons — politics, religion, gender, hair color, perspective — even among
neurotypicals disliking each other. Kishimi and Koga argue that people who
fear being disliked actually hate themselves: they fear their real self being
seen by others. Courage comes from letting go of that fear and learning to
like yourself.

Accepting dislike matters especially when research suggests neurotypicals
may dislike autistic people more than others (double empathy problem) — and
when people in general are judgmental regardless. The practical stance:
since full mutual understanding won't arrive soon, cultivate **self-empathy**
even absent neurotypical empathy — say no to unwanted activities; recognize
that making yourself miserable to be liked is worse than being disliked.

**Workbook exercise:** list things you do to be liked that make you
uncomfortable; ask whether you'd rather be disliked or do the activity; if
disliked is preferable, let the activity go and notice whether you're
happier. Penot's example: a friend wanted her to listen to podcasts despite
auditory processing limits — she'd rather be disliked than spend hours on
something she cannot follow. Ask what you gain from approval from people
who ask uncomfortable things, versus loving yourself and finding people who
love you as-is. "You matter. Your comfort and happiness matter as much as
anyone else's… Say no. Find the courage to be disliked."
