Group belonging was historically tied to survival, so a perceived signal
of rejection — a flat tone, a delayed reply, an ambiguous look — can
trigger a real fight-or-flight-level reaction even when no actual
rejection has occurred. For anyone whose history includes more frequent
real rejection, this detection system calibrates toward oversensitivity:
having been burned by real instances often enough, the mind starts
flagging ambiguous or neutral signals as further instances too, producing
false alarms that can damage a connection that was never actually at
risk.

The concrete technique is the same move as [thought
defusion](thought-defusion.md) applied to this specific pattern: naming
it explicitly in the moment it fires ("that's my rejection sensitivity
acting up") rather than treating the felt alarm as an accurate read of
the other person's intent. Naming does two things at once — it creates
enough distance to question the reaction before acting on it, and,
because the pattern has an understandable origin rather than being a
personal defect, it removes the additional layer of shame that otherwise
gets stacked on top of the reaction itself. This pairs directly with
[naming emotions specifically](naming-emotions-specifically.md): the
label isn't just descriptive, it's the intervention.

**Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD):** a psychological state that increases
sensitivity to and decreases tolerance of rejection, criticism, and taunting.
Many autistic people develop profound RSD from lifelong rejection, bullying,
and failure to meet internalized neurotypical expectations; general sensory
hypersensitivity may also lower the threshold. Combined with awareness of
being more disliked than peers, RSD pushes many autistic adults into extreme
people-pleasing — described by Penot as "one of the most dangerous parts of
ASD." If you cannot tolerate being disliked or cannot say no, even when
conforming is traumatizing, vulnerability to abuse and repeated quality-of-life
compromise rises. High PTSD rates among autistic people may partly reflect this
pattern alongside social-skills gaps and fear of rejection.
