---
type: concept
title: Doing Mode and Being Mode
description: >
  Doing mode drives change and accomplishment; being mode stops, witnesses
  the present without trying to fix it — and mindfulness shifts between them
  so action becomes more vivid and guided when it resumes.
sources:
  - title: "Wherever You Go, There You Are: Mindfulness Meditation in Everyday Life"
    resource: "Wherever You Go, There You Are (Jon Kabat-Zinn), Part 2: Stopping"
---

Much of daily life runs in **doing mode**: planning, fixing, rushing toward
the next thing. Meditation is not a special kind of doing — it is about
stopping and being present ("Don't just do something, sit there"). A
practical shift is to enter **being mode** even briefly: think of yourself
as an eternal witness, timeless, watching the moment without trying to
change it — what is happening, what do you feel, see, hear?

Stopping is framed as a kind of dying on purpose to the rush of time while
still living: if you actually died, obligations would evaporate and get
worked out without you. Taking a few moments to die on purpose to urgency
frees time for the present and paradoxically makes a person more alive now.
When you then decide to go, the going becomes more vivid, richer, and more
textured, giving guidance on worries and inadequacy.

This pair is central to MBCT and mindfulness-based approaches: [driven-doing
mode](driven-doing-mode.md) and [doing-mode rumination](doing-mode-rumination.md)
keep the mind locked in fixing when the self is entangled in goals; being mode
holds experience without immediately acting on it. In being mode, thoughts and feelings are related to as passing
events — arising, becoming objects of awareness, passing away — the
**decentered** perspective that lets uncomfortable states be tolerated without
automatic clinging or aversion. [Mindfulness](mindfulness.md) cultivates the capacity to shift modes
rather than staying locked in doing.

**Try:** Stopping once in a while through the day (five minutes or five
seconds), becoming aware of breathing, letting go into full acceptance of
the present including feelings and perceptions without trying to change
anything — then moving in the direction your heart tells you, mindfully
and with resolution.
