---
type: concept
title: Does Upset Add Options Test
description: >
  Before indulging a strong emotion, ask whether getting upset actually
  gives you more ways to handle the situation — usually it does not, and
  often it makes things worse.
sources:
  - title: "The Obstacle Is the Way: The Timeless Art of Turning Trials into Triumph, Expanded 10th Anniversary Edition"
    resource: "The Obstacle Is the Way (Ryan Holiday), ch. 7"
---

Chris Hadfield, after both eyes froze shut on a solo spacewalk: there are
always things you can think and do to make a situation better, but "There's
no problem so bad that you cannot make it worse also."

The test: "Does getting upset provide you with more options?" Sometimes
yes — but usually no. If an emotion can't change the condition or
situation, it is likely unhelpful, possibly destructive. Panic causes
people to override systems, disregard procedures, and react to survival
hormones rather than to what they actually need to respond to.

This pairs with [the buffer between feeling and
action](buffer-between-feeling-and-action.md): feeling an emotion and
acting on it are separate. You need not pretend not to feel — take a moment
if needed — but distinguish emoting about a problem from dealing with it,
"as different as sleeping and waking." Self-talk that fits: "I am in
control, not my emotions. I see what's really going on here. I'm not
going to get excited or upset."

For anxiety onset, a simpler mantra: "I am not going to die from this."
The constant question under pressure: "Do I need to freak out about this?"
— and the answer, for anyone who has practiced, should be no, because you
can control yourself or have caught yourself and realized upset adds
nothing constructive. See [equanimity under
pressure](equanimity-under-pressure.md) for the broader stance.
