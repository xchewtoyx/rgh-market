---
type: concept
title: Five-Minute Rule for Task Initiation
description: >
  Committing to just five minutes on a task, with explicit permission to
  stop once the timer ends, removes the outsized resistance to starting —
  the hardest part of most avoided tasks — because continuing past the
  start is reliably easier than beginning at all.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Megan Anna Neff), Chapter 6, entry 14: Overcome Autistic Inertia"
---

Resistance to starting a task is often disproportionate to the task
itself — the felt barrier to beginning is much larger than the actual
effort required once underway, an inertia effect where getting something
moving takes more activation energy than keeping it moving. Committing to
a large, open-ended block of time ("I'll work on this for two hours") can
itself become the thing being avoided, since the size of the commitment
matches the size of the resistance.

The technique: commit to five minutes only, with the explicit, genuine
option to stop the moment the timer ends — not five minutes as a secret
trick to build momentum, but five minutes as the actual, honored
agreement. In practice, continuing past the five minutes turns out to be
the easier path far more often than not, once the harder threshold of
starting has already been crossed — but the permission to stop has to be
real, not a rhetorical trick, or the technique stops working as a way to
lower the barrier to entry. This targets the same resistance addressed by
[concrete implementation plans](concrete-implementation-plans.md), but
specifically for the moment of starting rather than for planning the
action in advance — breaking a task down this small is itself the plan.
See [behavioral activation](behavioral-activation.md) for the same
principle applied across a whole low-motivation period rather than to a
single task.

Once a task is underway and genuinely absorbing, the opposite problem can
appear — difficulty stopping rather than starting. Setting an exit cue in
advance (an alarm, a scheduled check-in, another person as a prompt) and
treating it, when it goes off, as a moment to consciously ask "is this
still what I want to be doing right now?" rather than either a hard stop
or an automatic override, keeps deep absorption from running past the
point it's actually still serving its purpose.
