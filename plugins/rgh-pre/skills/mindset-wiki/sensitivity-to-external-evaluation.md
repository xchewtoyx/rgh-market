---
type: concept
title: Sensitivity to External Evaluation
description: >
  A history of frequent praise and infrequent criticism can teach someone
  to treat any single evaluation from others as an accurate, weighty
  verdict on themselves, making a rare piece of criticism disproportionately
  destabilizing.
sources:
  - title: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success"
    resource: "Mindset: The New Psychology of Success (Carol S. Dweck), Chapter 3: The Truth About Ability and Accomplishment"
---

If evaluations from other people (especially in childhood) skew heavily
positive and rare negative ones stand out sharply, a habit can form of
trusting others' evaluations as reliable reports of the truth about
oneself — including when a negative one eventually arrives. Someone with
the opposite history — frequent, casual criticism mixed in with praise —
tends to develop more resistance to any single evaluation, positive or
negative, mattering that much: one more critical comment among many
doesn't carry disproportionate weight.

The practical implication is that the intensity of a reaction to a single
piece of praise or criticism is not solely about the content of that
comment — it reflects how much authority has habitually been assigned to
other people's evaluations generally. This connects directly to
[mindset and self-knowledge accuracy](mindset-and-self-knowledge-accuracy.md):
building an internal, evidence-based sense of one's own ability (rather
than relying on the running tally of others' verdicts) reduces the amount
of power any single external evaluation — flattering or unflattering —
has to define self-perception. It is a useful diagnostic question for
anyone whose self-talk swings heavily on one comment: is this update
proportionate to the evidence, or proportionate to a longstanding habit of
outsourcing self-assessment to whoever spoke last?
