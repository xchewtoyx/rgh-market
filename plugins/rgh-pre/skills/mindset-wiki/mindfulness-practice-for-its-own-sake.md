The word "practice" for cultivating mindfulness does **not** mean repetitive
rehearsal aimed at an eventual polished performance. It means committing
fully, each moment, to being present — there is no performance, just this
moment. We are not trying to improve, get anywhere else, chase special
insights, or force nonjudgmental calm; nor indulging self-consciousness.

We simply invite ourselves to interface with this moment in full awareness,
intending to embody calmness, mindfulness, and equanimity right now. With
continued practice and firm-yet-gentle effort, those qualities develop on
their own from commitment to dwell in stillness and observe without
reacting or judging. Realizations and profound stillness do come — but it
is incorrect to say we practice in order to make them happen, or that more
is better than fewer.

The spirit is to practice for its own sake, taking each moment as it comes
(pleasant, unpleasant, good, bad, ugly) and working with what is present.
With this attitude, life itself becomes practice — the practice does you;
life becomes your meditation teacher.

Befriending practice also reveals that unawareness "is everywhere" — not
just goes with the territory but **is** the territory. The mind can be
cluttered like an attic; recognizing that clutter is itself progress.

This complements [non-striving in meditation](non-striving-in-meditation.md)
and [informal attention practices](informal-attention-practices.md): the
same non-performance stance can fold into ordinary activities so
[deliberate attention practice](deliberate-attention-practice.md) accumulates
without a separate "meditation session" frame.

For a sustained program (e.g. an eight-week course), you do not have to find
practice enjoyable — you do not have to like it at all. The challenge is to
take it on wholeheartedly: follow instructions, suspend judgment, and let go of
all agendas, even "to get better," to see what happens moment by moment. You are
responsible only for the input you bring; the outcome is unpredictable, unique,
and continually changing. If you can be here for this moment with things exactly
as they already are, that is the practice.

The closing discipline: practice every day even if some days it is only five
minutes — but remember that **the real practice is none other than your life.**
Brief moments, if fully present, can be profoundly reorienting. To know the
landscape of your own mind and body, visit regularly or take up "permanent
residency" rather than remaining a perpetual tourist — like gaining fluency in a
language through immersion rather than occasional phrase-book study.

## How long is long enough?

**Meditation has little to do with clock time.** Five minutes of formal practice
can be as profound as — or more than — forty-five. Sincerity of effort matters
far more than elapsed time, since practice steps out of hours and minutes into
moments that are dimensionless and therefore infinite. Mindfulness needs to be
kindled and nurtured, protected from the winds of a busy life or a restless mind,
like sheltering a small flame from strong gusts. Even one minute, if that's all
you can manage, is truly wonderful — you have remembered the value of stopping
and shifting from doing to being.

In clinical MBCT, forty-five minutes daily home practice is the standard — long
enough to settle into stillness, taste deepening wellbeing, and meet challenging
mind states (boredom, impatience, fantasy, anger, pain, grief). Many patients
find it becomes necessary, a lifeline. But what is challenging-but-doable for
one person at one time may be nearly impossible at another: a single parent of
small children, someone in perpetual crisis, cramped living quarters, medical
students, or high-stress workers cannot reasonably carve out extended non-doing.
For non-patient teaching, fifteen minutes daily (or twice daily) is the challenge;
failing that, ten or five. However many moments exist in forty-five minutes,
just as many exist in five — "we have plenty of time, if we are willing to hold
any moments at all in awareness." Better to grow into longer periods gradually
than never taste mindfulness because obstacles seemed insurmountable.

**Try:** Sit for varying lengths of clock time and observe the effects. Does
concentration lapse as you sit longer? Does impatience or time pressure arise?
If new to meditation, do "This is stupid," "Am I doing it right?", or "Is this
all I'm supposed to be feeling?" arise — right away or only after a while? Put
the welcome mat out for these mind states, investigate their qualities, and let
them be — you may learn what is strong and unwavering in yourself.
