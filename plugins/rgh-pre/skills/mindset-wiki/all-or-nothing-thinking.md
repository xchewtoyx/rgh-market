---
type: concept
title: All-or-Nothing Thinking
description: >
  Collapsing a situation into two extremes — total success or complete
  failure — with no acceptable middle ground amplifies self-judgment past
  what the situation warrants; deliberately holding both a positive and a
  negative truth at once ("gray thinking") counters it.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Megan Anna Neff), Chapter 4, entry 17: Practice Gray Thinking"
---

All-or-nothing thinking takes one imperfect moment (a single parenting
misstep, one awkward comment) and treats it as proof of a global, binary
verdict ("I'm a terrible mother," "I'm bad at this") rather than as one
data point in an otherwise mixed picture. This amplifies self-judgment,
sets an unrealistic standard (flawlessness) as the only acceptable
outcome, and reliably produces disappointment since a genuinely flawless
record is not actually the norm for anyone.

"Gray thinking" is the direct countermeasure: deliberately holding two
true things at once instead of letting one cancel the other out — "I am a
good mother, and I didn't handle that situation the way I wish I had."
The word "and" is doing real work here in place of "but": "but" implicitly
negates whatever came before it ("the party was nice, but I made a fool of
myself" reads as if the fool-of-myself part erased the nice part), while
"and" holds both clauses as simultaneously true without either canceling
the other. This is a lightweight, repeatable linguistic habit that keeps a
single flaw or setback from being read as an overall verdict — the same
underlying move as [mindset shapes response to
setbacks](mindset-shapes-response-to-setbacks.md), applied at the level of
sentence construction. See [incremental belief
updating](incremental-belief-updating.md) for the same "gray, not binary"
principle applied to revising an opinion or belief rather than judging
oneself after a setback.
