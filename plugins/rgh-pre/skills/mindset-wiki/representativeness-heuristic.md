People assess probability by how representative something is of a category —
how much it resembles a stereotype — rather than by full statistical
reasoning. The **Tom W problem** shows substitution in action: given a
sketch designed to fit computer science and poorly fit populous fields,
psychology graduate students ranked *probability* of Tom W's field identically
to *similarity* rankings — ignoring base rates and the description's
untrustworthy source.

Given a shy, orderly description of "Steve," occupation probabilities track
similarity to librarian vs. farmer stereotypes, even when base rates should
dominate.

**Two sins of representativeness:**

1. **Excessive willingness to predict low-base-rate events** — a New York
   Times reader on the subway is more likely to lack a college degree than
   hold a PhD, despite the stereotype fit. A shy poetry lover is a better
   bet for business administration than Chinese literature because the
   business population is so much larger.

2. **Insensitivity to evidence quality** — worthless information gets
   processed as if true unless actively rejected ([WYSIATI](wysiati.md)).
   For Tom W, stay near base-rate priors and adjust only slightly — the
   sketch is untrustworthy, so base rates should dominate.

Base-rate neglect is often **laziness rather than ignorance**: instructing
people to "think like a statistician" increases base-rate use; frowning
(which engages System 2) increases sensitivity to base rates compared to
puffing cheeks. Bayes's rule specifies how prior beliefs combine with
diagnostic evidence — e.g., 3% base rate plus 4× diagnosticity yields 11%
posterior, not the stereotype-driven answer.

**Sample-size neglect:** Which hospital (45 vs. 15 births/day) records more
days with >60% boys? Most say "about the same," but the smaller hospital
should show far more such days.

**Law of small numbers / gambler's fallacy:** People expect short random
sequences to "look random" locally; after red at roulette, black feels
"due." See [belief in small numbers](belief-in-small-numbers.md) for how
small samples produce extreme results and spurious causal stories.

**Illusion of validity:** Confidence tracks how well input fits the
prediction, not actual predictive accuracy.

Countermove when a vivid story feels decisive: ask what the base rate is,
how large the sample is, and whether you're judging by resemblance rather
than evidence — see [heuristic substitution](heuristic-substitution.md),
[conjunction fallacy](conjunction-fallacy.md), and [confirmation
bias](confirmation-bias.md).
