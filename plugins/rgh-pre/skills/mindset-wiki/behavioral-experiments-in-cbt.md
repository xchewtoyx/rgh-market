Aaron Beck described CBT as client and therapist being "scientific together" —
testing thoughts, not only talking about them. Behavioral experiments are a
key strategy for moving a new perspective from head to heart; some predictions
about the distant future cannot be fully tested, but the mental and behavioral
*coping strategies* used around them can be — do they make things better,
worse, or no different?

## Why run an experiment

Use one to:

- Test the validity of a thought or belief about self, others, or the world
- Test an alternative thought or belief
- Discover effects of mental or behavioral activities on emotions, health, and
  relationships
- Gather evidence clarifying the nature of the problem

People stick to beliefs and safety-driven behaviors because they seem true and
protective. Experiments often reveal the worst imagined scenario does not
happen, or that it is handled effectively even if it does. Frame change as
reversible — old ways remain available if new ways do not prove better — but
try long enough for a fair test.

**Aim for unambiguous disconfirmation** when possible: concluding the feared
outcome does not come true even without prevention (e.g., dizziness from anxiety
does not lead to collapse even without sitting down).

## Four-step method

1. **Describe the problem** — including safety behaviors (things done to prevent
   the feared catastrophe), in your own words, noting life impact.
2. **Formulate the prediction** — what you think will happen if you try a new
   way of thinking or behaving.
3. **Execute** — devise a real-life test (ideally more than one).
4. **Examine results** — did the prediction come true? Rate conviction 0–100%
   before and after processing results.

**Caution on subtle safety measures:** doing the experiment only when feeling
"right," only with "safe" people, with safety signals at hand, or while
using distraction or gripping behaviors leaves a "narrow escape" impression
rather than clear evidence the catastrophe did not happen.

## Competing theories (Theory A / Theory B)

Rather than only trying to disprove a negative prediction, develop an
alternative theory and gather evidence for which best fits — Occam's razor
favors the simplest adequate explanation. A "theory" here is an assumption
about why something happens.

Especially helpful when:

- **Predictions are months or years away** — e.g., illness anxiety about
  sensations signaling eventual death; test evidence for "I have a worry
  problem" rather than the catastrophic theory.
- **Beliefs cannot be conclusively proved or disproved** — e.g., others'
  opinions, jealousy about a partner's feelings; design tests for whether
  jealous feelings stem from jealous thoughts (Theory B) rather than reality
  (Theory A).

## Surveys and observations

**Surveys** help when thoughts, sensations, or behaviors feel abnormal —
intrusive harm images, vertigo, agoraphobic doom. Checklists or questionnaires
to others often reveal similar experiences handled without the same fear.

**Observations** collect evidence by watching others — e.g., whether admitting
confusion at work actually leads to ridicule or firing.

## Design checklist

- Appropriately challenging — accomplishment without overwhelm
- Clear plan: how, when, where, with whom
- Specific question — not merely "see what happens"
- Pre-decide how you will know if the prediction came true
- Plan response if the prediction does come true
- Use a behavioral experiment record sheet
- Anticipate obstacles and workarounds
- Check for bias when evaluating (discounting positives, mind-reading)
- Account for safety behaviors distorting results
- Plan consolidation: repeat, new experiment, or activity change

Treat negative thoughts with skepticism — a "pinch of salt." Frame changes as
experiments, not lifelong commitments, especially early on. Start small when the
problem feels big.

**No-lose perspective:** a successful experiment is good; an avoided experiment
reveals blocking thoughts; even a confirmed feared prediction shows how well you
cope and informs next steps.
