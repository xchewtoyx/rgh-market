---
type: concept
title: Mere Exposure Effect
description: >
  Repeated exposure to a stimulus — even unconscious — breeds mild liking
  and familiarity, which can bias preferences without any new information.
sources:
  - title: Thinking, Fast and Slow
    resource: "Thinking, Fast and Slow (Daniel Kahneman), ch. 6"
---

Robert Zajonc showed that repetition of an arbitrary stimulus induces
[cognitive ease](cognitive-ease-and-strain.md) and comforting familiarity —
the **mere exposure effect**. Turkish-sounding words repeated more often in
student newspapers were later rated more favorably as "good" versus "bad,"
even when readers could not recall seeing them. The effect replicates with
ideographs, faces, and random polygons; it strengthens when stimuli are never
consciously seen.

Zajonc argued this is a deep survival adaptation: novel stimuli warrant
caution until repeated exposure proves they are not followed by harm — then
they become safety signals. Even chicken embryos exposed to one tone before
hatching showed less distress to that tone afterward.

For wellbeing practice, repeated thoughts, people, or environments can
feel truer or safer simply from familiarity — not from merit. When you
notice automatic preference for "the way it has always been," ask whether
mere exposure is doing the work. Conversely, unfamiliar coping strategies
may feel wrong at first simply because they are new.
