---
type: concept
title: Process vs. Outcome Evaluation
description: >
  Judging the quality of a decision by whether it happened to work out
  conflates skill with luck — separating "was this a good decision" from
  "did this decision succeed" gives a more accurate and less
  self-punishing basis for self-evaluation.
sources:
  - title: "The Scout Mindset: Why Some People See Things Clearly and Others Don't"
    resource: "The Scout Mindset (Julia Galef), Chapter 8: Motivation Without Self-Deception"
---

Any single outcome is a mix of the quality of the decision that produced
it and variance — luck, timing, factors outside anyone's control. The
default instinct is to build a causal story purely from the outcome ("I
succeeded because I was skilled," "I failed because I wasn't good
enough"), which credits or blames the decision for effects that were
actually just noise. A more accurate habit, modeled by people who work
with highly variance-driven outcomes professionally, is to evaluate the
decision on its own merits independent of how it turned out: "not a great
decision, but I'd defend the reasoning behind it" or "a good decision,
even though it didn't work out this time."

This directly targets a common driver of harsh self-talk after a setback:
treating a single bad outcome as proof of a bad decision (or a personal
deficiency) rather than checking whether the decision itself was sound
given what was known at the time. It doesn't mean waving away every
failure as "just bad luck" — the discipline cuts both ways, crediting
genuine skill honestly and not over-crediting lucky wins either. The
practical habit is to ask two separate questions after any outcome: was
this the right call given the information available, and separately, how
much of the actual result was variance rather than the call itself? See
[mindset shapes response to setbacks](mindset-shapes-response-to-setbacks.md)
for the related distortion of letting a single outcome generalize into a
verdict on identity rather than staying scoped to the decision itself.
