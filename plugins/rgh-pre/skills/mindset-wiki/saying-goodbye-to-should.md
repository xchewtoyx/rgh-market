---
type: concept
title: Saying Goodbye to Should
description: >
  Unmasking often means catching inherited "should" rules and replacing them
  with what actually fits you — tested by asking whether an action comes
  naturally or was imposed from outside.
sources:
  - title: "How to Be You: Simple Ways to Love Who You Are"
    resource: "How to Be You (Ellie Middleton), ch. 9"
---

Long-term masking — especially for undiagnosed neurodivergent people in
neurotypical worlds — blurs which behaviors are genuinely yours and which
are a character you learned to play. Years of mimicking others plus
invalidation can erode trust in your own judgment and leave you relying on
external validation.

Middleton's operational test for peeling a layer: **every time you do
something, ask whether you are doing it because it comes naturally, or
because somebody once told you you should.** If it is the latter, that is a
mask layer to release — not by rebellion for its own sake, but by finding
what feels more natural.

Examples of the swap:

- "I should make eye contact" → "I can look wherever feels comfortable."
- "I should remember everything alone" → "I can ask for help or use tools."
- "I should put on a brave face" → "I can feel my feelings and turn to a friend."
- "I should juggle all household tasks alone" → "I can ask housemates or hire help."
- "I should concentrate eight hours at a desk" → "I can work with breaks,
  movement, and background stimulation."

Society's shoulds are often unexamined conventions — "that is how it has
always been done" — not individualized guidance. Letting go of should,
would, and could is framed as both practical (more sustainable functioning)
and an act of [self-compassion](self-compassion-statements.md): you deserve
accommodation and to be yourself.

This pairs with inquiry practice: many stressful thoughts are should
statements about self or others ([arguing with reality](arguing-with-reality.md),
[staying in your own business](staying-in-your-own-business.md)). The
should-test adds a body-and-preference check before the thought even
formalizes — "is this rule mine?"
