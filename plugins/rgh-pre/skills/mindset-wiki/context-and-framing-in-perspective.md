title: Context and Framing in Perspective
description: >
  Deliberately adjusting how wide or narrow you view a setback — its
  context in a lifetime and the frame you put on it — changes whether an
  obstacle feels crushing or workable.
sources:
  - title: "The Obstacle Is the Way: The Timeless Art of Turning Trials into Triumph, Expanded 10th Anniversary Edition"
    resource: "The Obstacle Is the Way (Ryan Holiday), ch. 9"
---

Perspective has two components: **context** — the larger picture, not
only what's immediately in front of you — and **framing** — your unique
way of interpreting events. Both can be injected to change a situation that
seemed impossible.

Pericles during a solar eclipse: sailors panic at darkness; he covers a
steersman's face and asks if he's scared of what he sees (nothing) — so
what does it matter when the cause differs? Fear is often about the
ominous explanation chosen over the simple one, not the obstacle itself.

Putting "I" in front of an obstacle ("I hate public speaking. I screwed
up.") adds you-in-relation-to-it rather than the obstacle alone — with the
wrong frame, something small consumes you. Isolation bias: one blown deal
in a lifetime of deals. Richard Branson: "business opportunities are like
buses; there's always another coming around." Ask: "Is our perspective
truly giving us perspective or is it what's actually causing the problem?"

George Clooney reframed auditions: producers need to find someone — he
became the solution to their problem, not a supplicant. "The difference
between the right and the wrong perspective is everything." Selective
editing — not to deceive others but to orient yourself calmly for the
task. See [agency over interpretation](agency-over-interpretation.md),
[cognitive reframing](cognitive-reframing.md), and [observing versus
perceiving](observing-versus-perceiving.md). "Where the head goes, the
body follows. Perception precedes action."
