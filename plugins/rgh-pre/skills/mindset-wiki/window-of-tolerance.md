type: concept
title: Window of Tolerance
description: >
  The range of stress and stimulation within which a person can stay
  regulated, clear-thinking, and engaged — exceeding it produces
  hyperarousal, dropping below it produces hypoarousal, and the width of
  the window itself is trainable over time.
sources:
  - title: "Self-Care for Autistic People: 100+ Ways to Recharge, De-Stress, and Unmask"
    resource: "Self-Care for Autistic People (Megan Anna Neff), Chapter 4, entry 24: Expand Your Window of Tolerance"
---

Between two thresholds sits a "window" where stress and stimulation can
be handled while staying balanced, clear-thinking, and socially
available. Above the upper threshold, the nervous system moves into
hyperarousal — anxiety, agitation, panic, a racing or scattered mind.
Below the lower threshold, it moves into hypoarousal — dissociation,
numbness, fatigue, a sense of detachment or shutdown. Neither state is a
failure of character; both are what happens once capacity has been
exceeded in either direction, and the width of the window varies between
people and can narrow temporarily under chronic stress or lack of
recovery.

The window is not fixed — it can be widened over time through consistent
practice: building a reliable base of [grounding
techniques](grounding-techniques.md) and general self-regulation skills,
prioritizing genuine recovery (see [restorative vs. cheap
rest](escape-as-avoidance.md)), maintaining clear personal boundaries, and
building familiarity with one's own early warning signs before a
threshold is crossed. Practically, this reframes noticing hyperarousal or
hypoarousal not as a problem to suppress in the moment but as a signal
that the current window has been exceeded — worth responding to with
recovery and regulation rather than by pushing further into the state
that produced it.
