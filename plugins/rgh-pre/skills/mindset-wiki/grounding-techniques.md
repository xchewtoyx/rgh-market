Grounding techniques fall into three types, each anchoring attention
through a different channel. Physical grounding uses body sensation —
running cool water over the hands, gentle deliberate movement — to give
attention something concrete and immediate to attach to. Mental grounding
redirects thought onto a simple, absorbing task — counting backward,
naming items in a category, a short mental puzzle. Sensory grounding
engages the senses directly with the immediate environment — texture,
scent, sound — with the 5-4-3-2-1 exercise as a structured version: name
5 things seen, 4 felt, 3 heard, 2 smelled, 1 tasted.

These work through the same mechanism as [attention displacement to
interrupt a thought](attention-displacement-to-interrupt-thought.md) and
[mindfulness](mindfulness.md) — occupying attention with the immediate,
directly observable present rather than the spiraling thought or feeling.
The important caveat is that grounding is a short-term tool for regaining
enough stability to actually process a situation, not a substitute for
that processing — used repeatedly to avoid ever feeling a difficult
emotion, it becomes another form of [escape](escape-as-avoidance.md)
rather than the temporary reset it's meant to be.
