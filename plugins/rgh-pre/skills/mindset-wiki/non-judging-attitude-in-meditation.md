type: concept
title: Non-Judging Attitude in Meditation
description: >
  Meditation cultivates witnessing whatever arises — including the judging
  mind itself — without condemning or pursuing judgments, so direct contact
  with experience replaces reactive liking and disliking.
sources:
  - title: "Wherever You Go, There You Are: Mindfulness Meditation in Everyday Life"
    resource: "Wherever You Go, There You Are (Jon Kabat-Zinn), Part 3: Non-Judging"
---

In meditation one quickly discovers a part of the mind constantly evaluating
experience against expectations and standards, often rooted in fear — fear
of not being good enough, that bad things will happen, that good things
won't last, that others might hurt us. We see through tinted glasses: good
for me / bad for me / neutral (barely noticed). In stillness, the judging
mind can come through like a foghorn: *I don't like the pain in my knee...
This is boring... I'm no good at this.*

"Meditation means cultivating a non-judging attitude toward what comes up in
the mind, come what may. Without it, you are not practicing meditation."
This does not mean judging stops — minds compare, judge, and evaluate by
nature. The approach is not to stop or ignore judging thoughts, but to
witness whatever arises without condemning or pursuing it, knowing judgments
are unavoidable and necessarily limiting. The interest is direct contact
with the experience itself — breath, sensation, sound, impulse, thought,
perception, or judgment — while remaining attentive to judging the judging,
or labeling some judgments good and others bad.

Thoughts are often uninformed private opinions, reactions, and prejudices
based on limited knowledge and past conditioning; unrecognized, they
prevent clear seeing and project judgments onto everything off a hairline
trigger. Becoming familiar with and watching this pattern fosters greater
non-judgmental receptivity.

Non-judging does not mean ceasing to act responsibly or that anything goes —
it means acting with greater clarity, balance, effectiveness, and ethics once
we recognize immersion in a stream of unconscious liking and disliking.
Liking and disliking can become chronic, feeding addictive behaviors.
Recognizing seeds of greed/craving (in wanting what we like) and aversion
(in rejecting what we don't) reminds us these forces work almost constantly —
with a chronic, viral-like toxicity that prevents seeing things as they
actually are.

Connects to [mindfulness](mindfulness.md)'s observing-vs-evaluating stance
and to [self-compassion statements](self-compassion-statements.md) as an
alternative to harsh self-judgment in practice.
