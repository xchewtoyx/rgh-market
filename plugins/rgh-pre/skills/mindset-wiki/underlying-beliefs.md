Beneath the judgments on a worksheet, there are often thoughts believed for
years that function as fundamental judgments of life — broader, more general
versions of stories, "like religions that we unconsciously live." A trivial
thought like "George should hurry up so we can go for a walk" can route
inquiry to unexamined beliefs such as "The present is not as good as the
future," "I'd be happy if I had my way," or "It's possible to waste time."
Attachment to these makes waiting, or perceiving others as slow, painful.

Underlying beliefs are the building blocks of your concept of heaven and
hell — how you'd improve reality if you had your way, and how bad reality
could look if fears came true. Discovering that long-carried painful beliefs
aren't true, and were never needed, is described as incredibly freeing.
Apparently positive beliefs can be as painful as negative ones: "My life
should have a purpose" can produce fear, snapping at family, retreat to
food and television, and a sense that ordinary activity is unimportant until
something "big" is found — with turnaround "My life should not have a
purpose" landing as relief ("what's already been lived has always been
enough").

Examples worth working on: "Life is unfair"; "It's necessary to know what
to do"; "If I don't suffer, it means I don't care"; "It's possible to make
a mistake"; "Parents are responsible for their children's choices." Prompts
for noticing them: whenever you feel defensive in conversation, or whenever
you're sure you're right — jot the belief for later [inquiry](inquiry-as-
thought-testing.md). Undoing one underlying belief allows whole families of
related beliefs to surface; the most pertinent turnaround is often the
180-degree reversal.

The [proof-of-truth technique](proof-of-truth-technique.md) is one of the
best ways to surface underlying beliefs from a specific judgment. In live
inquiry, a facilitator may help find "the story that is the real cause of
the suffering" hidden from awareness — by looking more deeply at the original
statement for the statement behind it, or by shifting inquiry to a painful
statement made spontaneously mid-session. Solo practitioners: if a new,
deeper painful thought surfaces while doing The Work alone, write it down
and include it in your inquiry rather than pushing past it.

Apply [the four questions of inquiry](the-four-questions-of-inquiry.md) to any
underlying belief you identify, then turn it around.
